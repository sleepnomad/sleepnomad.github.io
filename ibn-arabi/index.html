Cold open

There is a cup on the desk in front of you, and it exists. You exist too. Ask what those two facts have in common and the honest modern answer is: nothing worth saying. The cup is there, you are there, and the word "exists" just marks that they are there. It adds nothing to the cup and nothing to you. That answer feels obviously right, and a man who died in Damascus in 12401 spent his whole life arguing that it is exactly backwards.

His name was Ibn Arabi, and his claim, in plain words, is this. The "exists" is the only thing in the room that is not a shape. The cup is a shape. You are a shape. The desk, the light, the hand holding the cup, the thought you are having about it: shapes, every one, and shapes are what come and go. The existing does not come and go. It is one thing, not two, and there is no second kind of it. So the cup and you are not two things that happen to exist, but two shapes that one existing is currently in.

Everything else he wrote follows from refusing to treat that word as nothing. If being is one, then the world is not God and is not other than God, and he means both halves at full strength. If being is one, then there had to be a reason for shapes at all, and the reason is that mercy needs someone to receive it. If being is one, then what you are was known before you were, and your acts belong to that shape. They do not belong to a second person inside you who could have chosen otherwise, because there is no such person. If being is one, then nothing repeats, and the cup you have been looking at for a minute is a series of showings that rhyme. If being is one, then pain is one of its words. There is a child in pain whom the system cannot explain, and he does not pretend it can. If being is one, then every God anyone has ever prayed to is a God they shaped, including yours. The only error is to think the shape is the whole. And if being is one, then everyone you have ever loved was that one being in a face. That is why love never fits its object and never ends with it.2 And the last word in the sentence is mercy, and even the pain is said inside it.3

He put the whole thing in one image, and it is the image that carries everything here. The world is a sentence, and God is speaking it on one breath. Every thing is a word. A word is not the speaker, and it is not something other than the speaker either. It lasts exactly as long as the breath that carries it, and it says what the speaker knew it would say before it was said. And there is one word in the sentence that can hear the sentence, and that word is a human being.

This is not a comfortable philosophy, and what it costs should be said before it is defended. It costs you the ownership of your own acts. It costs you the God you already have, in the form you have him. It costs you the idea that the world is made of things, with you as one of them, standing behind a wall. What it gives back is smaller and stranger than what it takes. The wall you feel between you and everything else is real as a shape and false as a border. Then there is the minute you once had, on a walk or in grief or half asleep. For that minute there seemed to be only one thing going on. On this account that minute was not a mood, and it was not your brain misfiring. It was the ordinary case, seen once without the usual editing.

The argument has to earn each of those. It starts where the modern answer is strongest, with the claim that "exists" adds nothing to the cup. That claim is half right, and the half that is wrong is where everything else begins.

Part 1: Exists Is Not Nothing

Here is the claim that has to be earned first. Existence is not a property that things have. It is the one thing that is actually there, and everything else is the shape it is currently in. The cup does not have being the way it has a handle. The cup is a shape that being is in, for now, and so are you.

The cup comes first, and it comes apart. It has a colour, so remove the colour. It has a weight, remove that. It has a shape, a temperature, a place on the desk, a history of being made in a factory and bought and washed. Remove each of those in turn. At every step you are removing a property, and at every step there is still something you are removing it from. Now ask what is left when the last property is gone. The modern answer is: nothing. A cup is its properties, and when they are all gone there is no cup, and nothing was ever underneath. That answer is right about the cup. It is right that no hidden pellet of cup-stuff was ever sitting under the colour and the weight. But notice what the properties were doing the whole time. Each of them was there. The colour existed, the weight existed, the place on the desk existed. You removed the properties one by one, and you never once removed the existing, because the existing was not on the list. It was what every item on the list was doing.

The same thing done to yourself is harder to be calm about. Remove your name, your body, your memories, your habits, the sound of your own voice in your head. At every step something is being removed from something. The modern view says that at the end there is nothing, and the modern view is right about that too. You are your properties, and no small hard self was ever hiding under them. But the existing was never one of the properties, and it was never waiting at the bottom. It was the one thing that was true of every single item on the way down.

So there are two things in the room, and they are not two things of the same kind. There are shapes: the colour, the weight, the name, the memory. And there is the existing, which every shape is doing and no shape is. Ibn Arabi's first move is to refuse to treat the second as nothing just because it is not on the list. His words, in the enormous book he called the Meccan Openings, are that there is no existence except God's, and no effect except the shapes'.4 The second half will matter later; the first half is the claim. That sentence is easy to hear as a religious slogan, and it is nothing of the kind. It says that being is not shared out among things like money. There is one existing. Things do not each own a piece of it. They are the shapes it is currently in.

The picture that helps here is a wave. A wave is real. You can surf it, it can drown you, it has a height and a speed. But go looking for the wave apart from the water, and there is nothing to find. The wave is water in a shape, and when the shape passes there is no wave left over anywhere, only water. The cup stands to being as the wave stands to the sea. The picture has an edge, and here it is, so the picture is not trusted too far. Water is a stuff, a thing with properties of its own, and being has no properties, so being is no stuff. The wave teaches one thing only: that a thing can be entirely real and still own nothing of its own. Whether being is some kind of material, the wave cannot say, and it should not be asked.

Ibn Arabi did not invent this step. He inherited it from the philosophers before him, and the shape of it is simple. Take anything in the world and ask whether it had to exist. The cup did not have to. It could have not been made, and one day it will be broken, and the world will go on. Everything you can point to is like that. It exists, but nothing about it requires that it exist. So its existing must come from outside it. And if the thing it comes from also did not have to exist, the same question moves one step back. Nothing has been explained. At some point there has to be something whose existing is not borrowed. The philosophers called it the necessary being, and they thought of it as the first and greatest thing.5 Ibn Arabi takes the same step and then adds one the philosophers did not take. He notices that the borrowing never stops. The cup did not borrow existence once, at the factory, and then own it. The cup owns nothing about its existing right now, at this moment, while you look at it. It is a loan that is never paid off and never finished, only renewed. And then the step that is his own, and it is easiest to see against the man who stopped one step short of it. A Latin theologian of the next generation read the same philosophers Ibn Arabi read. He drew the same line between what a thing is and that it is. Existence, he wrote, is something other than the essence.6 The cup's being there is not part of the description of a cup. It is a further thing, given to the cup, and it can be withdrawn. So far the two men agree, word for word. Then the theologian says: and that given existing is the cup's own. It is a real act of being that belongs to the cup, lent by God but the cup's. A lent coat is on your back, not the lender's. Ibn Arabi says: no. A lent existence is the lender's. There is no copy handed over, no second act of being that is the cup's and not God's. There is one existing, and the cup is a shape it is in. That is the premise everything hangs on, and he does not derive it. The theologian cannot refute it, and Ibn Arabi cannot prove it. It is the fork in the road, and everything after it is what follows down his branch.

That is the whole first claim, and it needs its strongest opponent now, before anything is built on it. The opponent is the philosopher Kant, and his objection is one of the most famous sentences in philosophy. His example was money. A hundred real dollars, he wrote, contain no more than a hundred possible dollars.7 The description of the money is exactly the same whether the money is in your pocket or only in your imagination. So being, he concluded, is evidently not a real predicate.8 Those are the words of one of his English translators, and the point is plain in any language. "Exists" adds nothing to the description of a thing. It only says that the description has something answering to it.

This is very close to Ibn Arabi, and the place where it turns away is the whole disagreement. Both of them agree that existing is not on the list of properties. Both agree that you cannot find it by adding up what the cup is like. Kant concludes: so it is nothing in the thing, only a mark we put on descriptions that have been matched. Ibn Arabi concludes: so it is the one item in the room that is not a thing. Who is right? Kant's sentence is a general claim about the word, and it is right as far as it goes. You cannot get existence out of a description, however perfect the description is, and he used that to break a famous proof of God. Ibn Arabi does not disagree, and he never tries that proof. His claim is about what a description is a description of, which is a different question from what "exists" adds to one. A hundred real dollars and a hundred imagined dollars are described the same way. But one of the two is happening, and the happening is the difference between being rich and being poor. Kant says: that difference is just the description having an instance, and no extra ingredient is needed. Ibn Arabi says: call it what you like, the instance is there, and its being there is what I am talking about. Neither can force the other. Kant has shown that "exists" is not on the list. He has not shown that what is off the list is nothing. Ibn Arabi has taken what is off the list and called it one. He has not shown that it can be counted at all, and by his own account it is not the kind of thing that descriptions reach.

So the objection does not refute the claim, and the claim does not answer the objection. The honest state of things, then, because the rest of the argument stands on it. If existing cannot be found in any description, then Ibn Arabi's claim cannot be proven from the description side either. You cannot start from what cups and people are like and reason your way to the oneness of being. He knows this. He says of some of what he knows that it is known only by tasting. Setting up a proof for it, he writes, is of no use.9 That is either the most honest sentence in the system or the escape hatch that lets him say anything. The ruling on it comes near the end. For now what matters is the shape of where things stand. The claim that being is one survives the best objection there is, and no argument proves it either. It is a way of reading the one fact that every description skips.

So, in the cup's terms. Take every property off the cup and nothing remains, and the modern view is right about that. But the existing was never a property, so it was never taken off, and it was never found at the bottom either. It is the one thing every property was doing. The cup owns nothing of it. The cup is a shape being lent, and the loan is renewed at every moment, not paid once. The best objection, that existence adds nothing to a description, is true and beside the point, because the claim is about what descriptions are of. And the claim that the lent being is one, and the lender's, is his premise and not his conclusion.

Now the question that follows at once, and that has got this philosophy called heresy for eight hundred years. If there is only one existing, and it is God's, then what is the cup. Is the cup God. If it is, then God is a cup and a desk and a pain in your knee, and the word has stopped meaning anything. If it is not, then the cup has some being of its own after all, and everything just said is false. Ibn Arabi's answer is a formula with two halves that sound like a contradiction. The world is not He. And the world is not other than He. Both halves turn out to be exact, and a shadow is what shows it.

Part 2: Not He, and Not Other Than He

This time the claim is a formula, and the formula sounds like a dodge until it is worked through. The world is not the Real. And the world is not other than the Real. Ibn Arabi means both halves at full strength, and both are exact. Drop either half and the cup either becomes God or gets a being of its own. A being of its own is what was just ruled out.

Start with a man standing in the sun, and a shadow on the ground. Ask whether the shadow is the man. Plainly not. You cannot shake hands with it, it has no weight, and when he steps into the shade it is gone. Now ask whether the shadow is something other than the man, a second thing in the yard beside him. Also not. It has his outline and no other. Every movement it makes is his movement. It has nothing of its own, and yet it is there on the ground and you can point to it. The shadow is a thing that is entirely real and entirely his, and is not him.

Then a second picture. Pour water into a blue glass and the water is blue. Pour it into a red one and it is red. Is the colour the water's? No: the water has no colour of its own, and the same water in a clear glass shows none. Is the colour something other than the water, a separate blue thing sitting in the glass beside it? Also no. There is only water in the glass. The blue is what the water looks like from inside that shape, and nowhere else. Ibn Arabi used both pictures. In the Fusus, in the chapter he gave to Joseph, he wrote of what is called "other than the Real", what is named the world. It stands to the Real, he says, as a shadow stands to a person. It is God's shadow.11 On the same page he wrote of light seen through glass, which takes the colour of the glass while in itself it has no colour.12 And the water line he took from an older teacher: the colour of the water is the colour of its vessel.13

What the shadow and the coloured water share is a relation with no everyday name, and the formula is the name. The shadow, the coloured water, and the cup all have a shape that is entirely their own and a being that is entirely borrowed. So there are two true things to say about each of them, and they pull in opposite directions. Looked at as a shape, the cup is a cup and not God, because God is not a shape. Looked at as an existing, the cup is nothing but the one being, because there is no other existing for it to be. "Not He" is the shape speaking. "Not other than He" is the being speaking. Both are said of one cup, and they do not contradict each other, because they are answers to different questions.

Now the two beliefs the formula is built to refuse, each said at its strongest. Each is what most people hear when they meet this philosophy. The first is that everything is God. It sounds like the natural conclusion. If being is one and it is God's, then the cup is God. So is the desk, and the pain in your knee, and the man who hurt you. This view has a real appeal, and the appeal is worth hearing in full. It abolishes distance. There is nothing anywhere that is not holy, and every wall between you and the world comes down at once. Here is why Ibn Arabi will not say it. If the cup is God, then God is a cup. God has a handle and can be broken. And if everything is God, then God is the sum of everything: the total of all the shapes, added up. But a sum is itself a shape. It is the largest shape, but it is a shape, and being, as the cup showed, is no shape at all. So "everything is God" makes God the biggest thing in the room. Ibn Arabi's God is the only thing in the room that is not a thing. The formula's first half, "not He", exists to block exactly this, the way the shadow is blocked from being the man.

The second belief is the opposite one, and most religious people hold it without noticing. God is a being who made the world and stands outside it. He is over there, the world is over here, and there is a gap between with prayer going one way across it. This too has a real appeal, and it is the appeal of not being swallowed. It leaves you a self and God a self, two persons who can speak. Here is why it fails. If God is over there and the world over here, then there are two existings, God's and the world's. The world has some being of its own, enough to stand at a distance from him. But that is what the cup could not have. The cup owns nothing of its existing, at any moment, while you look at it. A world that stands apart from God is a world that owns its being, and there is no such world. The formula's second half, "not other than He", exists to block this. The shadow is not a second person in the yard.

So each of the two familiar beliefs keeps half the truth and throws away the other half. The one that says everything is God keeps the borrowing and loses the shape. The one that says God is far away keeps the shape and loses the borrowing. What is kept from each, said plainly, is this. From the first: there is no second being anywhere, so nothing is at a distance from God. From the second: the cup is not God, so the shape is real and it is yours. What is thrown away from each is the part that made it easy. The oneness is not a sum, and the distance is not a gap.

One note, because the phrase will be met everywhere else: "the oneness of being" is a label later writers gave this teaching.14 So far as anyone knows, he never used the phrase in all his enormous output. That does not make the idea less his. It means the slogan was made by readers, and the slogan is exactly the half-truth the first half of the formula refuses.

On the same page he has already made the light the Real, so the light that casts the shadow is the man. A picture with no edge is a picture a listener will trust too far, and this one has its edge exactly there.

Now the question this leaves open, and it is the one every serious reader of him asks. If there is one being, and it is complete, and it needs nothing, why is there a shadow at all. A man who is whole does not need his shadow. The cup exists, and existing is lent to it, and nothing about the lender required a cup. So what is the cup for. Ibn Arabi's answer is that a certain kind of thing can only exist in pairs, and mercy is one of them.

Part 3: Why There Is Anything

The claim here answers the question the shadow left standing: why there is a world at all, if the one being is complete. Ibn Arabi's answer is that the one being has aspects, which he calls the Names, and the Names are relations rather than things. A relation cannot show until it has two ends. Mercy needs someone to be shown mercy. The world exists because the Names cannot show without it, and the world is the showing.

A woman is a doctor. She has the training, the skill, the wish to heal. Now put her alone on an island with no one sick, no one at all, for the rest of her life. Is she still a doctor? In one sense yes: nothing has been taken from her. In another sense the word has gone hollow. Doctoring is something that happens between a doctor and a patient. Without the patient the skill is real and the doctoring never occurs. Or a man has been wronged, and he is ready to forgive. Then the person who wronged him dies before they meet again. The readiness is real. The forgiving never happens, because forgiving is not a state a man is in. It is a thing that passes between two.

What the doctor and the forgiver share is this. Some words name a thing that can sit alone: a stone, a colour, a weight. Other words name a relation, and a relation cannot show until it has both its ends. The Merciful is a word of the second kind. So are the Forgiving, the Provider, the Creator. There is no mercy on an island with one person. Ibn Arabi saw this and drew the conclusion that makes his account of creation different from the ordinary one. In the Meccan Openings he writes that the Names are relations.17 They are the differing ways the one being stands toward us, and our existing is what asks for them. And those relations, he says, are what scripture is naming when it lists the Most Beautiful Names. The Names are the ways the one being stands to what it shows, rather than ninety-nine things inside God. And most of them have no showing at all without something to stand toward.

Part 4: What You Were Before You Were

Now the claim that decides everything after it. Every thing has a fixed shape that the one being knew before the thing existed. That shape never itself exists. The thing is that shape, being shown. Ibn Arabi calls these shapes the fixed entities. Everything so far forces them on him, whether or not anyone likes where they lead.

Go back to the cup and the water, because the case is already on the desk. Water is poured into the cup and takes its edges. Ask now what each of the two gives the other. The cup gives the water its limits: this wide, this deep, this curve at the rim. Without the cup the water is a puddle with no shape of its own. The water gives the cup something it did not have: a filled shape, something actually there inside the outline. Without the water the cup's inside is a shape with nothing in it. Neither one is the other. The cup is not water and the water is not cup. And yet what you see on the desk, the filled cup, is nothing but the two together.

Now strip the case down, and the particulars go. In place of the water, put being: the one existing from the start, which has no shape of its own. In place of the cup, put a possibility: a way that being could be shaped. The filled cup is a thing in the world. Being gives it that it is there. The possibility gives it what it is: this and not that, a cup and not a desk, you and not your neighbour. And then the general form, which is the same as the case with the details removed. Every existing thing is two things at once: a shape that says what it is, and a being that says that it is. The shape is entirely its own. The being is entirely borrowed.

Here is the step that makes his account strange, and it is the step the argument cannot avoid. The cup exists. It sits on the desk with its shape and its being both present. But the shape itself, the pure possibility, this-way-and-not-that: does that exist? The answer has to be no. If the shape existed on its own, it would be a second being beside the one being, and that was ruled out at the start. If the shape were nothing at all, then being would have nothing to be shaped by. There would be one undivided existing, and no cup, no desk, no you. So the shape must be real without existing. Ibn Arabi's line for this is in the Fusus. In the chapter he gave to Idris, he says that the entities in their fixed non-existence have not smelled a scent of the existent.25 They are in no other place, and they wait in no storeroom. They never exist at all. They are known.

When you know what a cup is, the cup you know is not you. The knowing is yours; what is known is a cup, and stays a cup. The fixed entity of the cruel man is God's known, and it is never God's being. Then the objection every reader with some philosophy raises within a minute. This is Plato with the names changed. Now the question, and everyone can feel it coming. If what you are is a shape settled before you existed, and God's decree only reads it, then whose act is what you do. When you are cruel, or kind, or lazy, or brave, was that the water or the cup. On this account the being was lent, and the cruelty was in the shape. Which means it was never up to anyone, and also that it was nobody's fault but yours. Ibn Arabi says both of those, in one breath, and does not blink. He is held to it next.

Part 5: Whose Act Is It

The claim now is blunt, because he is blunt. In Ibn Arabi's system nothing you do could have gone otherwise, and he does not soften that. His response to it is not to soften the blame either but to sharpen it: there is no one to blame but yourself. Both halves are true on his account, and they do not cancel. The second is harder than it sounds and more useful than it looks.

There is a man who is stingy. He is the example here, not Ibn Arabi's, and he stands in for any trait you like. The man does not tip. He counts what he lent you. He is not cruel and not stupid; he is simply tight, and has been since he was a boy. The ordinary religious picture says God made him and could have made him generous. The ordinary modern picture says his genes and his childhood made him, and he might have turned out otherwise with different luck. Both pictures agree on one thing: somewhere there is a version of this man who is not stingy, and the real man fell short of it.

Ibn Arabi denies that version exists. Run the cup and the water over the man. Being is the water, lent to him at every moment. The stinginess is in the shape, in what he is, the fixed entity that never existed and was known. God gave being to a stingy man. God did not add the stinginess to a neutral man, the way a potter adds a handle. There was never a neutral man for it to be added to. The one being took the shape of this man. This man's shape has stinginess in it, the way the cup's shape has a curve at the rim. In the Fusus, in the chapter on Abraham, he writes that the known is you and your states. Knowledge has no effect on the known, he says. Rather, the known has an effect on knowledge.30 God knows the man as stingy because the man is stingy. The knowing follows the shape, and does not make it.

The natural answer is that the man's traits were formed, by his upbringing and by his own earlier choices, and could have been formed differently. There was no first moment when a man with no traits chose his first one. Go back as far as you like and there is always already a shape doing the choosing. There is a finding from another field that bears on one small step of this, and it is kept to that step. The step was taken at the cup: strip your properties away and no small hard self is found at the bottom. The border of "me", on that account, is not a hard line found in the world. Part 6: Nothing Twice

The claim to be earned now is that nothing of the cup lasts, not even for a second. That follows from what has already been said, rather than being a new doctrine bolted on. Creation is renewed at every instant. No showing repeats. What you call the same cup, sitting on the desk for an hour, is a series of showings that rhyme. The one thing that does persist under them is not a cup. And what you call the same self is the same kind of series, held together by something that never existed.

A candle flame. It looks like one thing. It has an edge, a colour, a height, and it stays where it is for an hour. But nothing in it stays. The gas that is burning now is not the gas that was burning a moment ago. The flame is a shape that a stream passes through, and the shape holds still while everything in it is replaced. Or a film of a man walking across a room. It looks like motion. It is a stack of still pictures, each one slightly different, shown too fast for the eye to catch the gaps. There is no walking in the film. There is a walking man in each frame, and the walking is what you see between them.

Both pictures have an edge, and it is the same edge. In the flame, the gas that streams through is a stuff with a shape of its own, a thing that would be there without the flame. In the film, the frames are real pictures, each with a walking man already in it. In both cases what persists under the change is itself shaped. In Ibn Arabi's version the one thing that persists has no shape at all. What streams through the cup is the one being, and the one being is not a cup, not a gas, not a picture of anything. So nothing cup-shaped lasts from one instant to the next. That is what makes his version different from every ordinary picture of change, and it is what has to be earned here.

Here is where it comes from, and it is two earlier results pushed one step. The cup showed that being is lent to it and that the loan is never paid off, only renewed. The water in the cup showed that the shape of the cup, the fixed entity, never exists at all. Put those two together. What is there on the desk is a shape being lent being. The shape cannot persist, because it does not exist. The being cannot persist as this cup, because being has no shape of its own to hold. So there is nothing cup-shaped on the desk that could last from one moment to the next. There is only the lending, and the lending is an act, and an act is over as soon as it is done. The next moment's cup is the next act of lending. It rhymes with the last one because the shape it lends to is the same shape, known and never existing. But nothing cup-shaped crossed the gap. Nothing was carried over.

He found this in scripture, and he read it there against the usual reading. The Qur'an says of the unbelievers, in the fiftieth chapter, that they are in doubt about a new creation.36 The standard translations render it as the resurrection: they doubt that God can make them again after death. Ibn Arabi takes it as a description of now. In the Fusus, in the chapter he gave to Shu'ayb, he writes of the people of unveiling. They see God showing himself at every breath, he says, and never repeating a showing.37 The new creation is not a future event. It is what is happening to the cup while you look at it, faster than the eye can tell. He offers this as something seen, not proven, and the argument above is the reason it should be taken seriously anyway.

Part 7: The Weakest Joint

What has to be said here is a concession, made early so that nothing that follows leans on what it cannot hold. Ibn Arabi's account of evil is the weakest joint in his system. He says that evil is lack: good is being and evil is the absence of being. And he says that every name of God, including the ones that harm, is a face of the one being. What follows gives that account at full strength, shows what it can carry, and then stops at what it cannot say and says so.

A man is thirsty. He has walked all day and there is no water, and the thirst is a pain in the throat and the head that will not stop. Then there is water, and he drinks, and the pain is gone. Ask what the pain was. It was the absence of water, felt. Nothing was added to him and then removed. A missing thing showed up as pain. And then a child in pain, in a hospital bed, from a disease with a long name. The pain is the absence of nothing the child can name. It is no thirst, and it points at no cup of water. It is simply there, and it is in the child. The child turns to no one, because the child is too young to know there is anyone to turn to.

Ibn Arabi's account is built for the first case, and the second is where it stops.

Here is the account. In the Meccan Openings he writes that good is entirely existence and evil is entirely non-existence.42 Elsewhere: non-existence is the evil, and evil is non-existence.43 Take the cup on the desk and it makes sense. Everything that exists is the one being in a shape, and the one being is what he calls the Real. So everything that is, insofar as it is, is good. Evil cannot be a thing, because if it were, it would be being in a shape, and then it would be good. So evil must be where being is not. The thirsty man's pain is a lack of water. The blindness of a blind man is a lack of sight. The cruelty of a cruel man is a lack of the mercy that should be there. Every evil, on this account, is a hole in the shape, felt from inside.

Part 8: The God You Tied

The claim now is the one that got Ibn Arabi called an unbeliever. It is stated here at full strength before it is defended. Every believer worships a God of their own shaping. The knower of God recognises him in every shape. And this is no relativism, because the error was never in the object. The error is the knot: the act of tying the one being to one shape and denying it in the rest.

Two people are praying in one room. One of them is praying to a God who is a father, who listens. That God can be hurt by what she does and pleased by what she does. The other is praying to a God with no face, who is beyond all likeness, who cannot be pleased or hurt because nothing reaches him. These two Gods cannot both exist. A God who can be hurt is not a God nothing reaches. Yet both people are praying, and both are turned toward something, and the ordinary view says at most one of them is right. Ibn Arabi's view is that each is right about what they see and wrong about what they deny. The one being does show as a father who listens, in the shape the first woman's heart can hold. It does show as what no likeness reaches, in the shape the second one's mind can hold. What each has done wrong is not the seeing. It is the tying: the decision that the shape they see is the whole, and the other shape is false.

Here is the text, because the text is stronger than any summary and stranger. In the Fusus, in the chapter he gave to Noah, he takes up the idolaters of Noah's people. In the Qur'an they urge each other not to abandon their gods, and five idols are named.53 Noah preaches against them. They refuse, and they drown. That is the story every reader knows. Then Ibn Arabi writes this. The Real has in every object of worship a face, which he who knows it knows, and he who is ignorant of it is ignorant of.54 And then the sentence that has been quoted against him ever since. If they had abandoned them, they would have been ignorant of the Real in the measure of what they abandoned.55

Each is right about the face and wrong to deny the other. The idolaters of Noah's story were turned toward a face too, and their flood is reread as knowledge. That is the critic's best card, and it is given to him. The critic is right that the text says this. He is wrong that it abolishes the difference between belief and unbelief, because the difference is the knot, the knot is called ignorance, and it costs. And the law stayed in his hand, which is what a man who thought all worship equal would not have written.

There is one more place where a person ties the one being to a single face and refuses it everywhere else. It is not a creed. It is a person. A person, loved, is a face of the one being too, and the ruling on the stone reaches the lover as well. The face is real, and the refusal of every other face is the error. What that does to love, and to grief, comes next.

Part 9: The One You Love

Now the one place a person guards hardest. Everyone you have ever loved was the one being in a face. That is why love never fits the person it is aimed at, and why it does not end when they do. The face was real, and it was never the whole. And the first mistake Ibn Arabi names in love is a mistake about what is loved, not a mistake in loving.

Think of someone you loved who did not deserve it. Not a story from a book: an actual person, and the year or two when you could not see straight. Everyone else could see what they were. You could too, at moments, at three in the morning, and it made no difference. What you loved was not what they were. Every friend who tried to tell you so was right about the person and wrong about the love. The love was aimed at something. The something was not in the list of their properties, because you had the list and it did not help. Then think of someone you loved who died. The shape is gone. A love that was a response to a person's properties should have ended with the properties. Ask anyone who has buried someone whether it did. It goes on aiming, at nothing, for years, and that is the fact to be explained.

Ibn Arabi's explanation is one sentence, and it is in the chapter on love in the Meccan Openings. No one has ever loved anything but his Creator.68 But he has been veiled from him, Ibn Arabi goes on. Veiled by the love of Zaynab and Su'ad and the rest of the names from the old love poems. And by the world and money and rank, and by every beloved there is. On the same page: the poets were praising him all along, from behind the veil of forms.69 And then the argument that makes it more than a pious reversal. If you loved for beauty, he writes, you loved nothing but God, because beauty is his.70 If you loved for kindness, the same. Whatever it was in the person that you loved, that thing was the one being showing in them. There is nothing else in anyone that could be there. In every respect, he concludes, the object of love is only God.

Part 10: The Third Thing

The claim here is that there is a level of reality between pure meaning and solid body. It is real, and it is no fantasy. It is where the formula "not He, not other than He" stops being a puzzle and becomes a description of something everyone has seen. Ibn Arabi calls it the in-between, and you were in it last night.

You dream of a parent who has died. They are there. They speak in their voice and they stand the way they stood, and you are glad, and then you wake. Were they there? No: they are dead, and nothing was in the room. Were they not there? Also no, and anyone who has had the dream knows why the second no is as strong as the first. That was them, present, and you spoke to them, and no photograph or memory does that. Then a mirror. You look in it and there is a face. Is that face you? You would say yes without thinking. Is it you? There is nothing behind the glass. You cannot touch it, and when you step away it is gone. Ibn Arabi used exactly this case, and his words for it are careful. A man perceives his form in the mirror, he writes, and he knows for certain that he perceived his form, in one respect.79 The sense of the passage is that in another respect he did not, because there is nothing behind the glass.

The modern objection is the one every listener has ready, and it has a better form than it is usually given. Dreams are brain events. The dead parent was a pattern of activity in a sleeping cortex. The unicorn is neither present nor absent; it is what the thought is of, and there is nothing puzzling in that. So there is no third level. Here is what can be said in reply, and here is where the reply stops, because it does stop. Ibn Arabi would not deny that the dream happens in you. Where else would it happen? And he would not deny that the parent is what the dream is about. The disagreement is about what a content is. The modern account says it is a feature of the vehicle. The brain event is about the parent the way a map is about a city, and there is nothing more. Ibn Arabi says a content is a region of being, the level where meanings have faces. The brain event is where that region is showing tonight. Neither can force the other, and the argument here should not pretend otherwise. Part 11: Mercy Comes First

The boldest claim in the system comes now, and it follows from what has already been argued rather than from hope. The Names are not equal. Mercy comes first: wrath exists only because mercy let it exist, and there is no judgement that mercy does not reach. So the last word of every judgement is mercy. The people of the Fire stay in the Fire and are not, in the end, in pain. Ibn Arabi holds this. He is entitled to part of it, and the question is where his entitlement stops.

Begin with the sentence from scripture that stands against him, and with what a listener hears in it. The Qur'an says of the unbelievers that they abide in the Fire forever.90 A listener hears: they are tormented forever. Now look at what the sentence attaches the forever to. It says they abide, that they stay. The everlastingness sits on the staying, and there is no word for pain in the verse. That observation is not his, and it does not settle anything. Other verses do speak of the pain, and one of them says it is never lightened.91 What the observation does is show the room a reading would need, and Ibn Arabi takes exactly that room. He keeps the forever. He quotes the not-lightened verse himself, and answers it by giving the torment a time rather than by softening it. And he says the people of the Fire come to a bliss anyway, which sounds impossible until his picture of it arrives.

Part 12: Two Eyes

The question now is how any of this can be known. Ibn Arabi holds that there are two ways of knowing God, reason and direct seeing. Each alone is a one-eyed man. And the end of knowing is not certainty but a bewilderment he prays for. What follows says what that means, then hands him the strongest objection to it, which is his own. Then it rules on what a stranger can take from it.

Two Gods, first, and the two ways of reaching them. The first God is reached by thinking. You start from the one being with no shape, and you strip. No body, because bodies are shapes, and no place, because places are shapes. Neither angry nor pleased, neither near nor far, because every one of those is a likeness to something in the world. When you have finished stripping, you have a God that nothing reaches, that no word fits, that cannot be pictured or prayed to. This is the God the theologians of his own tradition built, and the one his Noah chapter is aimed at. The second God is the one in scripture. He has a face. He has hands. He sits on a throne and comes down in the last third of the night and is pleased with one man and angry with another. He is the father who listens, from the room with the two women.

Each of these Gods is reached by one faculty, and each faculty, used alone, gets one of them and loses the other. Reason strips, and its God is the first one. The heart receives, and its God is the second. Ibn Arabi's verses on this are in the Noah chapter, and they are blunt. If you speak only of the God beyond all likeness, you are binding him, he says. If you speak only of the God who is like things, you are bounding him.99 And if you speak of the two together, you are on the mark.100 The same page adds a rule that stops this being a licence. If you think of yourself as a second thing beside God, it says, do not lean on the likeness.101 You will make him a thing like you. If you think of yourself as nothing beside him, do not lean on the stripping, or you will lose him altogether. The both-together is the mark for the one who has both eyes, and not a permission for anyone to say anything. The opening of that chapter says why the first alone fails, and it is a surprise. Stripping God of every likeness, he writes, is itself a way of limiting him.102 The theologians thought they were freeing God from every shape. They had tied him to one: the shape of having no shape. That is a knot like any other.

Part 13: The Word That Hears

The claim to be earned now is that the human being is the one word in the sentence that can hear the sentence. That is what Ibn Arabi means by the perfect human. Once more at the mirror, then. The world is an unpolished mirror: every shape is there and none of them sees. Now the last question, and it is the plainest one. What does a person do with this on a Tuesday. Not a saint, not a scholar. A person with a cup on a desk, a stingy streak, and a God they tied. Part 14: What It Does to a Life

The last claim is that the practice is subtraction. There is nothing to acquire, nothing to reach, no state to attain. There is only what to stop claiming, and the whole of what Ibn Arabi asks of a person is to stop claiming it. What follows is what that means at a desk, in a complaint, in a prayer, at a death. Then the one thing that stays hard after all of it.

First the skeleton, in plain steps, so that the whole of it can be carried out in one hand. One: existence is not a property of things; it is the one thing there, and everything else is the shape it is in. Two: so the world is not God and not other than God: a shadow, a word, a face in a glass. Three: there is a world because the one being has relations, and a relation needs two ends. Four: every thing has a shape that was known before it existed and never exists itself, and God's knowing follows the shape. Five: so your acts are the shape's, there was no otherwise, and the blame lands on you. Six: nothing of the cup lasts; every instant is a fresh lending. Seven: evil is lack, and the account cannot say why a lack hurts, or speak to the child. Eight: every believer worships a God they shaped, and the error is the knot. Nine: everyone you have loved was the one being in a face, and the face was real. Ten: shapes live at a level between meaning and body, and you were there last night. Eleven: mercy comes first, wrath exists only inside it, and the last word of every judgement is mercy. Twelve: reason strips and the heart receives, and using both is bewilderment. Thirteen: the human is the pupil the light goes through, and hearing the whole sentence ends in a servant. That is the entire system, and every step was because, not then.

Now the Tuesday, and the cup is still on the desk. Nothing has changed about the cup. What has changed is what you take yourself to be looking at. It is a word on the same breath as you. The wall between you and it is still there, as an edge, and was never a border. Ibn Arabi has a line for what the self actually is, and it runs against every teaching that calls the self an illusion. A modern dictionary of his terms records it. From yourself you know him, he says, not from another, because you are the nearest veil.126 The self, far from being nothing, is the last thing in the way, and the closest, and the one you look through.

The death. Someone you love dies, and the shape is gone from the world. Here the system says less than a person wants and more than they expect, and both should be stated. Now the thing that stays hard. The servant claims nothing. Not the being, not the shape, not the acts, not the hearing. That was the whole result of the pupil and the polish.
