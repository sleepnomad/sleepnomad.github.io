Cold open

An empire does not fall because something breaks its strength. It falls because winning spends it.1 A small, poor people can take a rich country because of a bond between them.2 Each of them is ready to stand and die for the others. Every prize of winning wears that bond away: the safety, the money, the walls, the laws, the palace, the ruler who no longer needs anyone.3456 So the fall is not the opposite of the success. It is the success, carried forward three or four generations.

Most people, asked why a great power declines, reach for one of two answers. Either its leaders went bad, through greed or stupidity or corruption, or something hit it from outside. The smart version of the same belief says that strength is made of things. Money, armies, roads, sound institutions, laws that are kept. A state that keeps those in good repair lasts, and a state that lets them rot falls. On that view, decline is a mistake that a competent ruler could have avoided.

That belief is reasonable, and it fails on the record Ibn Khaldun spent his life watching. In his own region, great ruling houses with full treasuries and working courts fell anyway, one after another. And the people who beat them were nearly always poorer, fewer and less organised.15 If strength were made of things, the richer side would win. It kept losing. What the poorer side had was a bond. What the richer side had done, without anyone deciding to do it, was spend its own bond on becoming rich.

Part 1: How to Catch a Lie in History

A respected historian writes that a certain ancient leader counted his army in the desert and found six hundred thousand fighting men. The historian is famous, careful in other places, and quoted by everyone after him. You cannot ask him where he got the figure, because he has been dead for centuries. So the question is how anyone could check it. The answer Ibn Khaldun gave is the first move of everything that follows. You do not check a report about the past by asking whether the reporter was honest. You check it by asking whether the world could have produced it, and for that you need a science of how human societies work.20 Nobody had built that science, so he built it.

There is an obvious objection here. This is just common sense. Anyone who stops to think knows an army has to eat. There is no need to call it a new science. Two answers, both from the text. Part 2: Why People Have Rulers

Many people carry a quiet belief that government is optional. On this view, rulers are laid on top of ordinary life, a cost we pay or a weight we put up with. A decent enough people, the thought goes, could manage without one. Ibn Khaldun thinks that belief is wrong, and his argument against it begins with a loaf of bread. His claim has two halves. The first is that rule is a necessity of human life, not a choice. The second is that who rules is never settled by wisdom or by right, but by force, and that force does not mean numbers or money.

He starts with the bread. The food one person needs for a single day is a little wheat, say. Before anyone can eat it, it has to be ground, kneaded and baked. Each of those steps needs tools, and the tools come from the blacksmith, the carpenter and the potter. One step further back, the wheat itself has to be sown, reaped and threshed. Behind that one day's food stands a whole crowd of people. A farmer, a miller, a baker, a smith, a carpenter, a potter, and whoever made their tools in turn. No single person can do all of it and still have a day left to live in. So, in his words, a man cannot do without a combination of many powers from among his fellow beings.35

Part 4: The Bond That Wins

A hundred brave people who each look after themselves can lose to twenty who move as one. That is easy to accept and hard to explain, because the twenty are not braver, stronger or better armed. Ibn Khaldun's explanation is the idea his whole book is built around. What the twenty have is a bond that makes each of them defend the others as he would defend himself. The bond grows out of kinship, but what really builds it is years of living and suffering together. And a group that has it in strength will, by its nature, reach for rule.

Two scenes show the bond before it has a name. In the first, bandits fall on a crowded market. There are hundreds of people present, many of them strong, some of them armed. Almost everyone runs. Each person is thinking about their own skin, and a man who stops to fight for a stranger is likely to fight alone. In the second, the same bandits fall on a camp of forty herders. Every man there turns towards the attack. A blow to a cousin is felt as a blow to himself, and each one knows, without asking, that the others will be beside him. The market had more people and more weapons. The camp is the one the bandits will not try twice.

The only difference between the two scenes is whether each person feels an attack on the others as an attack on himself. That is what Ibn Khaldun calls asabiyya, usually translated as group feeling. It is the readiness of a group's members to defend each other as one body, and to pay for it. It is what turns many people into one force.66 The word has a darker meaning as well, blind loyalty to one's clan right or wrong. His own religion condemned that, and he agrees, limiting the condemnation to a group feeling that is used for worthless purposes.67 The bond itself he treats as neutral, like strength in an arm, and that neutral bond is the sense used from here on.

The objection comes from two directions at once. A believer may say he has reduced faith to a tool for power. A sceptic may say he has stumbled on the debunking explanation and then lost his nerve. He has done neither, and the reason is in what his claim is about. It is not about whether a faith is true. It is about what a shared faith does to the inside of a group, which is to switch off the rivalry among its members. That effect is real whether or not the faith is true, which is why both the believer and the sceptic can test it. On his account, any shared cause that its members will die for should work the same way, by ending the competition inside the group. That is an inference from his argument, not a claim he made, but nothing in his mechanism limits it to religion.

Faith, then, is an amplifier, not a source. Joined to a strong bond, it can make a small people unstoppable. Standing alone, it gathers a crowd that scatters at the first charge.

What has been built so far is the whole story of the rise. A hard life makes a dense bond. A shared faith can multiply it. The bond pushes outwards until it reaches the city and the throne. And that is exactly where the story turns. The family at the head of the group has won. It now holds the state, the treasury and the army. The next question is what happens to the bond that carried it there, and his answer is that the new ruler goes after it himself.

Part 6: The King Turns on His Own

A hard, close-knit people has won. The family at its head now sits on a throne, with a treasury, a palace and a country to run. The natural guess is that the new king rewards the kinsmen who fought beside him, and rules through them. They are, after all, the people he can trust most. Ibn Khaldun says the opposite happens, and that it is in the very nature of royal power. Royal power, by its nature, will not be shared. So the founder's house pushes out the very kinsmen whose bond carried it to power, and replaces them with clients and paid followers. Every step of that is rational, and it is the first step of the dynasty's death.

He puts the claim in the heading of a section, and the heading is worth hearing whole. This is the point where his theory first shows its real shape. The rise and the fall are not two separate stories. The same act that makes the king secure is the act that starts the dynasty dying. Nobody has to be wicked, greedy or foolish for it to happen. It is enough that the king wants to stay king, and that the only people able to stop him are the people who made him.

The turning point is now in view. The bond that won the throne is cut by the throne itself, for reasons that make perfect sense to the man who cuts it. The dynasty now runs on clients, money and habit. The question that leaves is how long such a dynasty can run. Part 7: Three Generations

A man builds a business from nothing. His son grows up watching him do it and runs it well. His grandson inherits it, keeps it going by doing what was always done, and does not quite know why it was done. His great-grandson thinks the company's standing was simply his family's due, and loses it. Ibn Khaldun says that pattern is not bad luck. A house's power lasts about three or four generations. The bond and the skills that won it are learned by living them, and each generation lives them less. That is his clock, and it can be tested against modern evidence, which supports one half of it and breaks the other.

He states the clock twice, for two different things, and they need to be kept apart. The first is about the standing of a family, its prestige. The second is about the life of a ruling dynasty. They run on the same mechanism, but they turn out to fare very differently under testing.

Part 8: Luxury Becomes Need, and the Tax Curve

A young dynasty is cheap to run. Its rulers still live close to the desert life that made them, and they want little. An old dynasty is ruinously expensive, and the money it needs has to come from somewhere. Part 9: Injustice Ruins Civilization

When a state starts taking what its people make, they stop making it. That sounds like a small point about incentives, and Ibn Khaldun turns it into the account of how a dynasty finally empties. His claim is that injustice by the state, not an outside enemy, is what ruins a kingdom. And he means injustice in a wide sense, wider than theft. Ibn Khaldun's own rule was that the world decides, not the witness.170 So his theory has to face the world as it has since been measured. The result is mixed, and the mixture is clear. The hundred and twenty years fits some dynasties of his region, and fails even for the houses he served himself.

So far the mechanism holds, and the clock turns out to be a local tendency, not a rule even locally. The strongest objection goes further. It says that the modern state has broken the mechanism itself.

Here is the objection in its best form. A desert tribe could build its bond only from shared life, and shared life cannot stretch across a whole nation. A modern nation has millions. None of them has ever met more than a tiny fraction of the others. Yet millions of them will pay taxes, obey laws and, in war, die for each other. Modern states, the objection says, manage this by two means Ibn Khaldun never saw at scale. The first is organisation, the bureaucracy, the schools, the conscript army. The second is ideology, above all the national story. Together they tie millions of small, real bonds into one story people believe.

Those small bonds are the ones every person still has. Families, friends, the soldiers in one unit, the people of one town. Those are the bonds Ibn Khaldun described, and they have not gone anywhere. What the modern state does is tie millions of them together with a story everyone is taught, and an organisation that reaches every village. A bond that once needed a shared desert can now be manufactured by a school system and a flag.

So the objection agrees with the clock studies from another direction: the theory was built on North African cities and tribes, and travels badly outside them.

There is a second half to the objection, about money, and it is an argument rather than a measured result. In Ibn Khaldun's model, the economy grows only as people and farmland grow. So a court whose needs outrun that growth has nowhere to turn but its own subjects. That is what drives the climb up the tax curve and then the seizures. Where an economy grows for generations through technology and trade, a state's income can rise without its rates rising. A state with a growing economy is not forced up the curve the way his dynasties were. That removes one of the engines of his decline, or at least slows it a great deal.

So the ruling has to be careful. Modern states have changed two things. They can manufacture a bond across millions of strangers, and they can often grow their income instead of taking it. Both should slow his clock, though that is argued here, not measured. The slower European cycles measured earlier came from states before modern growth or national ideology, so they cannot settle it. The objection itself concedes something important. The manufactured national bond is built out of the small real bonds. Where those small bonds are strong, the national story has something to tie together. On this argument, where families, towns and units have stopped living a shared life, the story has less to hold. And a state whose growth stops is back on his curve, with costs it cannot cut and a base it can squeeze.

So the modern state did not repeal his mechanism. It changed how fast it runs, and it moved the bond from the tribe to many smaller groups bound together by a story. The question his theory asks of any state is still the right question. It asks whether the bond is being renewed by shared life and real stakes. Or is it being spent on comfort, while the story is left to do the work alone.

Part 12: What Your Group Is Spending

Every group that has won something together is spending the bond that won it. It spends it through exactly the rewards it won: the safety, the money, the comfort, the leader who no longer needs anyone. That is Ibn Khaldun's mechanism with the kings taken out. It applies to a company, a team, a family or a country as well as a dynasty. It also suggests why the usual fixes for a group gone soft can spend the bond faster. A stricter rule, more pay and a stronger boss each take something from it.

The whole cycle can be run once on a case that has nothing to do with deserts. It is an illustration, not a study, but every step in it is one already argued. Five people start a company in a cramped room. Nobody is paid much. Every week brings a new threat, a missed payroll, a lost customer, a rival with more money. Each of them covers for the others without being asked, because each knows that if one fails, all of them fail. That is group feeling, built the way he said it is built, by shared danger and shared life. And it lets five people beat companies a hundred times their size. Most such groups still die young, and the ones that win are the ones whose stories get told. The bond does not guarantee a win. It makes one possible.

They win. The money comes in, and with it the city. A real office, real salaries, real safety. Then the founder does what the king did. The co-founders, who think the company is partly theirs, are the only people who could challenge the founder's control. So they are moved aside, gently, and replaced by hired executives who owe their jobs to the founder alone. Every step is sensible, and every step trades the bond for safety.

The company grows, and the new people never faced the old dangers. They follow the processes the founders set up, and do not know why each one exists. Those who come after them treat the processes as the way things simply are. The rules are now enforced by review and penalty instead of held by conviction. And people learn, as his schoolboys did, to hide mistakes rather than fix them. Costs rise, because comfort has become need. Prices and fees rise to pay for it. The far-off offices and the small customers are neglected first, and the headquarters is the last place to notice anything is wrong. Then a smaller, hungrier rival, five people in a cramped room, takes the market.

Not every company goes this way, and the example proves nothing on its own. What it shows is that each step of his argument has an obvious equivalent outside of kingship. The measured evidence bears on single steps only, and none of it comes from offices.

The step that has been measured best is the first one, that shared danger builds the bond. A group of economists reviewed the research on what happens to people who live through war violence. Nearly twenty studies, from more than forty countries, found the same pattern. Exposure to violence tends to increase social cooperation at the local level, including community participation and prosocial behaviour. And the effect does not fade with time.180 If anything, they found, the opposite seems to be true.
