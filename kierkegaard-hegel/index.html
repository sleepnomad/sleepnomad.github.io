Cold open

Everything about you can be explained from outside. Where you were born, what you earn, what people like you do at your age, why you feel what you feel this evening. The explanations are usually right, and you half believe them. Then something has to be decided, and none of them decides it. You decide, and afterwards you cannot prove to anyone, not even to yourself, that it was right. People tell you it will all make sense later. Later is not when you had to act.

Two men built the two honest answers to that, and they could not both be right. The first was a German professor who died in 1831 with the largest explanation of everything ever attempted behind him.1 In it, every person, every nation and every idea is a moment in one story that reason tells about itself. A moment, in his sense, is not an instant. It is a stage inside a larger thing, the way a chapter is a moment in a book. The story can be read whole. The second was a Danish writer who was eighteen when the German died.2 He read him closely, and spent his short life saying that one thing had been left out. The thing left out was the person who has to live. Hegel said the truth of anything is the whole it belongs to.3 Kierkegaard said the one thing the whole can never hold is the single person deciding, now, in the dark.

Here is the ruling this fight ends in, said flat, before it is earned. The whole can contain everything about your life except your living of it. It can hold what you were, what you did, what it came to and what it meant. All of that can be said, and everything that can be said belongs to everyone. What it cannot hold is the deciding, while it is being done, by the one doing it. That is no fault in Hegel's whole. It is the price of any explanation, because to explain is to look back and to live is to look forward. So the leap Kierkegaard made famous does not break the system. It happens outside the system, in the one place the system cannot look, which is the present. Afterwards the system takes it in, as it takes in everything, and the present is where you are.

That ruling costs both men something, and it costs you something. Hegel's comfort is that history will carry you, and that someone will understand you afterwards. The institutions you live inside are your own freedom made solid. All of that is true and none of it arrives in time to use. Kierkegaard's comfort is that the decision is yours, and that in your own inwardness, before God, you are more than any account of you. That is also true, and it cannot tell you that you chose right, and it cannot tell you from a madman who felt the same certainty. Both consolations are withdrawn by the end. What is left is smaller and usable, and it is the reason the anxiety you feel before a real decision is not a malfunction. It is accuracy.

The fight has to be fought properly for that to stand. Hegel gets his whole case, and it is stronger than the version his enemies repeat. He did not think the individual was nothing. He thought the individual was the highest achievement of history and that he was the first to explain how it had been achieved. Kierkegaard gets his whole case, and it is stronger than the version his admirers repeat. He had nothing against reason, and he asked no one to believe nonsense on a whim. He was asking one exact question, which is whether an explanation of a life and the living of that life can ever be the same thing. Then the harder questions. Whether Kierkegaard read the man he was fighting or a rumour of him from Copenhagen lecture halls. Where, to the sentence, the two of them actually part. Which of them is right about that, and why the answer has to be earned twice, once against each man's best objection.

The case that decides it is an old man riding three days to a mountain with his son and a knife.4 He has no explanation he can give anyone. Hegel has a place for that man, and it is a courtroom that sits after the verdict of events. Kierkegaard has a place for him, and it is silence. Neither place is where the man was on the ride. Where he was on the ride is where you are on the night before you decide, and that is what this is about.

Part 1: The Truth Is the Whole

The version of Hegel his enemies repeat is the version most people carry, so it goes first. In that version, Hegel is the man who fed every human being into a machine called history and let the machine decide what they were worth. The individual disappears into the whole. That version is wrong, and the fight cannot start until it is gone. Hegel's whole is not the claim that you do not matter. It is the claim that what you are is made of everything you are not. You become an individual at all only by passing through what everyone shares. On his account, the single person is not history's victim. The single person is history's most expensive product.

Hegel begins his most famous book with the smallest thing anyone can claim to know. Something is in front of you right now. Not a type of thing, not a thing with a name, just this, here, now. It feels like the most certain and the most private knowledge there is. Nobody else is standing where you stand. Nobody else has this moment. Whatever is explained about you from outside, this at least is yours.

Now try to say it. The word is "now", and it is night as you say it. If you write the word down and keep it until noon, the word is still true and the night is gone. The "now" you meant, the particular one, has been left behind by the word you used for it. The word "here" does the same. In the next room it is just as true and it points somewhere else. The strongest one is "I". Every person in the world says the same word and means themselves. Hegel's sentence for this is plain. "I is merely universal, like Now, Here, or This in general".5

But watch how naturally it stretches. The "this" in front of you was the last thing you had that was only yours. If even that turns out to be sayable only as something anyone could say, then the private remainder is not a thing you know. It is a thing you mean, and meaning, on Hegel's account, is what knowledge looks like before it has started.

That move now comes off the table in front of you and runs on your life, because that is where it bites. You are a particular person, with a particular job in a particular town, married or not, born in one year and not another. The task is to say what is only yours in any of that. The job has a name, and the name is a category of work that thousands do. The town has a name and a history that were there before you and will be there after. The marriage is a form older than every language you speak. Even your feelings arrive with names on them, and the names were coined by strangers. You can subtract the categories one by one, hunting for the remainder that is not a category. What is left when the last one goes is not a small, precious core. It is nothing you can say, and so, by the sentence above, nothing you know.

With the particulars out, the shape is the same for anyone. Any life is a set of descriptions, and every description is something that could be true of someone else. That is what a description is. Then the general form, which is the first thing you have to hold about Hegel. A thing is what it is through what it is not. Your job is your job because of every job it is not. Your "now" is this now because of every now it is not. That general form is the story of your job with the details removed, and Hegel's word for the process is mediation. A thing is mediated when what it is comes through something else. Nothing, on his account, comes any other way.

This is where the caricature dies. A machine that dissolves individuals would say that the person is an illusion and the categories are real. Hegel says something stranger and more generous. The person is real, and is the hardest thing history ever made. In his lectures on history he ranks whole civilisations by one measure, how many of their people they knew to be free. The ancient East, he says, knew that one was free, the ruler. The Greeks knew that some were free, and kept slaves to prove it.8 Then, in his words, people came "through Christianity, to realize that man as man is free".9 Not this man because of his birth, or that man because of his office. Man as man. The ladder of peoples that carries that sentence is left where it is; the point does not need it. The idea that a single person has infinite worth, just by being one, had to be discovered, and it was discovered late.

Put the two halves together and you have his real position, the one Kierkegaard had to beat. First half: nothing about you is simply yours, because nothing about anything is simply itself. Everything is mediated, made of what it is not. Second half: the single person with infinite worth is real, and is the product of that mediation, the point history was working toward. The whole is what produced you, and it is the only place your worth can be seen.

Now the sentence the whole fight will be about, and the reading taken of it here. "The truth is the whole". Hegel goes on at once to say what he means. "The whole, however, is merely the essential nature reaching its completeness through the process of its own development". In the same paragraph he says it again. "Of the Absolute it must be said that it is essentially a result, that only at the end is it what it is in very truth".11 The sentence can be read more gently, as saying only that nothing means anything on its own. Read with the two that follow it, it says more. The truth of a thing is its place in a completed development, and a thing is what it truly is only at the end. That is the reading Kierkegaard fought, and Hegel's own next sentences support it, which is why the fight was fair on this point before it started. One thing Hegel adds has to be kept beside it, because the fight turns on it later. The bare result, he says, is a corpse; the whole is the result together with the process that arrived at it.12 So the whole holds the becoming too, but it holds it as having become.

Here is what that reading does to a life. If the truth of a thing is what it turns out to be, so is the truth of your life, seen whole. Not what it feels like from inside on a given night. Not what you meant. What it came to. The night you decided something is, on this account, a moment whose truth is its result, and the result is not yet available to you. Hegel is not saying that with contempt. He is saying it the way a man says the weather.

So let the viewer make the objection everyone makes, and let Hegel answer it, because his answer is the strongest thing he has. The objection is: there is a me that all this misses. Not my job, not my town, not my feelings with their borrowed names. Me, the one having them. Hegel's answer is short. Name it, he says, and say one thing about it. Every thing you say will be something that could be said of another person, and every word you use will be a word anyone can use. The "I" you are pointing at is the "I" from the first page, "merely universal". Subtracting every description, hunting for the one who has them, does not arrive at a jewel. You arrive at a blank, and a blank is not a self. It is the absence of one, and Hegel says so without any pleasure in it.

So Hegel has not shown that you are nothing. He has established that whatever you are, you are through what everyone shares. And the last private remainder, when you hunt for it, cannot be said and so cannot be known. Hegel contains the individual by making the individual a result. That is the whole of his position, at full strength, with nothing hidden. The question it leaves is exact, and it is the question the other man built his life on. If everything you can say about yourself is universal, what is left that is not. And is that remainder anything at all, or only the blank Hegel says it is.

Part 2: Lived Forwards

The first blow Kierkegaard lands is not against Hegel's whole. It is against the place Hegel has to stand in order to see it. Hegel himself says philosophy arrives only after the fact, and a whole built after the fact cannot hold an act while it is being done. That is not a charge Kierkegaard invented. Hegel wrote it into the preface of his own book on the state, in the calmest and most famous sentences he ever produced. Kierkegaard's contribution was to take those sentences seriously as a description of where a living person is.

Hegel is explaining what philosophy can and cannot do about the world it describes. "As for the individual, every one is a son of his time; so philosophy also is its time apprehended in thoughts".13 Philosophy, on his account, does not float above history handing down blueprints. It is a time understanding itself, and it can do that only once the time has taken shape. Then the sentence that has been quoted for two centuries. "When philosophy paints its grey in grey, one form of life has become old". The grey is the colour of a thing that has stopped growing. And the image that closes the preface. "The owl of Minerva takes its flight only when the shades of night are gathering".14

Hold that beside the sentence about the whole. The truth of a thing is its place in a completed development. The Absolute is a result, and a thing is what it truly is only at the end. His answer went into a journal in 1843, and it is one sentence long, with a second sentence that most people who quote it leave off. It is quite true what philosophy says, he wrote, that life must be understood backwards. But over that, one forgets the other proposition, that it must be lived forwards. And then the part that is usually dropped, which is the point. The more that proposition is thought through, he says, the more it ends in this: life in time never becomes properly understandable. He gave the charge its formal shape in the biggest book he wrote, published in 1846 under a mask, and the shape is worth having exactly.17 A logical system can be given, he says, but no system of existence can be given. He grants that Hegel's logic hangs together, and draws a line around it. Existence itself is a system, he writes, for God, but it cannot be one for any existing spirit. System and finality correspond to each other, and existence is precisely the opposite.18 The one still existing is not finished, and a system is a finished thing. His other name for the thinker who forgets this is absent-minded. Modern speculation, he says, has in a kind of absent-mindedness forgotten what it is to be a human being. A house, he wrote, can be finished with a bell-pull missing, and it is still a house. A system with its conclusion missing is not a system with one part missing. The serious sentence under the joke, that a system is a finished thing and a life is not, was aimed at Hegel, and it holds.

Kierkegaard's complaint, remember, was that the account never decides for me at the moment of decision. Hegel would say that complaint confuses two different things. Philosophy comes late. But you do not live inside philosophy. You live inside a family, a trade, a town, a law, a set of duties that were there before you woke up this morning. He writes that in an ethical community "it is easy to say what man must do". It is easy to say "what are the duties he has to fulfil in order to be virtuous". A person "has simply to follow the well-known and explicit rules of his own situation".22 That is Hegel speaking to the present, and it is not a small thing. The account does not decide for you, he says, because it never had to. Your situation decides. Its duties are known. The feeling of standing before a decision with no guidance has, on his account, one source. It is the feeling of a person who has stepped outside their own ethical world and is looking at it from nowhere. He thinks that person is in danger, and the danger comes back with a knife in its hand.

So Hegel does have something to say to the person in the middle of the day. He says: you are not in the middle of nowhere. You are in a world that tells you what to do, and most of the time it is right. Anyone who has ever been steadied by a job to go to or a child to feed knows that he is describing something real.

Kierkegaard does not deny it. It is to ask what happens at the one kind of moment the well-known rules are not made for. Leave aside the morning when duty is clear and gets done. Take the morning when the rules of your situation say one thing, and something in you says the rules are not the last word. No rule can tell you whether that something is a revelation or a fever. Hegel's ethical world is built for the first kind of morning. It has nothing to say on the second kind except that you have stepped outside. And the second kind of morning is the only kind anyone ever meant when they said the account does not decide. "It is easy to say what a man must do" is a true sentence about an ordinary day. It is offered as an answer to a question that was asked in fear. A man is about to be put on that second kind of morning, with a knife, and that is where this reply gets tested.

Hegel has one more move, deeper than the others. The middle, he can say, is only the not-yet-understood. Everything that happens in the middle of the day is understood at dusk. Nothing in the deciding escapes. The confusion you felt, the reasons you weighed, the thing you did, the thing it led to. All of it is in the account when the account is written. Your "middle" is not a different kind of place. It is the same place, seen early.

This is the crux. Kierkegaard's counter is that what is understood at dusk is the result. What was in the middle was the deciding. Take the account of a man who left his job. It has his reasons, his fear, his savings, the day, the letter he wrote, and what happened next. Read every line of it looking for the moment he decided. It is on no line, and no line is missing. The deciding is absent from the result because the account is an account. A decision looked back on is a decision already made, by someone who is now somewhere else. A decision being made is nothing looked back on. The two are different things, and never early and late views of one thing. Only one of them is ever in front of the one who has to act.

Hegel's reply is short. Point at the line that is missing. You cannot, because anything you say about the deciding is already in the account. And what you cannot say is, by his own sentence, something barely and simply meant. Kierkegaard's answer is that this is the one place the sentence fails. The deciding was never something the man knew; it was something he did. The unspeakable was untrue when it claimed to be knowledge. It never claimed to be knowledge. It claimed to be you, acting, now.

So the two positions now stand like this. Hegel says, and it is his own sentence, that understanding comes at dusk. Kierkegaard says, and it is his own sentence, that life is lived before dusk, and that there is never a rest from which to look back. Hegel's best reply is that the present is not unguided, because ethical life guides it, and that whatever the present holds is understood later. Kierkegaard's best reply is that the guidance fails on exactly the mornings that matter. What is understood later is the result and never the deciding. The whole fight now sits on one question. Is there anything in a decision that the account leaves out. Or is a decision just a transition the account has not explained yet, one more move in a story that will be told whole at dusk.

Part 3: Either, Or

The fight can now be narrowed to one word, and the word is transition. Everything else the two men disagree about follows from how each of them thinks one state becomes another. Hegel holds that every passage from one state to the next is mediated, which means explained by what came before. A contradiction, for him, is resolved by moving up to a level that holds both sides. Kierkegaard holds that the passages that make a life, choice, guilt, faith, are leaps. And an explained transition, on his account, is always one that has already been made, by someone who is no longer standing where you stand.

Two cases first. The first is a contradiction in thought, the finite and the infinite. Everything you can point at is finite, bounded, with an edge where it stops. The infinite is what has no edge. Set them side by side and they seem to exclude each other completely. Then notice that the infinite, if it is simply outside the finite, has an edge after all, the edge where the finite begins.23 So it is not infinite. The only way to think the infinite is as something that includes the finite instead of standing beside it. The contradiction did not get denied. It got resolved by rising to a way of thinking that holds both. That is Hegel's basic move, and on its own ground it is beautiful.

Now the two cases differ on one axis and the axis has a name. In the first case, the opposition can be held at once, from above, and the holding is the answer. In the second case, the opposition cannot be held. One side has to be taken, in time, by someone. Kierkegaard's word for the first is mediation, borrowed from Hegel. His word for the second is the title of his first great book. Either, or.

He wrote the second half of that book as a set of letters from a married judge to a young man who lives for the interesting. The judge's own case for a choice that will not wait is a young man deciding what to be, priest or artist. The either-or sits between choosing at all and not choosing. Between a life in which good and evil exist because you have taken a side, and a life of watching.

Hegel's move on an opposition has one German word, and the word has three meanings at once, and it needs all three. Kierkegaard's technical objection to that comes in a short, hard book about anxiety, in its introduction. It is aimed at the place where Hegel's logic and Hegel's account of life meet. In logic, he writes, no movement must come to be, "for logic is, and everything logical simply is".29 In a proof, nothing happens. The conclusion was already in the premises, the way the whole film is already on the reel before anyone turns the projector. Hegel's Logic reads as if the reel were turning by itself. Every category passes over into the next, the finite into the infinite, and the passing feels like an event. Kierkegaard's point is that there are no events in logic. So where did Hegel get the movement from. Hegel would answer that his logic is a realm of shadows, and that its transitions are not events in time. Kierkegaard, he would say, hears the word movement as if it meant becoming. Kierkegaard's charge survives that answer in one place only, and it is the place that matters: the shadows still cannot begin without someone deciding to begin. Where the sense of movement came from, he says, is existence, where things really do become other things in time. He carried that into a place where nothing passes. And then the trick ran backwards. Because the logic now seemed to move by itself, by necessity, existence looked as if it moved that way too. One state producing the next, with nobody deciding anything. The whole system's sense of inevitability is borrowed from the thing it claims to explain.

Hegel's reply, in its strongest form, grants nothing. Your choice was mediated too. Hegel will say that "this one, now" is the empty I from the page about saying "now", the word that is merely universal. Kierkegaard's counter carries the rest of the fight. Part 4: Three Days to the Mountain

In 1843 Kierkegaard published a short book about one story, and the book puts the whole fight on a single case. The book's own claim is brutal. If the ethical, the shared law that applies to everyone, is the highest thing there is, then Abraham is a murderer. It says the honour and the murder cannot both be true, and that the reader has to pick.

Part 5: The Unspeakable Is the Untrue

Hegel's best punch has been waiting since the page about the unspeakable, and this is where it lands. It lands clean. A faith that refuses all speech cannot be told from delusion, and cannot be told from evil. Not by the court, not by the family, and not by the believer. And Kierkegaard's own book, the one that put Abraham on the mountain, half concedes the point in its last chapter. The ruling here goes to Hegel, and here is why.

Hegel has three texts for the prosecution, and they have to be given in order, because each is stronger than the last. The first is the sentence about the unspeakable. "What is called unspeakable is nothing else than what is untrue, irrational, something barely and simply meant".60 In his book that sentence is about the bare "this" in front of your eyes, the thing you mean and cannot say. Turning it on a man's faith is an extension, and it has to be owned as one.61 But watch how little extending it needs once the two cases are side by side. Abraham's faith, on Kierkegaard's own account, is exactly a thing meant and not said. He can point at it, and pointing was the whole of what the bare "this" could do. He cannot put a sentence under it that another person could weigh. On Hegel's first page, that is the shape of the untrue.

Part 6: The Slaughter Bench

Hegel has an answer to the question that closed the last part, and it is a real one. His whole does contain the exception, the man who breaks the shared law and is not a criminal. But the terms are everything: it contains him only after the fact, and only if he won. And a vindication that arrives only in the result is one the person acting can never use. The person acting does not have the result. That is the same gap as before, the owl at dusk, but now it has a body count.

Hegel's court is set out in his lectures on world history. The whole thing rests on one conviction, and he states it flat: "Reason is the Sovereign of the World".76 History is not a pile of accidents. It is reason working itself out through peoples and states, toward the discovery that man as man is free. Then he asks the question that every honest reader of history asks, and he asks it in words no one has improved on. Looking at the record, he says, one sees history as a slaughter-bench. On it the happiness of peoples, the wisdom of states and the virtue of individuals have been victimised.77 It matters that this is a question in his text and not a verdict. He raises the carnage in order to ask what it was for. His answer is that it was for the result, and the rest of the lectures is that answer.

Part 7: Two Things Called Truth

Each man has one slogan, and the slogans are the fight in four words each. Hegel: the truth is the whole. Kierkegaard: truth is subjectivity. Each slogan is one man naming his own side with the word truth. The word does not mean the same thing in the two mouths. There are two different things called truth here, one for what is known and one for what is done. Kierkegaard keeps the word for his, knowing the risk. Hegel over-reaches with his. And once the word is taken away from both of them, the fight gets clearer, not weaker.

Hegel's slogan has been on the table since the first part, with the sentences that explain it. "The truth is the whole", and the whole is "the essential nature reaching its completeness through the process of its own development".96 "Of the Absolute it must be said that it is essentially a result".97 On that account, truth is a property of the finished. A thing is true when it has become what it is. The account of it is true when the account holds the whole of that becoming. This is truth as the fit between an account and a completed fact. It is the ordinary sense of the word, made total.

Now stop and hear how strange that is in Hegel's ears, because Hegel is right to find it strange. The second man is wrong. There is no God in the idol. Everything he believes about the object of his prayer is false. If truth is the fit between a mind and a fact, he has none, and the first man, whatever his coldness, has some. An engineer and a husband. The first case is a claim. An engineer says: this bridge will hold. The truth of that sentence is in the bridge. It does not matter how sincerely the engineer says it, or how much of herself she has put into it. If the bridge holds, the sentence was true. If it falls, it was false, and her passion is not a defence. Everything about the truth of the claim lives outside the person who made it.

The second case is a commitment. A man says to a woman: I will stay. Where is the truth of that. It is not in any fact about the world that exists at the moment he says it. Nothing outside him can make it true or false at that moment, because the thing it is about has not happened. The truth of "I will stay" is in the staying. It is made true, over years, by the person who said it, and by nothing else. And if he says it with no inwardness, as a habit, as the thing one says, it is already false. False in a way that has nothing to do with whether he later happens to remain in the house.

The two cases differ in one place. In the first, the truth is in the object, and the person is irrelevant. In the second, the truth is in the person, and the object does not yet exist. The first is truth for what is known. The second is truth for what is done. And that is the claim, said plain. Hegel's slogan is about the first kind, and he is right about it, and he extends it to cover everything. Kierkegaard's slogan is about the second kind, and he is right about it, and he calls it by the first kind's name.

Take the second man at prayer again with that in hand. His inwardness is real. His holding fast is real. It is his, and no account of him from outside contains it, for the reasons the owl and the judge gave. All of that Kierkegaard sees and Hegel's whole cannot hold. But none of it makes the idol God. The man's relation to his life is true in the way a commitment is true. His belief about the world is false in the way a claim is false. Kierkegaard's sentence, that there is more truth in him, is right about the first thing and wrong about the second. It is right that the how is real. It is wrong to call the how truth, because truth already has a job, and the job is the bridge.

Hegel's objection at this point is the courtroom's, and it has to be answered here or the ruling is unfair. A how with no what, he says, is nothing. Passion about nothing in particular is the idol-worship point again, inwardness certifying whatever it happens to contain. Kierkegaard's answer is that the what is specified, and specified exactly. The what is the paradox, the claim that the eternal God became one man, in one place, in time, and that is the next round. The how is what the person adds to a what that cannot be known. So the definition does not say "feel strongly about anything". It says "hold fast, with everything you have, to this one thing that cannot be proven". That answer is good enough to stop Hegel's objection from being fatal. It is not good enough to make the how into a truth about the world, because a what that cannot be known is still not known.

Kierkegaard would have an objection to the ruling. You have made faith a mere attitude, he would say. You have put it in a drawer marked feelings and told the engineer she owns the word truth. That is exactly the demotion the professors performed, faith as a stage, a warmth, something to go further than. The answer is no, and the no has to be exact. The holding fast is real. It is the only thing in a life that the account cannot contain. It is yours in a way nothing known is yours. What it is not is a claim, and so it cannot be true the way a claim is true. That takes nothing from it. A promise is not less than a fact because it is not a fact. Kierkegaard drew this very distinction himself, objective truth against subjective, and called his side the highest truth there is for an existing person. And he moved the word for a reason, not out of stubbornness. Truth as the agreement of thought with being, he argues, needs a being that is finished.103 An existing person's being is a becoming, so the finished agreement holds for God. A living person reaches it only in moments, by abstracting from their own life. What he did not do was give up the word, and the word is what his readers heard. And what readers hear, when the word is kept, is that sincerity makes things so.

And Hegel over-reaches in the mirror image. The truth is the whole is a fine sentence about what is known. Applied to the man saying "I will stay", it makes the truth of his promise what the promise turned out to be, seen from the end. That is a truth about the marriage, not the promise. Hegel has taken the word truth, which belongs to claims, and stretched it over acts, where it does not fit. Kierkegaard took the same word and stretched it the other way. Each man's slogan is his side of the fight wearing the other side's word.

So strip the word out of both, and read the fight bare. Hegel: the account. Everything that can be known about a life, held whole, at dusk. Kierkegaard: the act. The one thing in a life that is done rather than known, held fast, in the middle of the day. Neither of those needs the word truth, and neither is refuted by losing it. The account is complete about what it covers. The act is real and is not covered. That is where the fight has been standing since the owl, and it is clearer with the slogans off.

What is left is the what. Kierkegaard said the how needs a specified object, and named it: the paradox, the eternal in time, God become a man on a particular day. That is the one thing he said cannot be known, only believed or refused. And Hegel said, in as many words, that reason understands exactly that. The incarnation is the truth his whole philosophy grasps in the form of thought. So the next question is not about truth. It is about whether there is anything left to believe once the thing believed has been understood.

Part 8: The Paradox and the Concept

The centre of the fight is one event, and both men agree on which. God became a man, on a particular day, in a particular country, and died. Hegel says that thought grasps that event as the reconciliation of God and humanity, so that faith is a picture of what philosophy knows. Kierkegaard says that a faith which becomes knowledge has stopped being faith. Hegel is not misreading faith here. He is replacing it, and he has an argument for the replacement that most of his critics never answer. Kierkegaard is right that it is a replacement and not a completion. Whether the replacement is a loss turns on the one thing the two prayers established, and it is ruled on here.

Kierkegaard's account first, from a short book of 1844 that he wrote as a thought experiment. Suppose, it says, that the god became a particular man at a particular moment. What would that do to a person trying to understand it. His answer is that it would break the understanding on purpose. He calls the event the absolute paradox, and he says a strange thing about thinking. "The paradox is the source of the thinker's passion, and the thinker without a paradox is like a lover without feeling: a paltry mediocrity".104 Thought, on his account, wants to meet the thing it cannot think. "The highest pitch of every passion is always to will its own downfall", he writes.105 So the supreme passion of reason is "to seek a collision, though this collision must in one way or another prove its undoing".

So the two men are standing over the same event saying opposite things. Kierkegaard: it cannot be understood, and that is its point. The objection, as Kierkegaard would put it, is this. When you change the form, you change the content. A paradox understood is no longer a paradox. Raise a picture of a dying god to the concept of the unity of finite and infinite, and you have a different thing. The offence has been removed, and the offence was the content. So your philosophy does not keep what faith had. It keeps the words and drops the thing.

Hegel's answer, in his own voice from the same lectures, is stronger than his critics allow. It is religion's own demand. A faith that could not survive being thought would be a faith that did not believe its own content was true. Part 9: Did He Read Him?

Every joke about the system that people repeat comes from Copenhagen, and Hegel never lived there. So the fairness question is real, and it has a real answer, in two halves. In the jokes, Kierkegaard fought Danes and not Hegel. In the argument, he fought Hegel, closely and from the books. The facts the argument turns on are few, and only these are given. The skeleton of the argument, spoken once as plain steps, so the bones can be carried away on their own. One. The whole holds what can be said, and what can be said of an act is what it was. Say the particular and you say the universal, and the unspeakable, as knowledge, is nothing.155 Two. A life is lived before it is done. The owl flies at dusk, and there is never a rest from which a living person can look back.156 Three. So the whole holds every life as a result, and no life as lived. Hegel's corpse sentence does not save it: the whole holds the becoming too, but it holds it as having become. That is the fight's reading of his sentence. He would say the whole is the movement itself, and the answer is that a movement seen whole is a movement over.157 Four. That is the price of the instrument, not a hole in it, and Hegel says so himself when he says philosophy comes too late to instruct. An account that could hold the deciding while it was being decided would not be an account.158 It would be the deciding. Five. So the leap does not break the system. It leaves the system, which never had the present in it, and then the system takes back the record. Abraham is honoured by a people, which is a universal. Kierkegaard himself has become a name in the account he refused. Nothing that leaves the whole stays out of it, because the whole is written at dusk, and everyone reaches dusk.159 The record is the one thing the leap never claimed. Six. "Higher than the universal" is a ranking, and on rankings Hegel wins by default, because ranking is his game. The Abraham book has a better phrase for the same thing, an absolute relation to the absolute, and that is not a rung but a place.160 The single person is not higher. The single person is elsewhere, in the present, where no ranking is taken, and elsewhere is the plain word for that relation. Seven. Hegel wins on speech. The one who leaves owes an account, and one who refuses all account cannot be told from a madman, by others or by himself.161 Kierkegaard lost that point only for Abraham; his own judge, and his own authorship, are speech about what cannot be proven.

Take the two overclaims away and what is left is not a compromise. It is a harder position than either man held, because it has both of their wins in it and neither of their comforts.

One more thing needs ruling, and it is the leap. The picture most people carry in is that the leap breaks the system. A person of faith punches a hole in Hegel's whole and stands in the hole. That is not what the texts show. The system is intact. What Kierkegaard attacked was the logic's claim to move by itself, and to begin without anyone deciding to begin. He granted that a logical system can be given, and refuted no transition inside it. What he showed is that the system is a view from dusk, and that the leap happens in the morning, and that the two never meet. A leap does not break a photograph. It happened before the photograph was taken, and the photograph shows where the man came down.

So the answer to the question this began with is this. Yes, the whole can contain one person's existence, as the record of it, entire, at the end. No, it cannot contain the person existing, because that is not a record and cannot become one until it is over. The leap does not break anything. It is the name for what a person does where no record is yet possible. That leaves the question that matters to the person watching, and it is not about either man. If that is the ruling, what does it cost to live by. Hegel's answer has a price and Kierkegaard's answer has a price. The prices are different, and they are counted one at a time.

Part 11: If Hegel Is Right

Suppose the ruling had gone the other way, and Hegel had the whole of it. Suppose the truth of your life really is what it comes to, seen whole, and the present is a moment in that. You would be carried and you would be provisional, both at once. You would belong. The institutions you live inside would be your own freedom made solid. Someone would understand you, afterwards. And your present would be the price of a result you will not see. The comfort is real, and it is the comfort of the finished, and no one living has it.

The comfort comes first, because Hegel's is the more humane of the two answers and it is usually presented as the colder. The preface with the owl in it has a second image, and it is the one he cared about. "To recognise reason as the rose in the cross of the present, and to find delight in it", he writes. That "is a rational insight which implies reconciliation with reality".163 The cross is the present, with its suffering, its unfinished business, its bad mornings. The rose is the reason in it, the sense that the suffering is going somewhere. To see the rose in the cross is to be at peace with the world as it is. Not because it is painless, but because it is going somewhere you can, in principle, understand.

Part 12: If Kierkegaard Is Right

Now the other price. Suppose the ruling stands as given, and the single person is real and is not contained. No one and nothing can then carry your decision. You cannot be understood at the moment that matters most, and your own conviction cannot tell you that you are right. The accurate feeling, standing there, is anxiety. And what is left to you, when the carrying and the understanding and the certainty are all gone, is one thing. The choice is yours.

Kierkegaard has a word for what a person feels at the edge of a real decision, and he chose it carefully. Not fear, because fear has an object, and you are afraid of something in particular. The word is anxiety, and in the short hard book about it he gives the image that has never been improved. Anxiety, he writes, can be compared with dizziness, and he means the ordinary kind. A person whose eye happens to look down into a yawning depth becomes dizzy. And then the sentence: anxiety is the dizziness of freedom, which arises when freedom looks down into its own possibility. The depth is what you could do, and nothing outside you. You look at the fact that nothing is settled, that the next thing is yours to make, and the looking makes you dizzy. Then, he says, freedom faints, and grabs at the finite to hold itself up.174

The whole cannot understand you at the moment that matters, because the whole understands at dusk. The people beside you cannot understand you at that moment, because what you are doing is not yet a thing that can be said. And you cannot certify yourself; the courtroom settled that. What Kierkegaard gives back is only this: it was you. Not the order, not the role, not the result. The holding was done by the one holding, and that cannot be taken away afterwards by any account, however complete.

Hegel has one more thing to say here, and it is the best thing he can say. This is a stage, he can answer, and stages are what I describe. In his first book he described a shape he called the unhappy consciousness. It is "the consciousness of self as a divided nature, a doubled and merely contradictory being". It is the self standing alone before an unchangeable it cannot reach, exactly Kierkegaard's single person before God. And it passes, like every shape in that book. In Hegel's book the unhappy consciousness gives way, a few pages on, to reason.181 Reason, there, is the certainty that the world is not foreign to the mind after all. So Kierkegaard's whole picture, he can say, is a moment I have already described and already moved past. The dizziness is real and it is early.

What has been established, then, is the second price. If Kierkegaard is right, the decision is yours and nothing carries it. Part 13: Between the Palace and the Shed

Both comforts are gone, and something usable is left. Three things, and each was earned somewhere in the fight. Decide in the present, where the whole cannot see, because that is the only place deciding happens. Give the account you can, including the one sentence the mountain does not allow, that you might have been wrong. And expect the whole to judge you by results you do not control, which is not an injustice. It is the shape of being seen at all.

Kierkegaard has a picture for where a person actually lives, and it was saved for here because here is where it belongs. In his last great book he describes a thinker. The thinker "erects an immense building, a system, a system which embraces the whole of existence and world-history". Then, he says, look at the man's personal life, and you discover, to your astonishment, a terrible and ludicrous fact. He does not live in the immense high-vaulted palace. The cost of the second thing is that the account will be judged by the shared law. The shared law is not always kind, and it is not always right.
