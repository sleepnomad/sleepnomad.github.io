Cold open

In the spring of 1924, a writer from Providence, Rhode Island, wrote a long letter to a younger friend. In the middle of it he set down, in one sentence, what he believed about the forces that run the universe. "The gods are neither for nor against us, for they do not know what we want, or even that we exist".1 The writer was Howard Phillips Lovecraft, and that sentence holds his whole philosophy. By gods he did not mean the monsters of his stories. He meant the blind forces of matter and time, and he was saying that nothing out there is on anyone's side.2

Lovecraft is remembered for tentacles, cults and narrators who lose their minds, and those give the usual reading of him. On that reading, the stories are about what happens when a person sees the truth about the universe. The truth is that we are small and nothing cares, and seeing it drives you mad. If that were right, the man who believed it most completely should have lived in despair. He did not. He had dark years, and he said so in his letters. But when he worked the question through, he refused despair as a verdict on the universe, though not always as a mood in his own life. "I am not a pessimist but an indifferentist," he wrote to a friend in 1929.3 Pessimists and optimists, he argued, make the same mistake, because both still picture "a cosmos which gives a damn one way or the other".45

That is the claim everything here rests on, and it is his. The universe is not hostile. It is indifferent, and those are two very different conditions to live under. A hostile universe would at least be paying attention to you. An indifferent one removes the place you were standing when you asked whether you matter. What breaks the people in his stories is the expectation, still alive in them, that they were being counted. Indifference is only a horror to someone who was counting on attention.

From there the argument goes somewhere Lovecraft himself only half went. If nothing out there backs our values, our values turn out to be local. They are real for the creatures who hold them, the way a street is real to the people who grew up on it. They never needed backing from the sky, and they lose nothing when the sky turns out to be empty. Lovecraft's own anchors were exactly that kind of thing: old houses, the hill he grew up on, the customs of the place he came from.

And here the letter of 1924 turns. In it, he also describes a walk through the crowded streets of the Lower East Side of New York. He writes that the people living there "could not by any stretch of the imagination be call'd human".6 He saw more clearly than anyone that the universe gives no one standing above anyone else. In the same letter, with the same pen, he sorted strangers out of humanity.

That letter belongs inside the philosophy, as its hardest test, and it cannot be admired with the hatred wiped off. A value anchored only in the familiar has no defence against fear. In Lovecraft's case, fear of a changing city curdled into contempt for the strangers in it. His philosophy has no law in it that forbids contempt. But followed through, it removes the ground the contempt claimed to stand on, because it denies that nature decides who counts.

He also wrote the way out himself. A later novel is set in a dead city under the Antarctic ice. Its narrator stands over the bodies of alien beings, killed on their way back to their own dead city.7 They have been killed, and they have killed, and he has every reason to see them only as monsters. What he says of them is this: "whatever they had been, they were men".8

What follows is that philosophy whole. What Lovecraft believed, and why the stories turn it into horror. What he thought a person should feel about it, and where his own life shows it breaking. And what is left of it at three in the morning, for someone who has just looked up how big the universe is. They felt, for a moment, that nothing they do counts, and they want an answer that is neither a comfort nor a shrug.

Part 1: Harm Without Hatred

Every story Lovecraft wrote rests on one premise, and he stated it plainly in a letter to his editor in 1927. Human laws, human interests and human feelings have no standing at all in the universe at large. The horror in his fiction comes from that premise, which means it comes from being beneath notice rather than from being hated. His monsters can kill you, and some of them can read your mind. But in the stories that hold to his premise, none of them is against you.

Two kinds of monster make the difference easy to feel before it has a name. The first is the vampire of the old horror novel. He wants something from you, your blood, and often your soul as well. He picks you out, he follows you, he comes to your window at night because you are the one he has chosen. He is frightening, but in his own way he takes you very seriously. And the world he lives in takes sides. A cross drives him back, holy water burns him, and a prayer said with enough faith still has force. The universe of that story contains a war between good and evil, and you are a prize in it.

The second monster is a road crew laying tarmac over a field. Under the field there is an anthill. The crew has nothing against the ants. Nobody in the crew knows the ants are there, and if someone told them, the work would go on. The ants are crushed in the most thorough way possible, and nothing in the event is about them. No cross works against a road crew. There is no prayer the ants could say, because there is nobody listening who has the ants in mind.

Those two cases differ on one thing. In both, something enormous ends a small creature's life. In the first, the enormous thing has the creature in view, wants it and hates it. In the second, the enormous thing has some other business entirely, and the creature is simply in the way. The first is malevolence. The second is indifference. Lovecraft's best-known work is built on the second, and once that is seen, most of what is strange about his stories stops being strange.

Here is the premise in his own words, from that letter of 1927. Lovecraft was explaining to the man who bought his stories what he was trying to do in them. "Now all my tales are based on the fundamental premise," he wrote. The premise is "that common human laws and interests and emotions have no validity or significance in the vast cosmos-at-large".9 Validity means the laws do not hold out there. Significance means the interests and feelings do not register out there, as if they had never happened.

Then he says what this demands of a writer. He wanted his stories to give the feel of a place where human things do not reach, and his name for that was "real externality". To achieve it, he says, a writer has to forget something. He must forget "that such things as organic life, good and evil, love and hate", exist at all. And in the middle of the sentence he gives the reason: they are "local attributes of a negligible and temporary race called mankind".10 That sentence is long, and the important words in it come in pairs. Good and evil. Love and hate. He is not only asking the writer to forget the kind feelings. Hatred goes too, because hatred is just as local as love, a habit of one short-lived species on one planet. A monster that hates you is a human being in a costume, and Lovecraft thought such stories were childish.11

The hardest challenge to this comes from Lovecraft himself. In the same year as the letter, he finished a long essay on the history of horror fiction. In it he defines the true weird tale. It must carry a hint, he writes, of "a malign and particular suspension or defeat of those fixed laws of Nature". Those laws, in his words, are "our only safeguard against the assaults of chaos".19 Malign means meaning harm, so his own definition of the genre puts malice right at its centre. And some of his stories do have beings who plan to clear the earth of us, which has to be conceded, and comes up again later. So it is fair to ask whether the indifference is a philosophy he held or a pose he struck in one letter.

The answer turns on a difference between what a reader feels and what a story finally shows. The feeling of horror always arrives as the feeling of being threatened, because that is how a human body registers danger. A body cannot tell a hunter from a landslide, and the heart beats just as fast for either. So the stories borrow the feeling of malice, and Lovecraft's essay describes that feeling accurately, as a critic of the genre. But when the stories built on his stated premise reach their end, what they show behind the feeling is the landslide. The cultist's gods do not hate the sailors, and nothing in the story says they hate anyone. They use whatever minds are available, including the cult's, and go on with business that no human mind could share.

So the two can live together. The feeling his fiction produces is the feeling of being hunted. The fact his fiction reveals is that nobody is hunting. The letter of 1927 is a statement of the fact, and the essay of the same year is a description of the feeling. They are about two different things, and they do not contradict each other.

There is one more piece of evidence that the premise was a belief, and it is the letter quoted at the very start. In 1924, three years before the letter to his editor, he was writing to a friend, with no story to sell. He wrote that there is "no reason in fact to believe the cosmos and its forces anything but utterly indifferent to all that appears".20 And then the sentence about the gods, who are "neither for nor against us". Those are not the monsters of the stories. Those are the real forces of the real universe, as he understood them, described to a friend in private.

So the claim stands, and Lovecraft's horror is the horror of indifference. His beings may know we are here, may use us, and may kill us, but they do all of it without regard. What they never do is take us seriously. And that raises the question the stories keep pressing. Hatred, at least, is a kind of attention. A hunted animal is important to the hunter. It is still strange that anyone would find being ignored more frightening than being hated, and that strangeness needs explaining.

Part 2: Worse Than Being Hated

A universe that hated us would be easier to live in than one that does not notice us, because hatred takes its target seriously. An enemy confirms that its target counts. Indifference takes away something hatred leaves standing, which is a person's place in the scheme of things. That loss of place is what Lovecraft's stories are about, and it is why being used without regard frightens his characters more than any curse could.

Most people, asked to rank the two, would put the hostile universe at the bottom. It seems obvious. A universe that wants you dead is surely worse than one that merely does not care. It is worth holding that guess for a moment before testing it.

Start with what a hostile universe would give the people in it. First, it would give them a part to play. If something vast is against a person, that person is its opponent, and an opponent is a figure in the story. Second, a war has sides, so there would be a right side to stand on and a reason to stand there. Third, an enemy can be resisted, fooled, bargained with or outlasted. Even losing to it means something, because the loss can be a sacrifice, a test, a martyrdom. Every one of those gives suffering a shape that a person can carry.

There is a smaller version of this that anyone can check against their own life. Someone who believes they are being watched is frightened, but they also believe they are worth watching. Someone who believes the bad year they had was a punishment can at least ask what it was for, and try to change. Someone who believes the bad year was an accident has nothing to ask and nobody to ask it of. Many people, faced with that choice, will take the punishment. It hurts more and it leaves them standing somewhere.

Now compare the guess with what the stories show. In Lovecraft, the moment of real horror is almost never the moment a monster attacks. It is the moment a character understands where he stands. The most famous paragraph he ever wrote, the opening of The Call of Cthulhu, says exactly this, and it says it before any monster has appeared.21

This is worth taking slowly, because it is the engine of the whole body of work. Each science on its own is harmless. At school a person learns that the universe is enormous, and that our sun is one star in a crowd too large to count. In another lesson they learn that the earth is vastly older than our species. In a third, that we came from simpler living things by a process with no goal. A person can learn any one of these at school and go home and eat dinner. The horror arrives when the facts are joined into one picture with yourself inside it. A recent species, on a small planet, at the edge of an ordinary star, produced by no plan, and due to vanish.

So the narrator offers two exits from that picture. We shall "either go mad from the revelation", he writes. Or we shall "flee from the deadly light into the peace and safety of a new dark age".24 Notice what the second exit is. A new dark age means going back to a story in which humans have a place, any story, even a frightening one. The narrator would rather have myths and monsters than the plain position. That is the guess from earlier, turned inside out. Faced with a universe that ignores them, his characters run toward a universe that would at least hate them.

Against all of this stands a plain fact about modern life. The objection says that most educated people today already believe the universe is indifferent. It is in textbooks and on posters, and nobody runs screaming from a science museum. If indifference were so terrible, the knowledge would have broken people long ago. And if it does not break them, Lovecraft's horror looks like a mood dressed up as a discovery.

The answer is already inside the narrator's sentence about correlating. Most people do know the facts, but they hold them in separate rooms. The size of the universe lives in one room and the age of the earth in another. Their own life is in a third, and the doors stay shut. Knowing a fact is different from having it applied to yourself. The stories stage the moment the doors open, when the head's knowledge reaches the gut.

There is a version of that moment many people have had without reading a word of Lovecraft. It is three in the morning and you cannot sleep, so you pick up your phone. You end up looking at one of those pictures where the earth is a single pale dot in a band of light. Nothing in the picture is new to you. You learned all of it years ago. And yet for a moment the room tilts, because the dot is where your job is, and your rent, and the argument you had yesterday. That tilt is correlation. The facts you already had have just been joined, with you inside them.

The objection is right that most people recover by breakfast, but the recovery proves the narrator's point. We recover by closing the doors again. The island of ignorance is made of a habit, the habit of keeping information apart.

There is one more step, and it carries the rest of the argument. What made the picture frightening deserves a second look. It was frightening because it took away a place you thought you had. But a place can only be taken from someone who believed they had one. A creature that never expected the universe to arrange itself around it would look at the same pale dot. It would feel something else entirely, curiosity, or nothing at all. The horror of indifference is the horror of a disappointed expectation. It needs the old belief to still be alive somewhere inside the person who feels it.

A rival explanation sits in the same essay on horror fiction that defined the weird tale. Its first sentence is a theory of fear. "The oldest and strongest emotion of mankind is fear, and the oldest and strongest kind of fear is fear of the unknown".26 He did not mean a belief that a good argument could remove. He meant something lodged in the body and handed down, older than any idea a person holds. In his words, there is "an actual physiological fixation of the old instincts in our nervous tissue".27 And the old associations cling to whatever was once mysterious, he says, "however well they may now be explained".28 If that is right, the creature with no expectation would still feel the dread, and the expectation is doing no work at all.

So the two accounts divide the work. The inherited instinct supplies the shiver, the cold sense that something too large is out there. The expectation is what turns the shiver into a sentence about yourself, the sentence that nothing you do counts. A shiver needs no belief, while that sentence cannot be said without one.

That is why the comparison comes out the way it does. A hostile universe would keep the expectation intact, because it would still have us in view. An indifferent universe disappoints it completely, and that is worse to feel than any enmity. Lovecraft's narrators are people who still carry the old expectation and are made to watch it fail.

Stated plainly, hatred implies attention, and attention implies a place. Indifference removes the place, and the removal is felt as horror only by someone who expected a place. Lovecraft's narrators have the expectation, and his stories are the moment it breaks. Where Lovecraft himself stood is still open. He wrote these people from outside, which suggests he did not share their expectation. But a writer can use a frightening idea without believing it, the way a ghost story writer can use ghosts. His letters can settle whether, for him, the indifferent universe was a fact he believed or a mood he knew how to sell.

Part 3: A Materialist Who Wrote Ghosts

For Lovecraft the indifferent universe was a plain fact, the same kind of fact as the distance to the moon. He was a materialist, which means he believed there is nothing but matter moving by physical law. There is no soul in it, no free will and no purpose anywhere. The monsters were the part he made up. He wrote about the supernatural because he was sure it was false. For the length of a story, he wanted to feel what it would be like if it were not.

Writers of ghost stories come in two kinds. The first believes in ghosts. She attends séances, she thinks the dead can speak, and her stories are reports from a world she takes to be real. The second does not believe in ghosts at all. He writes them because a ghost gives him a feeling he cannot get any other way, a shiver at the edge of what is possible. Read side by side, their stories might look the same. But the first writer is expressing a faith, and the second is using an idea he knows to be empty. Lovecraft is the second writer, with one twist that makes him unusual. Underneath the invented monsters there was a belief he held as firmly as the first writer held hers, and that belief was the indifference.

The evidence is in the letters, written to friends with nothing to sell. In 1926 he described his outlook to a correspondent in a single long sentence. "I am an absolute sceptic and materialist," he wrote. He regarded the universe, he went on, "as a wholly purposeless and essentially temporary incident". It was an incident "in the ceaseless and boundless rearrangements of electrons, atoms, and molecules". And those rearrangements make up "the blind but regular mechanical patterns" of everything there is.30

So the question of belief has its answer. Lovecraft held the indifferent universe as a fact. A man who believes the universe is a purposeless machine, and that he has no free will inside it, seems to have every reason for despair. What remains is what Lovecraft thought follows for how a person should feel, once the universe has no verdict to give.

Part 4: Neither Pessimist Nor Optimist

The obvious conclusion from a purposeless universe is despair, and Lovecraft drew it for years before he took it back. His considered view was that despair makes the same mistake as hope. To say that life is bad, in the grand sense, is to pass a verdict, and a universe with no verdicts cannot back one. The pessimist and the optimist are both still waiting for the universe to give a damn, and they only disagree about which way.

He did not start there. The letter of 1924 that described his boyhood discovery of the stars also named the outlook he had formed. He called it "my present pessimistic cosmic views".39 As a young man he took himself to be a pessimist, and the word fit. He believed the universe was a blind machine, and he concluded that existence was a bad deal. This is the position most people reach when they first take the indifferent universe seriously. It is close to the position of the person at three in the morning.

There is a way to see why this is right that does not depend on Lovecraft at all. It turns on the word meaningless. Nobody calls a rock meaningless. Nobody complains that a cloud has failed to mean anything. The word fits only things that were supposed to mean something and failed, like a garbled sentence, or a ritual nobody remembers the reason for. So to call the universe meaningless, as a complaint, is to treat it as a failed sentence. It assumes the universe was the kind of thing that ought to have been saying something. The indifferentist drops that assumption, and once it is dropped the complaint has nothing left to stand on.

That is the core move, and it can be stated in four steps. First, a verdict on life as a whole, good or bad, needs a standard outside life to measure it by. Fourth, the feeling that existence is meaningless is the leftover of an expectation, and the expectation was the error.

Beside his laugh at the melancholiacs, it reads better as a man adding up his days, the pains against the pleasures, and finding the balance poor. That is a judgment from inside one life, about that life. It needs no standard from the sky. His own pain is real to him and so is his pleasure, and he can weigh them as a man weighs his own groceries. What he refuses is the leap from my days are hard to existence is a mistake.

The serious challenge comes next. This looks like nihilism with better manners. If the universe holds no values, then every value is something we made up. If we made them up, they are false, in the same way a story is false. The indifferentist has not escaped despair, on this view. He has only stopped complaining about it.

The answer is a distinction Lovecraft never drew in these words, and everything that follows depends on it. Two claims are being run together. One is that values are not written into the universe. The other is that values are false. The first does not lead to the second. A pain is not written into the universe either, and it still hurts the one who feels it. A value is a relation between a creature and something it cares about. The relation is real where it happens, the way Lovecraft's pleasure was real "while it lasts". Nihilism says values are false. The indifferent view says values are local. The quarrel, in the end, is over what real means. If real means written into the universe, then no value is real, and no pain is either. If real means present and doing work in the lives of creatures, then values are as real as anything that happens.

So the choice made at three in the morning can be put to rest. The view at three in the morning offered two honest options, despair or a shrug. What is kept from that view is its honesty: it refused a comforting story, and it was right to. What is discarded is the idea that despair is the honest conclusion. Despair is a verdict, and the honesty that rules out comfort rules out despair on exactly the same grounds. And the shrug, which says that nothing matters, goes too far in the other direction. Nothing matters to the universe, and that is a different claim from nothing matters. But one objection survives all of this, and it is the one the feeling at three in the morning actually rests on. It needs no standard from the sky. It is plain arithmetic: a speck of a creature, alive for an instant, in a universe of unthinkable size and age. Before anything is built on local values, that arithmetic has to be tested.

Part 5: Does Size Matter?

The size and age of the universe are not what make a life feel small. They are a picture of something else, the view of oneself from outside. That view can be taken in a small room as easily as under the stars. Make the universe smaller, or make human life last forever, and the feeling at three in the morning would still be available. Size cannot settle whether a life matters, in either direction, and seeing why changes what the feeling is about.

The objection deserves its strongest form first, and Lovecraft wrote it. Forget any standard from the sky. The plain numbers seem to do the damage on their own. On this view, a human life is a few decades long on a planet billions of years old. A human body is a speck on a speck, circling one star among more than anyone can count. Lovecraft said that by his thirteenth birthday he was "thoroughly impressed with man's impermanence and insignificance".49 Impermanence is the time half, and insignificance is the size half.

Part 6: Value Is Local

A value does not need the universe behind it to be real. It needs a creature for whom it is real, and a world close enough to that creature to be cared about. Lovecraft found his own values in the nearest things he had: old houses, inherited manners, the look of the town he grew up in. But he wavered all his life between calling those values real and calling them a pretence. The ruling here is that they are real, local and chosen, and that the word pretence was his mistake.

His clearest picture of the problem is a story called The Silver Key, published in 1929.79 It opens with one sentence that sets everything going. The story follows him as he tries, one after another, the things the modern world offers in its place.81

Pretend is the wrong word, and Lovecraft's own better argument shows why. Recall the indifferentist letter of 1929. There he said that no verdict from outside can make life bad, because outside there are no verdicts. Apply the same logic here. To call a value fake is also a verdict from outside. It measures the value against a standard of what is really real, out there, and finds it wanting. But the indifferent universe supplies that standard no more than it supplies the other. A sharper worry remains. If values are just what a person grew up with, then they are habits. A habit is not a value. A man who loves his street only because he was raised on it has no more chosen it than he chose his height. Calling that a value flatters a reflex.

The answer is that a habit becomes a value when it survives being seen for what it is. The man who knows his street is one street among millions, loved for no reason the universe recognises, and who loves it anyway, has chosen it. He has looked at the fact that his love is local and kept the love. That is the difference between a reflex and a value, and it is a difference only a person who sees the indifference can make. Surviving the question is not enough on its own, since a fear can survive being seen too. What makes the kept love a value is that it survives the question and claims no backing it lacks.

Lovecraft's own philosophy raises a problem with the word chosen. He denied free will. On his view, the man on the street was caused to love it. He was caused, too, to go on loving it after he learned it was one street among millions. Nothing in that history escaped its causes. So the line between a reflex and a value starts to look like one more cause, dressed up as a decision.

The line never depended on escaping causes. Take two sisters who grew up on the same street and love it. One has never asked whether her love is backed by anything. The other has asked, has seen that it is not, and loves the street still. Both histories are caused from end to end, and they are still different histories with different futures. The first love has not yet met the night when the question arrives, and it may not survive that night. The second has already met the question and lasted. Chosen, in this account, means kept after being seen, and someone who believes every event is caused can say that without contradiction. Part 7: Why Go On

The darkest form of the thought that nothing a person does counts is the question of why go on at all. Lovecraft asked it in his own voice. The indifferent universe gives no reason to die, for the same reason it gives no reason to despair, and it gives no reason to live either. What helped keep Lovecraft alive was local and particular, a handful of things he cared about. So the question has an answer, in four steps. First, the indifferent universe gives no reason to die, because it backs no single measure that could be laid on every life. Second, a verdict on one's own life, reached in a bad season, judges a future it cannot see. Third, what actually holds a life is local and particular. Fourth, because those reasons are local they can be lost, so keeping them, and adding to them, is the real work.

That last step matters most for anyone for whom this question is not abstract tonight. As a boy Lovecraft did not argue his way out. He was held, partly, by things he still wanted to know. For someone in that place now, the next step is not a better argument. It is another person, told the truth, tonight.

A life is not kept going by a positive balance, or by permission from the sky. It is kept going by things that are real where they live. Lovecraft's narrators meet something harder than a lost house. They meet a thing that fits none of the categories they sort the world with. The next question is why that, and not the size of the universe, is what breaks them.

Part 8: Why the Narrators Go Mad

The madness in Lovecraft's stories is a frame breaking, and the universe does nothing to the mind directly. Each of his narrators carries a frame, the set of human categories they assume can sort anything that exists. When something arrives that fits none of them, the frame gives way, and what gives way with it is the narrator's sanity. They go mad because they took a local frame for the measure of everything.

A biologist on two different expeditions shows what that means. In the first, a biologist on an expedition finds an animal nobody has ever seen. It has an odd shape and a strange way of feeding, and it is genuinely new. But it is an animal. It has a body, it eats, it breeds, and it can be measured, drawn and given a Latin name. The discovery is exciting, and it adds one more entry to a filing system that was built to receive it.

In the second, the biologist finds something for which there is no file at all. It is not clear whether it is alive. Its colour is not on any chart, its weight changes, and the instruments give answers that contradict each other. This discovery does not add an entry. It shows that the filing system itself was smaller than the world. The first discovery enlarges what is known. The second breaks the frame that knowing depended on. Lovecraft's horror is always the second kind.

His purest example is a story published in 1927, The Colour Out of Space.112 A meteorite falls on a farm in the hills of New England. Scientists from the local college come to examine it.113 The stone shrinks when it should not, stays hot when it should cool, and reacts to nothing in their laboratory.114 The story records their verdict. "At the end of the tests the college scientists were forced to own that they could not place it".115 They are good scientists, and their methods are sound. What fails is the assumption that every object in the universe will fit somewhere in their system.

That charge deserves a ruling and not an excuse, and the ruling splits it in two. There are two kinds of vagueness in Lovecraft, and they need to be told apart. The first is lazy. A monster is called unnamable because the writer has not done the work of imagining it, and the adjective stands in for the work. Lovecraft does this often, and the critic is right about it. Pages of his fiction are overwritten, and no philosophy redeems them.

The second kind is structural. In The Colour Out of Space, the object is defined as something no human word fits. A precise description of it would be a contradiction, because a precise description would mean the words fit after all. Here the failure of description is the subject. The prose has to fail, in a controlled way, because the claim being dramatised is that our categories are local. So the critic's charge holds for much of the work, and fails for the work that matters most to the philosophy.

Now go back to the narrators, because the colour shows exactly what breaks them. Every one of them starts out sure that the human frame is the measure of what can exist. That certainty is the island from The Call of Cthulhu. It is a placid place where every object has its file. When the colour arrives, or the city under the sea, or the alien builders under the ice, the certainty fails. And because they had built their whole sense of reality on it, reality seems to fail with it.

This is where the earlier argument about expectation returns, in a sharper form. The narrators did not just expect the universe to care about them. They expected it to fit them, to stay inside the categories a human mind can hold. That expectation is the same mistake Lovecraft's letter of 1929 caught in the pessimist. It treats something local, the human way of sorting the world, as if it were written into the universe. When the universe turns out larger, the local frame still works for local use. The mistake was thinking it was the only frame there could be.

Lovecraft himself did not go mad, and the reason is now clear. He had looked on man "as if from another planet" since boyhood.124 He knew his categories were local before any story tested them. So he could write the frame breaking from outside, with something close to calm, while his characters broke inside it. What separated the writer from his narrators was that he had stopped expecting the frame to be universal.

In short, the narrators hold a local frame and take it for the measure of everything. Something arrives that obeys laws outside the frame. The frame fails, and because they had mistaken it for reality, they experience the failure as the end of reality. A person who knew the frame was local would experience the same event as a discovery, terrible perhaps, but survivable. Readers pose a puzzle of their own. If the expectation is the error, and Lovecraft spelled out the error, the people who loved his stories should have let it go. What they did instead shows how strong the expectation is.

Part 9: Everyone Puts the Meaning Back

Within a few years of Lovecraft's death, his indifferent universe had been turned back into a war between good and evil. The people who did it were the ones who loved his work most. That reversal is one clear sign of how hard indifference is to hold. Even readers who had chosen these stories, and the friend who devoted years to keeping them in print, could not leave the universe empty. They put a moral story back into it, and they did it almost at once.

Part 10: They Were Men

Seen from far enough out, the indifferent universe can teach kinship. Once no creature is the centre, every mind that builds, struggles and is ground down by time is the same kind of thing. Seen that way, the differences between them shrink. Lovecraft wrote a version of this himself, once, near the end of a short novel set in Antarctica. It is the right response to everything argued so far, and it came from the same pen as the horror.

The natural worry runs the other way. If the far view takes away humanity's special place, it seems it should make every life look worthless, ours and everyone else's. Distance, on that worry, is the great flattener, and what it flattens is value. The novel puts that worry to a direct test.

A street was beautiful to him because it matched the streets of his childhood. A hundred dialects match nothing. The mechanism shows clearly in two men whose street changes. The first man loves his street, but it is not all he has. He also has a faith, or a belief that people everywhere share something, or a sense that his life is part of a larger story. Part 13: Two Kinds of Small

The same fact, that a person is small, can be felt in two opposite ways. Felt as a threat, smallness means insignificance, and it sends people back to clutch whatever is theirs. Felt as awe, smallness is a view, and it leaves the person smaller without making them feel they count for nothing. On this argument's account, which way it goes depends on what the person expected before the vastness arrived. Lovecraft went both ways at different times, and the difference between them is the practical core of his philosophy.

The common assumption is that feeling small is simply bad for a person. Shrink someone's sense of their own importance, the guess runs, and they will become anxious, defensive, maybe more selfish, holding tighter to what they have. Lovecraft's letters about New York fit that guess exactly. Psychologists have tested something close to that assumption.

In 2015 a group of researchers in California ran a simple study. Some people spent one minute looking up into a grove of very tall eucalyptus trees. Others spent the same minute looking up at the face of a tall building nearby. Then a researcher walked past and stumbled, dropping a handful of pens. The people who had looked at the trees picked up slightly more of the pens.198 In other studies in the same paper, people made to feel awe also reported feeling smaller. That letter can sound like the pretence problem again, feeling one thing while believing another. It is different, and the difference is worth making exact. The pagan gods in the woods were a game, and he knew it. The awe, unlike the gods, was no game. The space was really vast, and the suns were really ordered. His breathlessness was a true response to a true thing. Awe needs no false belief. It is what the indifferent universe looks like to someone who stopped expecting it to be about them.

This is where the earlier argument about expectation does its practical work, beyond anything the studies tested. What decides which kind of small a person feels is what they expected before. Someone who expected to be central hears the vastness as a demotion. The universe has taken away their place, and the feeling is insignificance, and from insignificance comes the grip on whatever is left. Someone who never expected to be central, or who has let the expectation go, meets the same vastness as a view. Nothing was taken from them, so what remains is the size and the strangeness, and those can be wonderful.

Put together, vastness makes people feel small, and that much has been measured. Felt as a lost place, the smallness is insignificance, and in Lovecraft's case it went with the clutch at the familiar. Felt with no place lost, it is awe, a smaller self that still counts. What separates them, on this account, is the old expectation, and the expectation can be dropped. Whether awe then makes a person kinder is not settled, and nothing here depends on it.

Lovecraft's own phrase for the good kind of small is in the essay on why he wrote. He said his stories came from "impressions of wonder, beauty, and adventurous expectancy".208 Adventurous expectancy is the right name for it. It is the feeling of standing at the edge of something much bigger than oneself and wanting to see more. The frightened narrator wants the doors shut again, and this feeling wants them open. One plain question is left. The person at three in the morning, staring at the pale dot on their phone, has felt the first kind of small. Part 14: After the Frame Breaks

The universe was never in a position to give a life meaning or to take it away, and so smallness is not a verdict on anyone. Value is local and real, held by creatures in their own corner of things, and it needs nothing from the sky. The danger is clutching that local value in fear, as Lovecraft did, until it turns against strangers. The cure is the view from further out, his own, where the stranger turns out to be the same kind of small as oneself.

Before that can be used, the whole argument should be laid out in plain steps, once, as it has been built. First, the universe Lovecraft believed in is indifferent: not hostile, not kind, simply without any view of us. Second, his horror is the feeling of that indifference arriving in a person who still expected to matter, or at least to fit. Third, despair and hope make the same mistake, because both picture a universe that gives a damn one way or the other. Fourth, values are therefore not false for lacking backing from the universe; they are local, real where they live, and chosen when kept with open eyes. Fifth, the same universe gives no reason to die and none to live. What holds a life is local and particular, and because it can be lost, keeping it and adding to it is the work.

Sixth, a value anchored only in the familiar, with every larger anchor gone, has no defence against fear, and it can curdle into contempt for strangers. Seventh, the verdict that some people count for less contradicts the philosophy, because it needs nature to hand out standing, and nature hands out none. Eighth, smallness can be felt as a lost place, which breeds the clutch, or as a view, which is awe. Ninth, the view from far enough out makes minds kin, because all of them are equally small in a universe that grants none of them standing.

Now go back to the person at three in the morning, with the pale dot on the phone. The room tilted. Something in them said that nothing they do counts. The philosophy has a precise answer to that feeling.

It starts with naming what happened. The tilt felt like a discovery, the discovery that they are worthless. It was really a disappointment. An old expectation, that the universe had them in view, was joined to facts they already knew and was found wanting. The facts are true. The worthlessness was never in the facts. It was in the expectation, and the expectation was the error, the same one the pessimist and the optimist share. The step back will come again, and on that Nagel was right.209 What can go for good is the verdict.

Then comes the warning, and Lovecraft's life is the reason for it. When the size of things frightens a person, there is a strong pull to hold tighter to what is theirs. With it comes a pull to see whatever is changing as an enemy. The new people on the street. The ones who talk differently. The pull feels like loyalty, and from inside it can feel like the only sane response to a universe with no ground in it. It is the clutch, and the clutch is the failure. It takes a local love and dresses it up as nature's verdict on who counts. That verdict is the one thing an honest look at the universe rules out.

And the cure starts from Lovecraft's own sentence, taken further than he took it. It is to take the view from further out, deliberately. From far enough away, the stranger on the street is a creature in exactly the same condition. They are small, temporary, attached to their own corner, and facing the same blind machine. Their street is as right to them as yours is to you. Their love is as local and as real. Seen from there, they are another small mind, doing what you are doing.
