Cold open

When you stop believing in the god you were raised with, the judging does not stop. Something in you goes on deciding what matters, what is forgiven, what is owed. It used to send those verdicts upward. With nothing left above you, it sends them to you. At first that feels like power. You are the one who decides now, the one who makes the rules, the one nobody can correct. Then it feels like standing alone on a roof.

One man wrote the record of that weight landing on him. He said the book was dictated to him by something he did not control.1 The book is Thus Spoke Zarathustra. A few years after finishing it, Friedrich Nietzsche collapsed in the street in Turin.2 Within days he was writing letters to his friends signed as a god.3 Forty-five years after that, Carl Jung sat down with a room of analysts in Zurich and opened the book.4 They read it aloud, a paragraph at a time, most Wednesday mornings, for five years.5 He read it the way a doctor reads a chart. He was looking for the exact page where the weight landed, and the exact page where the man began to give under it.

Jung's verdict was not that Nietzsche had seen nothing. It was worse than that. Jung thought Nietzsche had seen something true about what a person is, and had nowhere to put it except himself. The disease that ended him was in his brain, and that is not in dispute.6 What the book records is not the disease. It is the shape the man had already taken before the disease found him. An idea larger than you needs a home that is not you. Nietzsche had none. Jung, when the same thing came for him, built one, and it did not stop him being lifted off his feet. It did not keep his name off the shameful pages he signed in the 1930s. It kept him from signing as a god.

Part 1: The Reader Who Was Afraid of the Book

Jung was afraid of Thus Spoke Zarathustra before he had read a page of it. The ideas did not frighten him. What frightened him was that the book would show him what he was. That fear is the beginning of the whole reading, decades before the seminar. It is the first thing to get right, because it tells you what kind of question Jung was asking. His first question was not whether Nietzsche was correct. It was what it does to a person to carry what Nietzsche carried. And he asked it first about himself.

The scene is Basel, and Jung is a medical student. Nietzsche had taught at the same university and had left it.7 He had written his books in rented rooms across Italy and Switzerland, and had collapsed in Turin in January 1889.8 By the time Jung was a student, Nietzsche was alive but gone. And Basel still had people who had known him. Jung writes in his memoir that Nietzsche "had been on my program for some time". He put off reading him, he says, because he "felt I was insufficiently prepared".9 That is the polite reason, and the real one follows a page later. There were, he says, "persons at the university who had known Nietzsche personally". They "were able to retail all sorts of unflattering tidbits about him".10 A strange man, one who did not fit. And Jung already knew something about himself that he had told nobody. So he writes this: "I feared I might be forced to recognize that I too was another such strange bird".11

What did he know about himself?. Since boyhood, Jung says, he had felt that he was two people. He gave them numbers. Number one was the schoolboy, ambitious, ordinary, keen to get on. Number two was older than him, and did not belong to the present at all. It was close to the night and to dreams. It had a face: an old man from the eighteenth century, in buckled shoes and a white wig. He did not think number two was an illness. But he had never seen it written down by anyone else. So when he finally opened Zarathustra, what he found there was not a philosophy. He found number two, loose on the page, speaking in the first person, with nobody holding the leash.

Here is what he wrote about it afterwards, and both sentences matter. First: "And Zarathustra, there could be no doubt about that, was morbid".12 Then, a line later, he asks whether his own number two "was also morbid".13 Read those as a stranger would. The first is a verdict on a book. The second is the reason the verdict frightened him. If the thing he had carried since childhood was the thing Nietzsche carried, and Nietzsche's was sick, then his might be too. He gives the difference he clung to. Nietzsche had discovered his number two "only late in life". Jung, by contrast, "had known mine ever since boyhood".14 And the fault he saw in Nietzsche, in his own words, was that he "fearlessly and unsuspectingly" let his number two "loose upon a world".15

Fearless sounds like praise, and it is not, because the word that carries the weight is "unsuspectingly". Nietzsche, on Jung's reading, did not know what he was letting out. He knew something was speaking through him; he said so. What he did not suspect was what it was, or what it would cost to publish it under his own name. Jung closed the book. He says so: "Just as Faust had opened a door for me, Zarathustra slammed one shut". It stayed shut, he adds, for a long time.16 He did not stop thinking about it. As a doctor, within a few years, he was comparing its sentences with a ghost story. As a reader he kept the door shut.

So what kind of reading is this?. Not a philosophy seminar, because the people in that room were not there to decide whether the will to power is a good theory. They were there because they treated people, or were being treated. And to that room the book was a record of a certain thing happening to a certain kind of person. The editor of the published text puts the frame in his own voice, not Jung's. "For all his greatness and importance, Nietzsche's was a pathological personality".23 That sentence is the editor's, and no claim rests on it. What matters is smaller, and it is Jung's own practice. He read the book as a record of something happening to the man who wrote it, in the order it happened. And he read it with people whose job was to notice such things.

The obvious objection arrives here. Most people who love Nietzsche make it. Reading a philosopher as a patient is the cheapest way there is to avoid his argument. You do not have to answer a man if you can diagnose him. Every idea that ever frightened anyone has been called a symptom by somebody. And the person who calls it a symptom always gets to keep their own comfort. If Nietzsche says God is dead and you say "poor man, that was the illness talking", you have not answered him. You have changed the subject to his health.

That objection holds against one kind of reader and not another. It holds against anyone who uses the diagnosis to settle the argument. It fails against someone who keeps the two questions apart. Whether God is dead is one question. What happens to the person who says so, and then has to live in the space where God was, is another. The first is a question for philosophers, and the second is a question about load, the kind an engineer asks. A bridge can be correctly designed and still collapse under the wrong truck. Saying "the truck was too heavy" is not a comment on the design. Jung's reading, as it is used here, is a claim about the truck. It says: this idea has a weight, the weight went onto one man, and the book shows what that did. Whether the idea is true is not settled by that. It is not even touched by it.

And there is a second thing the objection misses. Jung did not begin by diagnosing Nietzsche. He began by being afraid that Nietzsche's condition was his own. A diagnosis you are afraid of sharing is the opposite of a way to avoid an argument. It is a man reading a book to find out whether he will end the same way. The same question gets asked of Nietzsche and of Jung. The answer comes out differently for each, and the difference is the point.

Jung feared the book because he recognised in it his own number two, the older one, running loose with no one holding it. Decades later he read that book aloud for five years with people trained to watch for exactly that. The record is a secretary's notes, taken as such. The question Jung asked first was not whether Nietzsche was right. It was what carrying the idea does to the carrier. The book carries its own account of how it was written, and Jung did not have to find the loose thing in it. Nietzsche told him where it was.

Part 2: One Does Not Ask Who Gives

The book was written by a process Nietzsche did not control, and he said so himself, in print, in the plainest words he had. That was never Jung's discovery. Jung's contribution was to take Nietzsche at his word, and then to ask what it means when a man takes dictation and signs his own name.

Start with two people, because the difference between them is the whole matter. The first sits at a desk with a problem. She turns it over, tries a sentence, crosses it out, tries another. By evening she has a paragraph she can defend, and she knows why each word is there, because she put it there. If you asked her where the paragraph came from, she would say: from me, slowly. The second sits at the same desk, and something arrives. It comes whole, with a shape and a rhythm already, and a voice that is not quite the one he speaks with. He writes as fast as he can, and he does not revise, because there is nothing to revise against. If you asked him where it came from, he would hesitate, and then he would say: I do not know. It was given.

Both of them wrote something, and only one of them can say she wrote it. That is the difference, and it is not a difference of quality. The second kind of writing is often better. It is a difference of ownership, and ownership turns out to matter enormously. Try to argue with your own opinion and you will see why. You cannot, because there is nobody on the other side of the table. You can only argue with something that is not you.

Nietzsche described the second kind, and said it was his. Near the end of his working life, in the book where he looked back over everything he had written, he came to Zarathustra. He first set down where the central thought had come to him. He is walking by a lake in the Swiss mountains, in August 1881. He stopped, he says, beside a huge rock piled up like a pyramid.24 "There this thought came to me".25 The thought was the eternal return, and it will matter again. He wrote it on a sheet of paper and signed the sheet, not with his name, but with a height. "Six thousand feet beyond man and time".26 Notice what the signature does. It gives the thought an address that is not a person. It came from a height, and the man on the path was where it landed.

Put those two accounts side by side. The first writer would never say "one does not ask who gives", because she knows who gave. She did. The second writer says exactly that, and he says it as a boast. He is proud that nothing in him hesitated. He is proud that he had no choice. That is the confession, and it is a confession made in the register of triumph. The man is telling you that the book came from somewhere he could not see, and that he took it, and that he did not ask.

Then the room in Zurich, and what Jung did with this. He did not paraphrase it; he had it read out. Near the start of the seminar he asked, "Would it not be worthwhile to read that description of his inspiration?".31 Then, to the room: "he describes it so wonderfully".32 And the passage was read, in English, to twenty-five people whose job was listening to how people describe what happens inside them. The point of reading it aloud was evidence, not admiration. Jung wanted the room to hear a man describe, unprompted, the arrival of something that was not the man. In the consulting room that description has a name. The patient says: it was not me, it came, I had no choice. Sometimes that is the truth about a poem. Sometimes it is the first sentence of a case. Jung's whole reading turns on the claim that in Nietzsche it was both.

Jung's name for the phenomenon was cryptomnesia, hidden memory: a memory that comes back without its label. Not a lie, not plagiarism, not a reference. A thing you once read arrives as if you had just thought of it. The part of you that filed it never told the part of you that writes. That is the proof the inspiration passage could not supply on its own. It shows the mechanism. The writer says: one does not ask who gives. And here, in one small scene, we can answer him. The objection is obvious: so what?. Every writer is fed by everything they ever read. Half of literature is buried memory surfacing without its label. A sailor's tale from a ghost book turning up in a poem proves that Nietzsche had a memory, and nothing worse. Inspiration is not a symptom. If it were, the whole of poetry would be a ward.

That objection is right, and it aims at the wrong thing. The danger Jung is tracking is not inspiration. A writer who takes dictation and knows it is dictation is in no danger at all. The danger is in what the writer does with the thing that arrives. So go back to the second writer at the desk, to whom something came, whole, in a voice not quite his own. There are two things he can do next. He can treat it as a visitor, something other, something to be questioned, and put his own name only on the questions. Or he can treat it as himself, at last, the real him, finally speaking, and put his name on all of it. The rabbit island shows that things were arriving in Nietzsche from below, in detail, unlabelled. The inspiration passage shows what he did with them. He did not ask who gave. He took. He signed the sheet with a height, and then he signed the book with his name.

What is established is narrow. The book came from somewhere Nietzsche could not see. He said so, proudly, and one small scene in it lets us check that he was telling the truth. Jung said Nietzsche felt himself a mere instrument of what came.43 What he did not do was keep its words apart from his own. He never asked it, on the page, who it was, and he put his name on all of it. A man can be fed from below and be fine. What made Nietzsche's case dangerous was the size of what arrived, and where that size had come from. When a man has just announced that God is dead, where does the force that used to be God's go?. Part 5: A Man With No Earth

The book preaches a life lived in the body, and the man who wrote it had almost none of that life. That gap, and not any idea in the book, is where Jung locates the break. The gap is real. By a rule drawn from the book itself, a gap like that is fatal. And Jung's way of putting it is one of the two places his reading goes wrong.

Begin with what the man's own body was, as he described it. The same late book that described the inspiration opens with his health. The loneliness in the book is the structural cost of a mind with no second party, nothing outside it that can say no. Be careful about what kind of loneliness this is, because the ordinary kind is not it. A man who has no friends can make one. This man cannot, and the reason is in the song's own lines. Every person who approaches him is lit by him. They come as pupils, admirers, readers, people warmed by the light. They cannot warm him back, because what they have to give is what he gave them. That is the loneliness of the height, and it follows from the landing. Whoever the god's weight has landed on is above everyone by definition. Above, there is nobody.

Now the transcript, and what Jung made of the song. He read it late in the seminar, and his reading is careful. He did not call it the confession of the whole book. He said something more exact. In the Night Song, the transcript records, Nietzsche "realizes the nature of the spirit profoundly".107 "He is still identical with it, but to such an extent that he begins to become aware of the inhuman or superhuman nature of the spirit".108 And then: "he feels his own reaction against it".109 The spirit, here, is the voice from the height, the thing that has been speaking through him all along. The small lit part has taken itself for the whole, and has been speaking from the god's height for a hundred pages. And here, for one chapter, the man inside notices what the height costs. He is still up there. He has not come down, and Jung says so, "still identical with it". But he has felt, for the first time, that the thing he has become is not a human thing, and that he does not like it. Jung called the run of chapters that begins here "Nietzsche's approach to himself",110 and, in his own vocabulary, "a sort of descent to his inferior function".111 In plain words: a descent to the side of himself he had never developed. Part 10: The Same Experiment With a Rope

Jung's whole method is Nietzsche's experience with one change. When the figures came for him, he talked to them instead of becoming them, and he wrote down what they said as theirs, not his. That says nothing about Jung being stronger, or saner, or wiser. It is a claim about a single procedure.

Remember the second writer at the desk, the one to whom something came, whole. Both men were that writer. The difference is what each did next. Nietzsche wrote "Zarathustra says", put the voice in the first person, and signed it: I did, and I had no choice. Jung wrote down what the voice said, then wrote his own reply, and the reply argued. He asked the figure what it meant, told it when he did not believe it, and kept the two apart on the page.

The material is the same. The voice is the same kind of voice, and there is no evidence that Jung's was smaller or safer than Nietzsche's. The difference is that in the second case there are two parties in the room, and the small lit part is one of them. It does not become the voice. It does not silence the voice either. It stays where it is, at its own size, and talks. Jung gave the procedure a name in the memoir, and it is plain enough to keep. "The essential thing is to differentiate oneself from these unconscious contents by personifying them, and at the same time to bring them into relationship with consciousness".236 Personify: treat the thing that came as someone, not as you. Relate: do not walk away from it either. Argue with it, at your own size. The ego stays in the room as one party among others, instead of becoming the whole. The red book shows it happening, and shows the man losing. Early in it, two figures, an old prophet and a young woman, tell Jung who they are, and he tries to talk them down. "You are symbols", he tells them, "I am simply too confused to see through you now".237 The old man answers him. "You may call us symbols for the same reason that you can also call your fellow men symbols, if you wish to".238 "But we are just as real as your fellow men".239 "You invalidate nothing and solve nothing by calling us symbols".240 Jung wrote that down in the figure's voice, as the figure's, with his own confusion beside it. That is the whole procedure on one page: two parties, and the man at his own size, being answered back by something he did not make. Part 11: The Roof

You may keep the death of the god. You must give up becoming the superman. The judging does not stop when you stop believing in the one who used to do it. It only changes address. And the only safe address for an idea bigger than you is somewhere that can say no to you.

Go back to the roof. The person standing on it stopped believing what they were raised on. They understood that they were supposed to make their own values now. And they found that nobody was allowed to tell them they were wrong. That was the problem, and they were right that it was. What they did not have was an account of why it happens. Here it is, as steps. One. The judging, the deciding what matters, the forgiving, do not stop when the god goes, because they are what a mind does. Two. With no image above to send those verdicts to, they land on the only address left, which is you. From inside, that does not feel like being judged. It feels like being the judge. It feels like height. Three. A person swollen to that size cannot be corrected, because everything they can see, they have lit. Nothing around it has its own light, so nothing can tell it no. Four. Sooner or later a thought arrives that is too large for one person, and a person who cannot be corrected cannot survive contact with it. It either goes down, or it becomes the thought and signs its name. Five, and this is the only step that is advice. Growing to the size of the idea is the road to the signature in Turin. The work is to build, outside yourself, the thing that can hold the idea, and the test of that thing is whether it can refuse you.

The person on the roof will object here, because they have heard where this is going. You are telling me to get a god back. Something above me, something that can refuse me, something that holds what I cannot hold. That is a church with the paint scraped off. I left, and I left for reasons, and the reasons have not changed.

Answer it with Jung's own words, because he faced it from the floor of his own seminar. Jung had a word for the thing that holds a weight too big for a person, and the word was symbol. A symbol, in his sense, is not an emblem somebody designed. His rule for it: "A symbol is never an invention. It happens to man".258 And then the sentence the objection is entitled to hear, said to the room before anyone objected. "He wiped out his symbol when he declared that God was dead". "God is such a symbol".259 A member of the room refused that. "It is somewhat difficult to think that Nietzsche had no symbol", she said, "I think he had two symbols, two creeds". "He believed in the superman and the idea of the eternal return".260 So the question was put to Jung directly. Is the superman not exactly the kind of thing being asked for, something above the man that holds the weight?. Jung's answer draws the line in the right place. It did not hold, he said, "because it was only what his mind did: his mind invented those ideas".261 "So I would say the superman is an invention, not a symbol".262 He never conceded her point, and it stays in the record beside his. There it is. Asked what Nietzsche lacked, Jung said: God, or something that does what God did. So the person on the roof is right that Jung, by his own account, would hand them a god back.
