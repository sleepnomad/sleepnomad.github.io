Cold open

A poor student in Saint Petersburg builds an argument for killing an old woman, and nobody in the novel ever talks him out of it. The case is short. She is a pawnbroker who squeezes the poor, she is sick and spiteful, and when she dies her money goes to a monastery1.2 Kill her, take the money, and with it a hundred ruined lives can be rescued.3 The man who first says it aloud, in a tavern, calls it "simple arithmetic".4 Dostoevsky's Crime and Punishment takes that arithmetic completely seriously, and then destroys it. It does not do it with a better argument. It does it by following the one man who acts on the argument, and showing what acting on it makes of him.

That argument has a modern form, and many careful people hold it without ever saying it out loud. It runs like this. Morality should be reasoned, not inherited. What counts is what an act does, and one harm that prevents a hundred is worth doing. The disgust you feel at the thought is upbringing, not evidence. History honours plenty of people who broke the rules of their day. So if you have really thought a thing through, and you cannot find the flaw, then refusing to act is not virtue. It is nerve you do not have.

The novel's answer is that this belief fails, and that it fails in the doing rather than on paper. The sum of one life for a hundred becomes two lives within minutes.5 The second is a gentle, beaten woman of exactly the kind the plan claimed it would help.6 The money is buried under a stone and never even counted.7 Asked why he did it, the killer gives one reason after another and takes most of them back.8 Before any court has touched him, he cannot lift his arms to embrace his own mother.9 And the one man in the book who really can live outside every rule spends his last night alone in a cheap hotel. So the claim is this. An argument you cannot answer can still give you no right to act on it. The test of an idea that permits you to harm someone is not whether you can defend it in a room. The test is what living it makes of you, and whether you can still stand among other people once you have. A private right, claimed in secret, to take another person's life cuts the one who takes it out of the human world. And the way back, in this book, is not a better theory. It is other people.

That claim has two limits. This is a novel, and Dostoevsky decided every fever and every coincidence in it, so it cannot prove an argument false the way a counterexample can. What it can do is test what a person must become in order to carry the argument out. And where there is real evidence, about killing, about keeping a terrible secret and about how offenders come back, it points the same way. The second limit is the book's own ending. Its last pages tell a conversion very quickly, and a classic study of Dostoevsky calls them the weakest section of the novel.11 The case against the argument is complete before those pages begin. Only the way back depends on them, and it is the thinnest part of the book.

Part 1: One Life for a Hundred

The argument at the centre of Crime and Punishment is a real argument, and it is stronger than it first sounds. Most people who reject it do so with a feeling rather than a reason. That matters, because a feeling is what the argument tells you to distrust. Heard in full, with nothing taken out, it is hard to answer.

Most people reach first for one of three replies to him, and none of the three is as strong as it feels. The first is simply that murder is wrong. But that is the conclusion under dispute, said again with more force. The student knows murder is usually wrong; his claim is that this case is the exception. The second is that the killer would be caught and punished. That is a reason to be careful, not a reason the act is wrong. If the killer could be certain of never being caught, this reply would have nothing left to say. The third reply is the disgust itself: the thought of the axe, the blood, the old woman's face. And the student has already answered that one. Disgust, on his account, is the voice of the ordinary person, and the ordinary person is exactly what a reformer has to overrule.

The argument is also harder to dismiss because the rest of us trade lives for numbers all the time. A hospital with one free intensive care bed and two patients gives it to the one more likely to live. A government sets a speed limit knowing that a lower one would save a few lives each year. It decides that faster traffic is worth those lives. A vaccine that saves millions is given to everyone, although it will kill a very small number of people. In each case someone has done exactly the student's arithmetic, and most of us think they were right to do it. So the rule "never weigh a life against other lives" is not a rule anyone actually lives by.

Put together, the case is strong. If outcomes are what count, and lives can be weighed, then one cruel old woman against a hundred rescued lives looks like an easy sum. The feeling that says no looks like squeamishness. And the person who acts in spite of the feeling looks like the only one in the room taking morality seriously.

Now the case has to be looked at again. The examples that make it strong are not the same kind of act as the one in the tavern. The intensive care bed, the speed limit and the vaccine share something the student's plan does not. In none of them does one person pick out another particular person and kill her with his own hands. In none of them is the decision made in secret, on one man's private authority, with nobody else able to check it. And in every one of them the people deciding answer to others for what they decided. So what the hospital and the vaccine actually show is narrower than the student needs. They show that lives can be weighed. They do not show that anyone may appoint himself to do the weighing and the killing alone.

The best-known defender of judging acts by their results saw this gap clearly. John Stuart Mill built his ethics on outcomes, and he faced the objection that nobody has time to calculate every act. His answer was that humanity has had "the whole past duration of the human species" to learn what different kinds of act lead to. The rules against killing and stealing are that learning, stored up. People talk, he wrote, as if moral learning started "at the moment when some man feels tempted to meddle with the property or life of another". As if that man had to start "considering for the first time whether murder and theft are injurious to human happiness".23 That is the reply of someone who takes outcomes completely seriously. So his reply cuts against the student, and leaves the concern with outcomes standing. A man alone at a tavern table, re-running the sum for one particular old woman, is the man Mill says should not trust it.

That narrows what the novel is attacking. Crime and Punishment is not an attack on caring about consequences. Its target is one specific step: the step from "the sum favours it" to "so I may do it myself, now, in secret". Every strong example in the student's favour stops short of that step, and that step is the whole of what Raskolnikov will take.

The novel plants the first objection to that step in the tavern itself, and gives it to the officer, almost as a joke. When the student has finished, the officer asks him a plain question. "Would you kill the old woman yourself?". The student answers at once. "Of course not! I was only arguing the justice of it". The officer replies with one line. "If you would not do it yourself, there's no justice about it".24 Then he suggests another game, and that is the end of the conversation. The officer is not a philosopher, and he means something simple. An argument whose author would never act on it has not yet been tested. The student can hold the whole sum in his head because he never has to carry the axe up the stairs. Whether the sum survives being carried up the stairs is the question the officer raises and walks away from.

After all that, the first part of the case still stands at full strength. The sum is a real sum. The easy objections to it are a restatement, a fear of being caught and a feeling the argument has already dismissed. The good examples on its side all stop before one particular step, and a serious defender of outcomes refuses that step too. The officer's question remains, and the student never answers it. Weighing lives is one thing. Giving yourself the right to end one, alone, is another, and it needs an argument of its own. Raskolnikov had written exactly that argument, months earlier, in an article about who has the right to step over the law.

Part 2: The Right to Step Over

Raskolnikov's own theory is not about outcomes at all. It is about permission. It says that a few people have the right to step over the law, even over blood, and that everyone else does not. And the question it leaves him, the one he cannot stop asking, is which of the two kinds of person he is.

Two men in this story hold nearly the same opinion, and the difference between them is what the book is about. The student in the tavern believes the old woman's death would do more good than harm. Asked whether he would kill her himself, he says of course not.26 He has an opinion about what would be good, and that is all he has. Raskolnikov believes the same about the old woman. But he has also worked out, in writing, that certain people are entitled to act on such opinions, alone, against every law.27 He has an opinion about what would be good and a theory about who may do it. The first is a judgment about the world. The second is a claim of permission. Believing the world would be better without the old woman does not, by itself, entitle anyone to remove her. Raskolnikov's article supplies the missing step: a theory of who is entitled to act.

A fair objection comes here. Every brave act is done trembling. A soldier going over the top is terrified, and a whistleblower lies awake for weeks before going to the press. Part 5: Cut Off From Everyone

The punishment in this book begins long before any court, and it is built into the act itself rather than laid on him from outside. The obvious objection is that this is a novel. Dostoevsky decided that his murderer would faint at the police station, fall into a fever and recoil from his mother. That mechanism has been studied, in people who are not characters in anyone's novel. Psychologists who study secrecy have asked large numbers of people to track the secrets they keep. Part 6: The Theory in a Good Suit

It would be comforting if Raskolnikov's theory were a fever, one hungry student's private madness. The novel refuses that comfort. It shows the same logic in a respectable man, in a good suit, saying things his whole society would nod along to. And it puts the link between that respectable logic and murder in the murderer's own mouth.

The respectable man is Luzhin, the prosperous lawyer engaged to Raskolnikov's sister. We first meet him in the mother's letter, and one detail there is enough. He chose a bride "who had experienced poverty", the letter says, because "it is better for a wife to look upon her husband as her benefactor".97 He is not a villain from a melodrama. He is careful, ambitious and proud of being modern, and he thinks of other people chiefly in terms of what he can get from them.

Part 7: The Investigator Who Does Not Argue

The theory has a hole in it, and it is not a hole in the logic. It has no picture of the person who will carry it out. It treats the killer as a calculator who will act exactly as the calculation says. The magistrate Porfiry's whole method is to supply the missing picture.

When Raskolnikov finally tries to say why he killed, the reasons multiply, and he takes back nearly every one. Sonia's part of the case comes to this. His reasons, laid out one by one, mostly cancel each other, which is how a man talks when the reasons came after the act. The theory could not tell her sacrifice from his crime, because it only measures whether a line was crossed. The question it ignores, whose life and for whom, is the one that separates them. Part 9: The Man Who Succeeded

The novel tests the theory twice, and Raskolnikov is only the first test: he tries to live it and cannot. Beside him Dostoevsky puts a second man, Svidrigailov, who can live it, standing outside every rule without the collapse that breaks Raskolnikov. And that man ends emptier than Raskolnikov does. Svidrigailov, too, is written to fail, by an author with a case to make, and that objection is fair. Nothing stops a real man with no conscience to speak of from living a long, comfortable and apparently contented life. The novel picks a man who ends in despair and presents him as the verdict on a whole way of living. That is true, and the novel cannot prove that every such man ends in the street at dawn. What it argues is something about the stance from the inside. In the novel, a man who holds that nothing binds him comes to feel that nothing has any claim to matter. Svidrigailov's boredom is not a punishment that arrives at the end; he describes it himself, in the bath-house speech, long before the gun appears. But the step from no rules to nothing mattering is Dostoevsky's conviction, not a law of logic. A person can accept no rule from outside and still love things, and the novel shows only a man who could not. And even he, at the very end, wanted the one thing the stance cannot produce: to be loved by someone free to refuse.

Two tests have now been run. Raskolnikov tried to step over and could not live there. Svidrigailov stepped over and could live there. He found that the far side is a small grimy room where nothing matters, and the one thing he wanted could not be taken. Between them, the book has shown both ways the theory can go: the man who breaks, and the man who does not break and is emptied. That leaves Raskolnikov, later that same day, walking to the police station. A confession, a trial and a prison sentence ought to settle the matter. Whether they settle anything inside him is a different question.

The epilogue's claim, that a man comes back through a tie to another person and not through punishment, is the thinnest part of the book. Outside the novel it has real support, with real limits. Men bound into a life with someone else commit markedly less crime than the same men alone. Condemnation that keeps the person inside the circle works differently from condemnation that throws him out. The evidence backs the direction of the epilogue, not its inner change. And it names a price, which the narrator counts only for the man and which also falls on the person doing the holding.

Part 14: A Reason You Could Say to Everyone

Openness, properly understood, is not about how an act looks or whether anyone sees it at the time. It is about the reason behind the act. Could that reason be told to everyone, the person who pays for it included, without turning nearly everyone against it?. Raskolnikov's reason cannot survive being told. The family in the attic could tell theirs to almost anyone. And the siege he throws back at his sister is not cleared by this test, because the test was never built to clear anything.

Part 16: Is the Disgust Evidence?

The revulsion that stops a person from killing is not proof, and it can be wrong. The best modern evidence shows it often fixes on the wrong details. But it is not empty noise either, whatever its source. It reacts to the act of harming someone yourself, even when you know the act is pretend. And the people most ready to override it, in these puzzles, turn out not to be the people who care more for strangers. It is information about the doer, which is the one thing the sum has no column for.

Part 17: The Flaw You Cannot Find

Failing to find the flaw in your own argument is weak evidence that it has none. The person who builds an argument is badly placed to test it. People are harder on an argument, and better at telling a good one from a bad one, when it seems to come from someone else. Raskolnikov's flaws were findable, and two other people found them in a single evening, once the argument was on the table. So the refusal he felt was not cowardice, and what an argument you cannot fault needs is someone to disagree with it.

Start with where the argument was tested before the murder. His room, the narrator says, "was more like a cupboard than a room". He had become so "isolated from his fellows that he dreaded meeting, not only his landlady, but anyone at all".366 For a month the plan grew in that cupboard, with one man to state it and the same man to check it. And by the end, the narrator says, "his casuistry had become keen as a razor, and he could not find rational objections in himself".367 He had thought it through, and he could not find the flaw. And in the same breath the narrator says he was hunting for arguments, as though something were forcing him.368 The razor was being used to sharpen a case, not to cut it open. He was the case's advocate and its judge at once, and he was the only person in the room.

Part 18: The Test the Argument Cannot Pass

The strength of an argument is not permission to act on it alone and in secret. That is the claim Crime and Punishment leaves standing. And it comes with a test that anyone can carry out of the book. Could you tell your reason to everyone, the person who pays included, without nearly all of them rising against it?. And would you still be one of the people you love afterwards?. An argument that cannot pass that test has not earned the act, however hard it is to answer. Failing it is a warning, though a reason can fail it and still be right, and then the price of acting is paid in the open. Passing it proves nothing on its own, since a crowd can do wrong together.
