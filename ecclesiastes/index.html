Cold open

There is a book in the Bible which argues that nothing you do leaves anything over for you. The wise die exactly as fools die. The good are not reliably paid for being good, and within a generation nobody remembers either of them. The same book then says, again and again, that nothing is better than to eat your bread, drink your wine and take pleasure in your work. Twice it simply orders it. Most readers hear these as two moods, a dark one and a brave face put on afterwards. They are one argument. Because nothing is left over, what you are handed while you live is the whole of it, and that is the reason to take it.

Most people run their lives on a quiet piece of accounting. Effort goes in, and something is supposed to build up: skill, savings, a good name, children, some piece of work that outlasts you. Rest is allowed because it has been earned, and pleasure is fine as long as the account can afford it. Every so often the account gets checked. The promotion you wanted for three years was good for about a week, and then it was just your job. Someone you knew who did everything right got sick at forty. Someone who cut every corner is doing fine. And under all of it sits the plain question of what will actually be left of you.

The book is called Ecclesiastes, a Greek name for a man who speaks to a gathering. Its speaker calls himself Qohelet, which means much the same thing, and English translations usually call him the Preacher. Almost the first thing he does is ask the account's question directly. He asks what profit a person has from all the work they do under the sun. Profit, in plain business terms, is what is left over when every cost is paid, the part that stays yours. Then he answers it, without flinching.

Later rabbis saw the difficulty. The Talmud records that the sages wanted to suppress it, because its words contradict each other. It says sorrow is better than laughter, and it commends mirth. It praises joy, and it asks what joy is good for. They kept it because it begins and ends with words of the Law, as if a dangerous middle could be guarded by its edges. That is one way to hold a hard book. The same rabbis also took a second way, working through each contradiction to find its reason, and that second way is the one followed here. The middle needs no guarding, and each contradiction has an answer.

Here is the argument in a few lines. There is no profit from a life, nothing carried over and nothing that stays yours once you are gone. There is a portion, the share you are handed while you have it: the bread, the work in your hands, the person beside you. The portion is not a reward paid out of the account, and it is not a distraction from checking it. It comes through work, and it is still received: nobody can store it, and nobody can earn the power to enjoy it. It is everything a life was ever going to be.

That answer has a price, and it will be named as it comes. The book closes the account and offers no plan against the injustice it describes so clearly. It also calls the portion good because God gives it, which leaves an honest question for anyone who does not believe that. How much of the argument survives without the giver? What follows is the whole argument, where it holds, and exactly where it strains.

Part 1: What Is Left Over

The whole book answers one question: what profit is there from a life, meaning what is left over once every cost has been paid. Its answer comes in a single word, repeated from the first page to the last, and the word is breath. That word has to be heard the way the book uses it, because the most common way of hearing it is wrong.

The book opens with the line most people half remember. The old King James translation reads: "Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity". The line is usually heard as a verdict of emptiness. Everything is empty, nothing matters, so why bother. The Hebrew word under it is hevel, and it means breath, the small cloud that leaves your mouth on a cold morning. The book uses it as a picture, and the picture carries two ideas at once.

Take two things you can see on that cold morning. One is a stone on the path. It will be there tomorrow, and if you pick it up, it stays in your hand. The other is your own breath in the air in front of you. It is completely real, you watched it happen, and it is warm. It is gone in a second, and if you close your hand on it, there is nothing in your hand. You also cannot see through it while it is there. That is what the book means by breath. A thing is breath when it does not last, and when it cannot be grasped or seen to the bottom. It is not a thing that is fake or worthless. The breath was real. It simply was not a stone.

This matters because it is tempting to hear the word as meaningless. The book itself rules that reading out. The speaker who calls everything breath also calls food good, and work good, and light sweet, and a life shared with someone you love. A meaningless thing cannot be good. A brief thing can be, and so can a thing you do not fully understand. So wherever this argument says breath, it means brief and ungraspable, not worthless, and the difference will carry the whole book.

The book pairs breath with a second picture, and it uses the pair again and again. The old translation says "vexation of spirit", which hides the image. In the Hebrew the picture is chasing the wind, or trying to herd it. Breath is what the thing is. Chasing the wind is what trying to hold it is like. Between them they describe a person reaching, over and over, for something that is really there and cannot be kept.

Now the question itself. The King James has it this way. "What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?". Profit is the right word, and it should be heard in its plain business sense. A shop takes money in and pays money out, and profit is what remains at the end of the year, the part the owner keeps. The question asks whether a life works like that. All that effort goes in, all those years, and the question is whether anything is left over at the end that is yours.

Under the sun is the book's phrase for the world as anyone can see it. It means what happens in daylight, to anybody who looks, without special knowledge of what lies beyond. It can sound like a world without God, and the book itself will rule that out later. For now it is enough to hear it as a promise about method. The speaker will only claim what can be seen.

Here is what the question does to an ordinary life, run line by line the way the book runs it. Take a working life of forty years. The first entry is skill: a person becomes very good at something. Skill lives in a body and a mind, and both wear out, and at death the skill goes too. The second entry is money. Money can outlast its owner, but at the moment of death it belongs to somebody else. They may spend it well or badly, and the dead will not know which. The third entry is a good name. A name is remembered by the people who knew its owner, and they die too, usually within a few decades.

The fourth entry is children, and it is the one people defend hardest. The fifth is work that outlasts its maker: a building, a business, a book, a garden. These seem to be the answer to the whole question, because they obviously last. The book answers them almost at once. "One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever". Read that slowly as an answer to the ledger. Something does last, and it is the earth. What passes is the generation, which means the people, which means you.

The objection deserves its full strength before the book's answer is judged. A woman builds a school in her town, and it teaches children for a hundred years after her death. A man raises three children well, and they raise theirs well, and something of the way he treated people runs down four generations. Surely that is profit in exactly the sense the question asked. The effort went in, the life ended, and something stayed.

The answer has two halves, and it helps to keep them apart. The first half is that what lasts is no longer hers. The school stands, and she is not in it. She does not know it stands, she cannot enjoy its standing, and the people who run it will change it in ways she would hate. The second half is that what was hers did not last. Her pride in it, her mornings walking past it, the sense of having made something, all of that was real and went with her. The same two halves apply to the children. They carry something of their father, and they carry it as their own lives, not as his account continuing. His love for them was his, and it ended when he did.

So a profit would need two properties at once. It would have to last beyond the person, and it would have to remain the person's own. The school has the first property and not the second. The pride has the second and not the first. The book's claim, in plain terms, is that nothing in a human life has both. The listener can test that against anything they own or have made: a house, a reputation, a pension, a garden. Each one fails on one side or the other. This test sharpens the book's question in plain terms, and the book never states it this way itself.

This is a sharper claim than the one the book is usually credited with. It does not say that nothing lasts, which is false, since the earth lasts and schools last and books last. It does not say that nothing matters, which the book denies outright. It says that the category of profit is empty. There is no line in the account for something that both survives you and is still yours. That is why the word is breath and not nothing. Breath is real while it lasts, and it is never a stone.

The ledger view was given its full strength, so now it has to be said which parts of it survive. Several things in it are kept. Effort is real, and it produces real things. Some of those things are good, and the book will say so repeatedly. Some of them will last long after the person who made them. What is thrown out is one assumption underneath the ledger: that all of this adds up into a total that belongs to you. That assumption is the account itself, and it is the thing the book closes.

There is one reasonable reply left, and it points to the next step. Perhaps nothing is left over only because people die, and death is simply bad luck in the design. If so, the problem is local, a matter of one ending, and the rest of the world might still be building toward something. The book does not accept that. Its next move is to look away from people altogether, at the sun and the wind and the rivers. The world itself, it says, runs the same way.

To gather it up. The question is what is left over from a life, the part that lasts and is still yours. The word for the answer is breath, which means real and brief and impossible to hold, and never worthless. Nothing a person makes is both lasting and still their own. What remains to be seen is whether that is a fact about human death alone, or a fact about the way the whole world moves.

Part 2: The Sea Is Never Full

The lack of profit is not an accident of human death. The book's second claim is that the visible world moves constantly and adds nothing up. The human eye, it adds, is built on the same pattern. If that is right, the account cannot come out positive even while a person is alive, and death only makes the result final.

The book makes this case with four pictures in a row, and it is worth going through them slowly, because each one does a different job. The first is the sun. It rises, it goes down, and it hurries back to the place where it rose. Nothing about the sun is lazy. It is working every day, and every day it arrives exactly where it started.

The second is the wind. In the King James the wind "whirleth about continually, and the wind returneth again according to his circuits". It goes south, it turns north, it circles, and it comes back. The word circuits is the key, because a circuit is a path that ends where it began. There is enormous motion in it, and at the end of each circuit no distance has been covered at all.

The third picture is the most famous. "All the rivers run into the sea; yet the sea is not full". Then the rivers go back to the place they came from, and run again. Two ordinary things show what that line is claiming. Put a bucket under a tap and turn the tap on. Each second of water adds to what is there, and after a while the bucket is full, and you can carry it away. Now think of the sea. Rivers have been running into it for thousands of years, far more water than any bucket, and the sea is no fuller than it was. The bucket and the sea both receive water. Only the bucket accumulates. The book is saying the world is the sea and not the bucket: water pouring in without end, and nothing piling up.

Then the fourth picture turns from the world to the person watching it, and this is where the argument bites. "All things are full of labour; man cannot utter it: the eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing". The eye is placed in the same list as the sun, the wind and the rivers. It takes in sights all day, every day of a life, and it never reaches the point of being full.

Think about what that means in an ordinary evening. Someone watches one episode of a series, then another, then a third, and at no point does the eye report that it has now seen enough. Someone travels a long way to see a famous coastline and is genuinely moved by it. Within an hour they are looking at the map for the next view. Neither of these is a flaw in the person. It is the design. Seeing is the sea, taking in every river and never full.

This step carries the weight of the passage, and it is a second claim, separate from the first. The first was that nothing lasts and stays yours after death. This one is that nothing fills even during life. The objection a moment ago was that perhaps nothing is left over only because people die. Here the answer comes. Setting death aside, a life of seeing and hearing and working would still not fill. The eye doing the receiving is the sea. The account does not fail at the funeral. It fails a little every evening, in the ordinary hours when the person is still very much alive.

Now to the most famous line of the passage. "The thing that hath been, it is that which shall be; and that which is done is that which shall be done". And then the famous close: "there is no new thing under the sun". Before going on, it is worth making a guess about what that means. The obvious reading is a claim about invention. Nobody ever makes anything new, every idea has been had before, and the modern world is secretly the ancient one. Most people who quote the line mean something like that, and most people who reject it are rejecting that.

The book's next two sentences show what it actually meant. First: "Is there any thing whereof it may be said, See, this is new? it hath been already of old time, which was before us". Then: "There is no remembrance of former things; neither shall there be any remembrance of things that are to come with those that shall come after". Compare that with the guess. The claim is less about invention than about memory. A thing feels new because what came before it was forgotten. The newness is produced by the forgetting.

The old translation says former things, and the next chapter makes it plain that people are included. There the speaker complains that the wise man is remembered no longer than the fool. So the line reaches the listener directly. Whatever you did, the people who come after will not remember it, and they will feel as new in their lives as you felt in yours. That feeling will be accurate for them, and it will rest on your being forgotten.

The strongest objection here is an obvious one. Progress is real. People fly, talk across oceans, and carry the world's libraries in their pockets. The speaker of this book could not have pictured any of it. Whatever he meant by nothing new, the modern world looks like a flat refutation. And a book that is wrong about this may be wrong about the rest.

On technology, the objection wins, and it should be conceded plainly. If the line means that no new tools or discoveries appear, it is false, and there is no point defending it. But the objection was meant to show that something accumulates, and here it has to be checked against the ledger of the school and the children. A profit had two properties. It had to last, and it had to remain its maker's own.

Run progress through those two tests. It lasts, certainly. Knowledge piles up over centuries in a way the sea never does. But to whom does it belong? It belongs to the species, which is to say to whoever happens to be alive to use it. The people who made each step are dead, and almost all of them are forgotten, which is exactly what the line about remembrance said. The listener can test this in a few seconds. Name the person who first worked out how to keep water clean enough to drink in the city you live in.

So progress is a real accumulation, and it sits on the lasting side of the ledger, the side that is not yours. It belongs with the earth that abides while the generations pass. The objection is right that something piles up, and wrong that it piles up for anyone in particular. What survives of the book's claim is this. Under the sun, what accumulates does not belong to the one who made it, and what belongs to a person does not accumulate.

Here is the whole argument of the passage as plain steps. One, the world moves in circuits, and its motion adds nothing up. Two, the eye and the ear run on the same pattern, receiving without ever filling. Three, memory does not keep what came before, so what feels new is partly what was forgotten. Four, the one real accumulation, human knowledge, belongs to whoever is alive and not to those who made it. So nothing lasts and stays its maker's, and nothing fills even while the life goes on.

There is still a way out for the ledger, and it is the one most ambitious people quietly rely on. Perhaps ordinary lives do not add up because they are run on too little money and too little knowledge, with no freedom to try things properly. A person with every advantage might find the thing that fills. The book takes that possibility seriously enough to test it, and the test is its most famous story.

So much for the world. The world under the sun is a sea that is never full, not a bucket, and so is the eye that watches it. Nothing new is mostly nothing remembered, and what does accumulate belongs to nobody who made it. The book's next move is to hand one man unlimited money, wisdom and pleasure, and see whether he finds the surplus that ordinary lives miss.

Part 3: The King's Experiment

The last hope of the ledger is that ordinary lives fail to add up only because they are run on too little. The book tests that hope with the one person who could test it completely, a king with unlimited money, time and learning. He tries every route to something left over, and each route gives him something real and nothing left over. Out of that test the book's second key word appears for the first time: portion.

First, who is speaking. The book opens with "The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem". It never says Solomon, but the description points at him, the richest and wisest king the tradition remembered. The book is not his. Its Hebrew is centuries later than Solomon, with a couple of words borrowed from Persian. Scholars place it in the fourth or third century before Christ. After the royal test is over, the speaker talks about kings the way a subject does, from the outside, with caution.

So the king is a costume, and the costume has a job. The question of profit needs a test with no excuses left in it. If an ordinary person finds no surplus, it can always be said that they were too poor, too busy or too ignorant to find it. A king who is also a sage has none of those limits. Putting on his clothes turns the book into an experiment with every variable set to its maximum.

The first route is knowledge. He gives his heart, as he puts it, to search out by wisdom everything that is done under heaven. He finds that what is bent cannot be straightened, and that what is missing cannot be counted, so some lacks no effort can make up. He also finds that the more he understands, the more there is to grieve over. In his words, "in much wisdom is much grief: and he that increaseth knowledge increaseth sorrow". Wisdom will get its own hearing later, because the book does not abandon it. For now the result is simple: knowing more did not produce anything left over. It produced a clearer view of the problem.

The second route is pleasure, and here the order of the telling matters. He announces the verdict before he describes the experiment. "I said of laughter, It is mad: and of mirth, What doeth it?". Then he describes it. He tries wine, carefully, keeping his judgment while he does it. He builds houses and plants vineyards. He makes gardens and parks and plants every kind of fruit tree in them, and he digs pools to water the trees.

He buys servants, and has servants born in his house. He owns more herds than anyone before him in the city. He collects silver and gold and the treasure of kings, and he gets singers, men and women, and every delight a person can have. Then comes the line that ties the experiment to the eye: "whatsoever mine eyes desired I kept not from them". The eye that is never satisfied with seeing is here given everything it asks for. This is the exact test of whether the sea can be filled, if only enough rivers are poured in.

Now read the result carefully, because it comes in two verses and most readers only hear the second. The first verse says: "for my heart rejoiced in all my labour: and this was my portion of all my labour". He enjoyed it. The work of building gave him real joy, and he names that joy with a word that will carry the rest of the book. It was his portion, his share, what fell to him from all that effort.

The second verse is the verdict. He looks at everything his hands have made. "And, behold, all was vanity and vexation of spirit, and there was no profit under the sun". Breath, chasing the wind, and no profit. Put the two verses side by side and the whole book is visible in them. The joy was real, and it was his portion. The joy was not left over, and it was not profit. He does not say the pleasure was fake, or shallow, or secretly unhappy. He says it was a share he received while it lasted, and that it did not add up to anything he could keep.

The third route is to compare wisdom with folly directly, and here he is scrupulously fair. "Then I saw that wisdom excelleth folly, as far as light excelleth darkness". The wise man has eyes in his head, and the fool walks in the dark. That is a real difference, and he never takes it back. Then he adds the fact that wrecks the comparison: "and I myself perceived also that one event happeneth to them all". The wise man dies, and the fool dies, and the book adds that neither is remembered for long.

Then the argument turns dark. "Therefore I hated life; because the work that is wrought under the sun is grievous unto me". He hates his own work, and he gives a precise reason. He will have to leave it to whoever comes after him. "And who knoweth whether he shall be a wise man or a fool?". Everything he built with skill and wisdom will pass to someone who did no work for it at all.

One detail in that complaint should be kept for later. The book says the man who did not work will receive the whole estate as his portion. The word is the same word the king used for his own joy a few lines earlier. A portion, it turns out, is not simply wages. It usually comes through work, and it is still what falls to someone, whether they earned it or not. The next part builds on exactly that.

The king's experiment is not only a literary device, because a real man ran it in the nineteenth century and wrote down the results. Leo Tolstoy, in a short book about his own crisis, describes himself at the moment it struck. He was not yet fifty. He describes what he had. "A good wife who loved me and whom I loved, good children, and a large estate which without much effort on my part improved and increased". He was famous, and he was physically strong. By any ledger, his account was full.

And a question stopped him, which he wrote down in plain words. "What will come of what I am doing today or shall do tomorrow?". It is the profit question in a different language. He could not answer it, and the failure was not abstract. He writes that he hid a cord from himself so that he would not hang himself from a beam in his own room. He stopped taking his gun out shooting, so that he would not be tempted to use it on himself.

When Tolstoy looked for anyone who had faced the same question, one of the witnesses he quoted at length was this book. He copied out the lines about the wise man remembered no longer than the fool and the king who hated life. He set its speaker beside Socrates and the Buddha, among the wisest people who ever lived, all giving the same answer. For Tolstoy at that point, the book was evidence for despair. He will come back at the end, because where he eventually landed is close to where this book lands, and the difference between them matters.

The modern version of the experiment is smaller and more familiar. Someone sets a goal that will make things different: the house, the title, the finished degree, the number in the account. They reach it, and for a week or so it is exactly as good as they hoped. Then it becomes the floor they stand on, and the eye has already found the next thing. Nothing went wrong. The goal delivered its portion in full, and it was never going to deliver a profit.

There is an obvious objection to all of this, and it is worth answering properly. Perhaps the king's problem was simply that he was a king. He had everything handed to him, and people who have everything get jaded. A person struggling for something real, a nurse on night shifts or a parent working two jobs, finds meaning the king never could. The book has already answered this, in its own words. The king did not report boredom. He reported that his heart rejoiced in all his labour, which means the work itself, the building and planting, gave him joy. The failure was never that he felt nothing. It was that what he felt did not accumulate.

The parent working two jobs has the same structure with smaller numbers. The child fed is a real good, and the hour together on Sunday is a real joy. Both are portion. Neither is carried over into a surplus the parent keeps after death. So the objection is right that struggle can be full of meaning, and wrong that it escapes the ledger's problem. The result is that meaning is found in the portion, by kings and parents alike, and profit is found by neither.

At the end of the second chapter, the king says the first of the sentences that close readers know this book by. "There is nothing better for a man, than that he should eat and drink, and that he should make his soul enjoy good in his labour". He adds that he saw this was from the hand of God. God gives wisdom, knowledge and joy to the one who pleases him, and gives the sinner the work of gathering and heaping up for someone else.

And then the passage closes with a line that is easy to skip: "This also is vanity and vexation of spirit". The line most likely points at that gathering and heaping, not at the meal. And even read more widely, it would call the meal breath in the book's sense, brief and real, which is a description and not a retraction.

The experiment is over, and its result is clear. The richest possible test produced joy that was real and nothing that was left over. The king's first conclusion is that nothing is better than to eat and drink and enjoy the work. It remains to decide whether that sentence is resignation, the shrug of a man who lost the argument. It might instead be a finding, the real answer to the question the book began with.

Part 4: Profit and Portion

The sentence the king ended on is a finding, not a shrug. The book separates two things that the ledger treats as one. Profit is what is kept and carried over, and it does not exist. Portion is the share handed to a person while they have it, and it does exist, and it is the whole of what anyone gets. Once the two are apart, the advice to eat and drink stops sounding like a consolation and starts sounding like the conclusion of an argument.

Two ordinary cases make the difference sharp. A farmer brings in a harvest and does two things with it. Part of it goes into the granary, stored against the winter and the years after. Part of it goes onto the table that night, and the family eats it. Ask the ledger which of those is the real result of the year, and it points at the granary. The meal is a cost, a deduction from what could have been saved. The book points the other way. The granary is breath, since the grain rots or is sold or passes to strangers when the farmer dies. The meal is the one entry that was fully real, and it was fully received.

The same shape turns up in money. Someone is paid on a Friday. Part of the wage goes into savings, and part of it buys an evening with friends. The savings are what the ledger counts. The evening is what the person actually had. In the book's two words, what stays and carries over is profit, and what is received and used up in the living of it is portion. Every life is made of portion, and the ledger has been keeping score of the wrong column.

The book comes back to the portion over and over. Scholars who study the book usually count seven passages that say it, spread from the second chapter to the eleventh. Five of them are findings, in the form nothing is better than this. Two of them are commands. Read together, they make one argument, and the argument has a shape that is easy to miss when they are read one at a time.

Here is the shape. Most of the portion passages come directly after one of the book's hardest observations, and they read as a conclusion from it. In the third chapter, the speaker has just said that humans and animals die alike and nobody knows where the human spirit goes. The next line follows. "Wherefore I perceive that there is nothing better, than that a man should rejoice in his own works; for that is his portion". The old English says wherefore, and the Hebrew simply says and I saw. The conclusion is carried by the order: first the fact that nobody can see what comes after, then the portion.

The same happens in the eighth chapter. The speaker has just said that good people sometimes get what the wicked deserve, and the wicked what the good deserve. Then: "I commended mirth, because a man hath no better thing under the sun, than to eat, and to drink, and to be merry". And he gives a reason, and the reason is the phrase that matters most here. The joy, he says, "shall abide with him of his labour the days of his life". The Hebrew verb means to go along with, to accompany.

That phrase answers the ledger. The ledger asked what stays with a person. The answer is that joy goes along with them, through their labour, for the days of their life. It is the one thing from all that labour that is truly theirs while it lasts. Nothing stays with them after, and so what stays with them during is the whole of what they get. That is a direct answer to the book's opening question.

Now the detail that the resignation reading always misses. If the advice were only a shrug, it would mean something like this: nothing matters, so relax and have a nice dinner. That assumes enjoyment is easy, that anyone with bread on the table can eat it with pleasure. The book denies exactly that. It says the power to enjoy is a gift of its own. Of the fortunate man it says that God "hath given him power to eat thereof, and to take his portion". Then, flatly: "this is the gift of God".

The power to eat is a separate gift from the food. And the book gives the case where the second gift is missing. A man has riches and wealth and honour, and lacks nothing his heart wants. "Yet God giveth him not power to eat thereof, but a stranger eateth it". He loses it, to death or to someone who takes it, and a stranger enjoys what he owned. The book calls this breath, and an evil disease. A modern extension of the same picture is familiar. Some people sit at a good table unable to taste anything, because the mind is on tomorrow's meeting or last year's loss. The bread was there. The power to eat it was not.

This changes what the advice is. A shrug says that pleasure is available to anyone who stops worrying. The book says that pleasure has two parts, the thing and the capacity to take it, and that neither part is fully under a person's control. Both are received. That is why the book calls the portion a gift. It is also why the heir to the king's estate receives the estate as his portion without having worked for it. A portion is what falls to someone, never a balance paid out of the account.

There is a way to test how much this book changed the old advice, because the advice is much older than the book. In an older version of the Gilgamesh epic, written down more than a thousand years earlier, the hero has lost his closest friend to death. He is running across the world looking for a way not to die himself. At the edge of the world he meets a woman who keeps a tavern, and she tells him to stop.

In a translation from 1920, where she is called the maiden Sabitum, her words run like this. "The life that thou seekest, thou wilt not find". The gods, she says, gave death to mankind and kept life for themselves. So fill your belly, rejoice day and night, keep your clothes clean, wash your head. Then two lines that are hard to hear without feeling them. "Care for the little one who takes hold of thy hand!" And let your wife rejoice in your embrace.

Now set that beside the ninth chapter of this book. "Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy, and drink thy wine with a merry heart". "Let thy garments be always white; and let thy head lack no ointment". Live joyfully with the wife you love, all the days of this brief life. It is nearly the same list: the food, the clean clothes, the washed and oiled head, the person you love. Heard side by side, the resemblance is plain.

The list is almost identical. In the epic, the advice is given to a man who has just been told he cannot have what he came for. It is the consolation prize. The real prize, life without death, belongs to the gods, and the tavern keeper is telling him to settle for second best. This book keeps the list and calls it the portion: "for that is thy portion in this life". And it adds a reason the epic does not give: "for God now accepteth thy works". The Hebrew is stronger than the old English: God has already approved what you do.

With that approval added, the consolation prize has become the share itself. The bread is not what a person gets instead of the real thing. In this book there is no other real thing on offer for the one who eats it. The bread is already approved, and it is the portion, which means it is what was meant for that person. That approval is no sign read off good fortune. The same chapter says no one can tell from events whether God loves or hates them. The same meal, eaten in the epic, is a sad compromise. Eaten in this book, it is the point.

This is also the place to deal with a tempting way to read the whole book, because the text here refutes it. On that reading, the body of the book shows life without God. Under the sun, on this view, means the world with heaven shut out, and so of course it looks like breath. Then the ending brings God back and the despair is answered. The reading has real attractions. It protects the book from sounding faithless, and it gives a clean story, with darkness first and light at the end.

The text does not allow it. Here is one sentence from the fifth chapter, in two halves. It says it is good and comely for one to eat and to drink. Then: "and to enjoy the good of all his labour that he taketh under the sun". And it goes on: "all the days of his life, which God giveth him: for it is his portion". The phrase under the sun, the words God giveth, and the word portion all sit in that one sentence. God is inside the under the sun world as the giver of the portion, from the second chapter on.

So the phrase has to mean something else. It names the world of the living, seen from inside, with God present and no view of what comes after. The portion passage after the death of humans and animals ends with a question. Who shall bring him to see what shall be after him? Nobody can, and that is the limit the phrase names. So the reading that the body of the book is godless does not fit its own sentences. What replaces it is harder and better: God is present, God gives, and God does not show anyone the total.

That leaves the long argument among readers of this book about its mood. One side says the joy passages are resignation, the faint praise of a man who has lost and is making the best of it. The other side says the speaker is a preacher of joy, and the dark passages are the shadow that makes the light visible. Both sides can point to verses, and both miss the word the book itself keeps using.

It is not resignation, because a resigned man does not call enjoyment a gift that can be withheld. It is not a cheerful gospel of joy, because the portion passages sit among death and forgetting and breath. The joy is received. A received good is real, and a person can lose the power to take it, and it was never theirs to keep. That is exactly the kind of good the book calls breath.

Here is the argument as plain steps. One, profit is what stays and carries over, and the book has shown there is none. Two, portion is what is received and lived through, and it stays with a person for the days of their life. Three, the portion has two parts, the good thing and the power to enjoy it, and both are given. Four, the book adds that God has already approved the portion, which turns the old consolation prize into the share itself. So the advice to eat your bread with joy is the conclusion of the argument about profit, and not a retreat from it.

Here is where this stands. The ledger kept score of the wrong column. The portion is real, it is given, and it is everything a life contains. But a gift comes when the giver decides, and the book is very aware of that. So the next thing to settle is who decides when each thing happens in a life, and whether a person has any say in it.

Part 5: A Time for Everything

Everything in a life has its right time, and the book's claim is that the times are given to a person rather than chosen by them. It adds something sharper. People were given a sense of the whole of time, and no access to it. That gap, between feeling that there is a total and never being shown it, is exactly the shape of the ache that drives the ledger.

The passage that makes this case is the poem of times. It opens: "To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven:". Then comes a list of pairs, fourteen of them, each one a thing and its opposite. A time to be born and a time to die, a time to plant and a time to pull up. A time to weep and a time to laugh, a time to mourn and a time to dance. A time to keep silent and a time to speak, a time for war and a time for peace. Most people feel they know what it means.

The usual reading is attractive, and it should be heard at its best. It says that life has seasons, and that a wise person learns to read them. There is a right moment to speak up and a right moment to stay quiet. There is a right moment to leave a job, and a right moment to stay. Grief has its season, and it will pass, and laughter will come round again. On this reading the poem is advice about timing, and the lesson is to choose your moments well.

If the poem were advice about good timing, the next line would say something like: so the wise person waits for the right season. What the book actually says next is its opening question again, in nearly the same words. He asks what profit the worker has from all his work. The poem turns out to be evidence for the problem of profit, and only incidentally about timing.

Look again at where the list starts. A time to be born, and a time to die. Nobody chooses either. The poem opens with the two times no one has ever set for themselves, and that tells the listener how to hear the rest. The time to mourn arrives when someone dies, whether you are ready or not. The time to tear down arrives with the storm or the bank or the diagnosis. The time for war is declared by others.

So the charity owed to the usual reading can now be settled. What is kept is that actions have fitting moments, and that a sensible person notices them. What is dropped is the idea that the person sets the clock. The times come. A person can recognise a season and act well inside it. They cannot order it up, and they cannot know in advance when the next one will arrive. That is why the poem leads straight back to profit: a life whose seasons are handed to it cannot be planned into a surplus.

Then comes the sentence that carries the most weight. In the King James it reads: "He hath made every thing beautiful in his time". And then: "also he hath set the world in their heart". And then the limit: "so that no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end".

One word in that sentence has two meanings, and a choice has to be made openly. The old translation says the world. In later Hebrew the word came to mean the world, and in the Bible it usually means a very long stretch of time, reaching toward forever. The second meaning is the one that fits, a sense of the whole of time, because the rest of the sentence is about exactly that. What cannot be found out is the work of God from the beginning to the end. The thing placed in the heart and the thing withheld are the same thing, seen from two sides.

Here is what that means in a picture. Someone finds a single page torn from a novel. They can tell it is part of a story. There are characters on it with pasts, a sentence that begins on the page before, and a conversation that plainly goes somewhere. The page makes them want the book. They can feel there is a plot, and they would give a great deal to know how it ends and what this page is for. Now set beside that a dog lying in the sun. The dog is inside the same story, and it does not wonder what its afternoon is for. It has its portion, and no sense of the whole to torment it.

The human being is the one creature that holds the page and wants the book. That is what the verse says was placed in the heart. And the analogy has an edge that should be named. A torn page can, in principle, be matched to its book in a library somewhere. The verse says the whole cannot be found out by anyone. Later the book adds that even a wise man who claims to know it cannot. So the page is a picture of the wanting, and not a promise that the book exists on any shelf a person can reach.

This is where the ledger comes from. The wish for a profit, for a life that adds up to a total, is the page wanting its book. It is not a stupid wish, and it is not a modern invention. It was put there, in the book's own words, by the same hand that made each thing beautiful in its time. The ache is built in. What is withheld is the view of the whole that would let anyone read their life as a sum.

What the book does with that follows the pattern of the portion passages, and a listener can predict the next line. It returns to the portion. There is nothing better, it says, than to rejoice and to do good in one's life. Everyone should eat and drink and enjoy the good of their labour, because that is the gift of God. The page cannot be read as a chapter of a finished book, and it can still be lived as a page, fully.

Then the book explains why the gap exists, and the explanation is where many modern readers flinch. "I know that, whatsoever God doeth, it shall be for ever: nothing can be put to it, nor any thing taken from it". Then comes the purpose: "and God doeth it, that men should fear before him". God's works are fixed, nothing added and nothing taken, and the book says this is so that people will fear before him.

Fear here has to be pinned down, because the word sounds like terror, and terror would make nonsense of the book's joy. The clearest clue is a line from the fifth chapter about how to behave in the house of God. "For God is in heaven, and thou upon earth: therefore let thy words be few". Fear, in this book, means knowing which side of the gap you are standing on. God is the one who sees from the beginning to the end. The person is the one holding the page.

In practice the difference is small to look at and large to live. One person writes a ten-year plan and treats it as a promise the world has made. Another writes the same plan, works just as hard at it, and holds it as a guess. The second person has the posture this book calls fear of God. Someone without the book's God can share that posture, though not the whole of the fear, which in the book also has an object.

There is a serious objection here, and it is the charge of fatalism. If every time is set, and nothing can be added or taken away, then effort looks pointless. Why plan, why work, why choose, if the season will come regardless? A reader could take this passage as permission to stop trying.

The answer is that a person's own action is part of how the times come. The poem does not picture seasons arriving over the heads of idle people. A time to plant is a time when someone plants, and a time to build is a time when someone builds. The fatalist treats his own effort as standing outside the pattern, when it is one of the threads it is woven from. And since nobody knows which season is next, waiting cannot even tell him when to start. The book will later say it plainly. Sow in the morning and keep working in the evening, because you do not know which will prosper. So the times are given and the work is still required, and the only thing surrendered is control.

That settles the times. The times come to a person, the whole is felt and never seen, and fear means living on the near side of that line. But one kind of time matters more than the rest to anyone who has been wronged. Straight after this passage, the speaker says to himself that God will judge the good and the wicked, because there is a time for every purpose. The next step is to check whether that time for judgment ever actually comes under the sun, where anyone could see it.

Part 6: The Same Fate

What anyone can see under the sun does not support the belief that the good are paid and the wicked punished. The book says so, repeatedly, and it refuses to smooth the point over. It also refuses to give up the belief. It holds the belief and the observation side by side, and deletes neither, and that refusal is its honesty.

The belief is not foolish, and its strongest form comes first. The book of Proverbs, which sits beside this one in the Bible, states it plainly. "The fear of the LORD prolongeth days: but the years of the wicked shall be shortened". The modern version drops the religion and keeps the shape. Be honest, work hard, keep your word, look after your health, and on the whole things go better for you.

On average, that is often true, and this book agrees with a good deal of it. It has its own proverbs about the lazy fool who ruins himself. It knows that folly usually costs. Virtue often helps. The trouble is that an average is a fact about many lives, and the ledger wanted a promise about one.

The first thing the speaker reports is the courts. "And moreover I saw under the sun the place of judgment, that wickedness was there". The very place built to sort the good from the wicked had wickedness sitting in it. His first response is the belief. He says to himself that God will judge the righteous and the wicked, because there is a time for every purpose. The time for judgment exists. He simply does not say it is a time anyone will see.

Then he goes further, into a passage many readers find hard to bear. He says that what happens to humans and what happens to animals is the same. As one dies, so does the other, and they all have one breath, so "a man hath no preeminence above a beast: for all is vanity". They all go to one place, all come from dust and return to it. Then he asks the question. "Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth?".

That question needs to be heard exactly, neither harder nor softer than it is. He does not say the human spirit goes nowhere. He does not say it goes up. He says nobody knows. By what anyone can observe, a dead person and a dead animal look the same. No one has seen where either went. The judgment he believes in is given no place he can point to.

The second thing he reports is oppression, and he describes it with care. He sees "the tears of such as were oppressed, and they had no comforter". On the side of the oppressors there was power, and still the oppressed had no comforter. Then comes his verdict, and it is very dark. "Wherefore I praised the dead which are already dead more than the living which are yet alive".

And he goes one step further. Better than both, he says, is the one not yet born. That one "hath not seen the evil work that is done under the sun". This sounds like the purest pessimism, the view that it would have been better never to exist. It needs to be looked at closely, because it seems to sit badly beside the joy passages.

So it has to be checked carefully, starting with what the verdict is attached to. It comes straight out of the scene just described, the tears with no comforter. The reason it gives is specific: the unborn have not seen the evil work. Every living person who looks has seen it, so the verdict falls on the living in general. It is a judgment on what watching oppression with no comfort does to a person, and the book does not take it back.

The second place the book says something like this makes the condition explicit. A man has a hundred children and lives many years, "and his soul be not filled with good". He even goes without a burial. Then, the book says, a stillborn child is better off than he is. The condition is written into the sentence. The man had everything the ledger counts, the long life and the huge family, and his soul was not filled with good. He was never given the power to take his portion, and so the verdict falls on him.

Other dark lines name no condition at all, and honesty requires saying so. The king hated life. The seventh chapter says the day of death is better than the day of one's birth, with no reason given. That line is read with its neighbours shortly. But the two strongest verdicts, the ones about the unborn and the stillborn, both name what makes a life worse than none.

Now the result can be stated. The pessimism is real, and the book does not take it back. Its two strongest verdicts give two reasons. The first is having seen the evil done to people with no comforter. Every living person has seen it, so that is not a condition but a wound. The second is a soul never filled with good, and that is exactly what the portion addresses. For the man with a hundred children, the portion is the dividing line between a life worth having and one worse than none. The wound the book leaves open, and it comes back at the very end. And the same book will later say that a living dog is better than a dead lion.

The third thing the speaker reports is what delay does to people. "Because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil". That is a sharp observation about human beings, and it holds up. When wrongdoing is not punished soon, people learn from it. Delayed justice teaches its own lesson, which is that nobody is keeping the ledger at all.

Then comes the contradiction that most troubled the old readers, and it sits in three consecutive sentences. First the belief, stated as knowledge: "yet surely I know that it shall be well with them that fear God". Then its other half: "But it shall not be well with the wicked, neither shall he prolong his days". Then, straight after, the observation.

"There is a vanity which is done upon the earth; that there be just men, unto whom it happeneth according to the work of the wicked". And the reverse is also true: wicked men get what the good deserve. He ends: "I said that this also is vanity". In one breath he knows it will go well with the good. In the next he has seen it go badly with them.

There are two easy ways out of this, and the book takes neither. The first is to delete the belief. That is the cynic's move: the good are not paid, so goodness is for fools, and the whole idea of justice is a story. The second is to delete the observation. That is the pious move: if the good suffer, they must not really have been good, or the suffering must secretly be a reward. Both moves buy a tidy ledger, and both buy it with a lie.

What the speaker does instead is keep both, and give the gap between them a name. He calls it breath, something real that he cannot grip or see through. He is saying that the injustice is real, that he cannot see through it, and that he will not pretend otherwise. He also will not pretend he has stopped believing. The ruling here is that this is the only honest report available to someone who holds a belief that their eyes keep contradicting.

There is one more objection, and it is the one most believers would reach for first. Perhaps justice is done after death, and the ledger is balanced there. Then the good who suffer are paid later, and the belief and the observation stop contradicting each other. The book does not say that. It says who knows whether the human spirit goes up. At the very end it says the spirit returns to God who gave it, and that God will bring every deed into judgment. It does not say when, or where, or how, and it never offers that judgment as a payment a person can plan around.

So what survives of the belief is this. Justice belongs to God, and the book keeps saying so. It is not visible under the sun, and it cannot be entered in anyone's ledger as an expected return. The speaker places one more fact beside all of this, the one that no average can touch. "There is one event to the righteous, and to the wicked". Both die. Whatever else differs between them, that event is the same.

Stripped to its steps, the case runs this way. One, the doctrine that goodness is paid is often true on average, and this book agrees. Two, an average is not a promise to any one person, and the courts, the oppressed and the good who suffer show it. Three, its two strongest verdicts name what makes a life worse than none, and one of them is exactly what the portion answers. Four, the speaker keeps both his belief and his observation, and calls the gap between them breath. So goodness cannot be a line in the ledger, and the portion answers one of the book's two darkest reasons, while the other stays open.

The accounting of justice ends there. The good are not reliably paid under the sun, and the book will not lie about it in either direction. That creates a real problem for anyone who wants to be good. If goodness does not reliably pay, then the obvious reason to be good has gone. That leaves the practical problem of how a person should be good at all, once the guarantee has been taken away.

Part 7: Be Not Righteous Over Much

Goodness pursued as an investment is a way of destroying yourself. That is the warning inside the book's strangest piece of moral advice. What it recommends instead is goodness with no guarantee attached, held by someone who knows they are not the judge of how it turns out. The same chapter then says where that kind of goodness is learned, in the house of mourning. And it shows what happens when the ledger is turned on other people.

The advice comes straight out of the observation that the good are not reliably paid. "There is a just man that perisheth in his righteousness, and there is a wicked man that prolongeth his life in his wickedness". Proverbs promised that the fear of the Lord adds days to a life. He has seen the good man's days cut short, and the wicked man's days prolonged.

Then comes the line that has embarrassed a great many readers. "Be not righteous over much; neither make thyself over wise: why shouldest thou destroy thyself?" And straight after it, the balancing line. "Be not over much wicked, neither be thou foolish: why shouldest thou die before thy time?".

There are two ways to read this, and one has to be chosen and said. The first reading takes it as a recipe for moderation: be good, but not too good, and bad, but not too bad. On that reading the book licenses a little wickedness for the sake of balance. The second reading takes it as a warning against a particular kind of righteousness, the self-appointed kind. That is someone who has made their own goodness into a claim, a position they hold against the world.

The reason for choosing the second reading is in the verse itself. The danger it names is that such a person will destroy themselves. The world does sometimes destroy honest people, and the book has just said so. This warning is about what a person does to themselves. What destroys people from the inside is a particular expectation laid on top of their goodness, and the verses just before have shown what it is. It is the belief that goodness will be paid.

Take two people. Both work for the same company for twenty years. Both are honest, reliable and kind to the people under them. Both are laid off in the same week, in a round of cuts decided by someone who never met them. The first one believed they had a contract with the world. Twenty years of doing everything right was an investment, and the return was supposed to be security. When the layoff comes, what breaks is not only the job. It is the whole account. They become bitter, or they conclude that honesty was for suckers, or they cannot stop replaying what they should have done differently.

The second one was just as good, for just as long, and did not believe in the contract. They were honest because honesty was the right way to treat people, and kind because the people were there. The layoff hurts them, and it may frighten them badly. But nothing they believed has been refuted. Their goodness was never a deposit, so there is nothing to be defrauded of. The book is warning the listener away from being the first person. Being righteous over much, in its sense, means being righteous as a strategy. And a strategy that the world does not reward will destroy the one who staked everything on it.

The second half of the advice closes the other exit. If goodness does not pay, it might seem to follow that wickedness is fine. The book blocks that at once. Wickedness and folly still kill people early, often enough, and the speaker says so. The point of the pair is not a midpoint between good and bad. Folly often shortens a life, and goodness does not reliably lengthen one, so neither can be worked as a machine for outcomes. A person who knows that is freed to be good for its own sake.

The next verse seems at first to favour the moderation reading. It is good to take hold of this, it says, and not to withdraw your hand from that. The this and that are the two warnings just given, and the verse ends: "he that feareth God shall come forth of them all". Fear of God, as the torn page showed, is the posture of someone who knows they hold one page and not the whole story. That person is not the one keeping the books. So they can be good without demanding payment, and avoid folly without fooling themselves that prudence guarantees anything. They come out of both traps.

A few lines later the book adds a sentence that makes the self-styled righteous person impossible anyway. "For there is not a just man upon earth, that doeth good, and sinneth not". Nobody's goodness is complete. So nobody's account could balance even if the world did pay, because everyone would owe as well as be owed. The listener can take the last step alone. Anyone claiming their goodness entitles them to a return is claiming to be the one person the sentence says does not exist.

The same chapter opens with a run of sayings that sound gloomy and turn out to be practical. A good name is better than precious ointment, and the day of death than the day of one's birth. Then: "It is better to go to the house of mourning, than to go to the house of feasting". And the reason: "for that is the end of all men; and the living will lay it to his heart".

This is where the line about the day of death belongs. Read with the sayings around it, the day of death is praised for what it teaches the living. At a funeral, a whole life is suddenly visible, and the people in the room find themselves asking what mattered in it. A feast does not do that. The heart of the wise, the book says, is in the house of mourning, and the heart of fools is in the house of mirth. It is a claim about where people learn, not a wish to die.

This is the lesson the first worker, the one who believed in the contract, needed, and the claim behind it can be tested. Before hearing the result, a guess is useful. Suppose someone learns they have little time left. The popular expectation is the bucket list: travel, novelty, crowding in every experience they have missed.

A psychologist at Stanford has summarised her laboratory's studies of this question, and the result runs the other way. When time is felt to be short, people narrow their lives toward the people who matter most. Old people, asked whom they would spend half an hour with, choose close family and old friends over interesting strangers. The telling part is what happens when the time horizon is changed. Young people asked to picture moving far away soon start choosing like the old.

The most striking group was young men with HIV, in the years before there was effective treatment. They saw their social world, in her words, "the same way that very old people do". Compare that with the guess. Faced with an ending, people did not reach for more. They reached for the people they already loved. The caution belongs with the result. Most of these studies ask what people would choose in imagined situations, and they measure preferences, not whole lives. But the direction is consistent, and it is the direction the book described. The living lay it to heart.

The chapter also gives a rule for good days and bad days, and the old translation is exact. "In the day of prosperity be joyful, but in the day of adversity consider". The reason it gives: God has set the one against the other, "to the end that man should find nothing after him". Good days are portion, and they should be taken as portion, with joy. Bad days are for thinking. Neither can be predicted from the other, and that is the point.

Then the speaker turns the ledger on the search for good people, and here the book says something ugly that should not be softened. He says he found more bitter than death "the woman, whose heart is snares and nets". He says he searched, "counting one by one, to find out the account". He found one upright man in a thousand, and not one woman among them all.

That is hostile, and there is no honest way to make it say something kinder. Two things can be said about it plainly. First, his own conclusion a line later blames everyone: "God hath made man upright; but they have sought out many inventions". The word there means human beings, men and women alike.

Second, and more important for the argument, look at the method he used. Counting one by one, to find out the account. He tried to audit humanity the way the ledger audits a life, item by item, looking for the balance. The audit failed, and it produced a bitter and unjust verdict on half the human race. And the book leaves a clue that it sees what happened. In Hebrew, the word for account and the word translated inventions come from the same root, to reckon. People were made upright, and they went looking for many reckonings. Whether or not he meant it against himself, the one finding he keeps judges the calculating habit, and his own audit with it.

One loose thread is left. Goodness survives the loss of its guarantee, and becomes cleaner for it. But the book has just shown its own speaker falling back into the ledger, counting people one by one. The habit is hard to break, and there must be a reason it keeps returning. What needs explaining is what keeps the ledger running in ordinary lives, day after day, even in people who know better.

Part 8: Two Handfuls and the Wind

The ledger keeps running for two reasons, and the book names both. Most work is driven by comparison with the neighbour, and appetite grows with what it is fed. Between them, they guarantee that getting more of what was wanted cannot close the account. The book's answer is small and exact: one handful with quiet, and someone to share it with.

The first reason comes in one sentence from the fourth chapter. The King James puts it this way: "I considered all travail, and every right work, that for this a man is envied of his neighbour". The Hebrew is blunter than the old English. It says that all toil and all skill are a man's rivalry with his neighbour. The reading used here is that one, because the verse is explaining where the work comes from.

That is a large claim, and it sounds cynical. So it should be tested on an ordinary case. Someone gets a raise at work, a real one, bigger than they expected. That evening they learn that a colleague at the same level got a larger one. Nothing about their own money has changed, and yet the raise now feels like an insult.

Here is a question worth guessing at before the answer. Suppose your own income falls by some amount. Now suppose instead that your income stays put, and your neighbours' incomes rise by the same amount. Which would hurt your happiness more? Most people would say their own loss, obviously, since that is money actually leaving their hands.

An economist tested something close to this, using a large American survey that recorded people's happiness and the earnings around them. He found that "higher earnings of neighbors are associated with lower levels of self-reported happiness". And the size of the effect was the surprise. A rise in the neighbours' earnings and a similar fall in one's own income reduced happiness by amounts of about the same size. So the guess was wrong. The neighbour's gain, which costs you nothing, weighs about as much as your own loss. The caution is that this is a survey, which shows a pattern rather than proving a cause. But it is exactly the pattern the verse describes.

Now the consequence for the ledger. If the work is driven by rivalry, then its goal is not an amount. It is a position, being ahead of the neighbour. And the neighbour is also working. Every gain one person makes raises the bar for everyone else, and theirs raises the bar for the first. A race run against a moving line has no finish. That is why the verse ends where every such verse ends: this also is breath, and chasing the wind.

The book does not conclude that work should stop, and it closes that exit at once. "The fool foldeth his hands together, and eateth his own flesh". The man who refuses to work in protest does not escape the problem. He starves. So the book offers a third option between the rival's race and the fool's folded hands. "Better is an handful with quietness, than both the hands full with travail and vexation of spirit".

The picture is worth holding for a moment. One hand full is enough to eat. It is the portion. The second hand is the extra, the part that exists to be more than the neighbour's, and filling it costs quiet. The book does not say the second handful is sinful. It says it is expensive, and that what it costs is exactly the thing the first handful was for.

Then the book gives a portrait that is hard to forget. "There is one alone, and there is not a second; yea, he hath neither child nor brother". Yet there is no end to his work, and his eye is not satisfied with riches. Then comes the line that carries the portrait. In the Hebrew, the man asks it himself, in the first person. For whom am I toiling, and depriving myself of good?

It is the right question, and nothing in his life answers it. He works for no one, and he has no one to leave it to. He has deprived himself of every good, and there is no one his work is for. He is the ledger with no owner, an account kept for its own sake, and the book calls it breath and a sore trouble.

What follows is a warm passage, and it is the answer to him. "Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour". If one falls, the other lifts him up, and there is sorrow for the one who falls alone. Two lying together are warm, and one alone cannot get warm. Then the famous close: "a threefold cord is not quickly broken".

Notice what the word reward is doing there, in a book that has just denied profit. The reward of two is not a surplus left over after death. It is being lifted when you fall, and warm at night, and harder to break. The word reward there is a wage, and the book has no quarrel with wages inside a life; what it denies is the surplus. This reward is portion, and it is shared. The lone man's problem was never that he earned too little. It was that he had no one to eat with, and so every handful went into the second hand.

The second reason the ledger keeps running is appetite, and the fifth chapter states it as a rule. "He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver; nor he that loveth abundance with increase". Then an observation about wealth that every rich household knows. "When goods increase, they are increased that eat them". More money brings more staff, more dependants, more costs, more people with a claim on it.

And the owner, the book asks, gets what good from it, except looking at it. That is the eye that is never satisfied with seeing, now looking at its own possessions. Then comes the line that is almost a lullaby. "The sleep of a labouring man is sweet, whether he eat little or much: but the abundance of the rich will not suffer him to sleep". The worker sleeps because his day is done. The rich man lies awake guarding what he cannot eat.

This invites a direct test, because the question of whether more money makes people happier has been measured carefully. If the book were saying that past a certain point money buys nothing, it would be in trouble.

In 2023 three researchers who had disagreed about exactly this question ran a joint analysis, and published it under the title "a conflict resolved". They asked people at random moments how they felt right now. They found that for most people, happiness keeps rising with income and never levels off, though it rises more slowly the more there already is. Only the least happy group, about one person in seven, levelled off, at around one hundred thousand dollars a year.

That slowing has an exact shape, and an earlier paper from the same study gives it in numbers. Going from twenty thousand dollars to sixty thousand buys about the same rise as going from sixty thousand to one hundred and eighty thousand. Each equal step in happiness costs about three times as much money as the step before.

So here is what the measurement does to the book. The verse is about the lover of silver, a type these studies cannot pick out. For the ordinary earner, more money did bring more happiness. Money does help, on average, and it keeps helping, and anyone who took this book to mean money is worthless was wrong. The book itself never said that. It says elsewhere that wisdom is a shelter and money is a shelter.

But the measurement also shows the shape the book described. Each rise costs a multiple of the last one. A person who wants to feel the next step as strongly as the last has to multiply what they have, and then multiply it again. It is a climb that gets steeper with every step. The measurement cannot confirm the verse about the lover of silver. What it does show is the diminishing return: more helps, and each more costs more, so the account never closes.

The sixth chapter puts the whole of this in two lines. "All the labour of man is for his mouth, and yet the appetite is not filled". And then: "Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the desire". The Hebrew says the walking of the appetite, and the picture is exact. The sight of the eyes is what is actually in front of a person now. The appetite is always walking ahead, toward the next thing. Better the first.

There is a serious objection to all of this. Rivalry and appetite built the modern world. The drive to do better than the next person produced cathedrals, vaccines and most of the good work anyone has ever admired. A person without appetite would build nothing, and a society without rivalry might never cure anything. If the book is telling people to want less, it is telling them to be less.

The book's own words answer this. It calls the rival's labour every right work, which grants that the work is skilful and real. It condemns the fool who folds his hands. What it denies is only one thing: that the rivalry can deliver satisfaction to the person running it. The cathedral stands and the builder is not satisfied, because the neighbour's cathedral is taller. What survives is that rivalry can produce excellent work, and cannot produce the rest that it promises.

The steps, set out plainly, are these. One, much work is driven by rivalry, and a measured pattern matches the claim. Two, a race against a moving neighbour has no finish. Three, appetite grows with what it is fed, and each step of satisfaction costs a multiple of the last. Four, the answer is one handful with quiet, eaten with someone else. So the portion is small, it is shared, and it is the only part that can actually be eaten.

Both engines of the ledger are now named. Comparison and appetite keep the ledger running, and they explain why even people who know better keep it. The obvious hope is that a clear enough mind could see through both, and step out. That is the promise of wisdom, and the book has been circling it since the king's first experiment. Wisdom is the book's candidate for that way out, and it is tested next as hard as money was.

Part 9: Light That Does Not Show the Map

Wisdom is the best tool a person has, and it works locally, not globally. It lights the next step, and it never shows the map. The book is careful on both halves of that claim. It grants wisdom a kind of profit, a real advantage inside a life. It denies wisdom the one thing the ledger wanted from it, a view of the whole.

Start with how the book itself knows things, because that is wisdom at work. The speaker almost never appeals to tradition, or to a revelation, or to what his teachers said. He says I saw, I considered, I said in my heart, I tested. He applies his heart to search out everything done under heaven. He reports what he finds, including what he did not want to find.

This method is why the book could be so honest about the good who are not paid. A teacher repeating a doctrine can leave out the good man who died young. A man reporting what he saw cannot. Much of what the speaker calls seeing is really reflection, thinking about what he saw, and not eyesight alone. Wisdom, in this book, is observation plus hard thinking about it, and both have limits.

Now what wisdom can do, because the book grants it a great deal. In the king's experiment, he saw that wisdom excels folly as light excels darkness. The word translated excels there is the same Hebrew word as profit, the word from the book's opening question. So the book does grant a profit of a kind. Wisdom has an advantage over folly, a real one, and it is the advantage of light over darkness.

Later the book gives a picture of that advantage, and it is a very practical one. If the iron axe is blunt and the man does not sharpen it, he has to swing harder. Wisdom is the advantage that sharpens it. The same word again, profit, now meaning the edge that lets a person do the job with less force. A wise person gets more done, wastes less, and walks into fewer holes.

The book also gives a story. There was a small city with few people in it, and a great king came against it and besieged it. "Now there was found in it a poor wise man, and he by his wisdom delivered the city". The book draws the moral plainly: "Wisdom is better than weapons of war".

Then the story turns, in the same sentence. "Yet no man remembered that same poor man". The city was saved, the man went back to being poor, and nobody thought of him again. The book adds the lesson: "the poor man's wisdom is despised, and his words are not heard". And a second lesson, from the other direction: "one sinner destroyeth much good".

Put the story's two halves together and the whole position on wisdom is visible. Wisdom has a profit in the local sense. It saves the city, sharpens the axe, and lights the step. It has no profit in the ledger's sense. The poor wise man was not paid, not remembered, and not listened to afterwards. And his wisdom was fragile. Years of it could be undone by one fool with power. As the book says elsewhere, a few dead flies make a whole jar of perfume stink.

This sharpens the opening question in a way that matters. The book is not saying that nothing has any advantage over anything else. Wisdom beats folly, light beats darkness, a sharp axe beats a blunt one, and those advantages are real every day. What the book denies is a surplus that survives. The wise man and the fool die alike, and the advantage of light ends when the eyes close. Inside a life there are relative profits, and at the end of it there is no absolute one.

Wisdom also has a cost, and the king named it in the first chapter. In much wisdom is much grief, and more knowledge means more sorrow. The wiser a person becomes, the more clearly they see the oppressed with no comforter, the good man cut off, and their own death. A fool is spared some of that by not looking.

So there is an obvious question. If wisdom brings grief and no surplus, why keep paying for it? The book's answer is in the picture it chose. The wise man's eyes are in his head, and the fool walks in darkness. The grief is the price of seeing where one is going. A person who stops thinking to avoid the sorrow does not avoid the pit. They only stop seeing it, and the book has already said what happens to the fool who folds his hands.

Now the limit, which the book states three times and more sharply each time. First, about himself: "I said, I will be wise; but it was far from me". Then, about what he was looking for. "That which is far off, and exceeding deep, who can find it out?". And finally, in the eighth chapter, the general rule.

However much a person labours to find out the work that is done under the sun, they will not find it. And then the line aimed straight at the experts: "though a wise man think to know it, yet shall he not be able to find it". The book is not saying the wise know nothing. It is saying that a wise person who claims to see the whole plan is claiming something no one has.

This is where a modern objection has real force, and parts of it should be conceded plainly. The book's examples of what cannot be known are sometimes things that are now known. It says a person cannot know how the bones grow in the womb of a pregnant woman, or which way the wind will go. Anyone who has seen a scan of an unborn child, or checked tomorrow's weather, knows those are no longer closed questions. Some of its examples of the unknowable are now understood, and it is better to say so than to defend them. Its point in that verse, though, was about the work of God who makes everything.

But the objection has to be checked against what the book was actually denying. Its claim was that no one can find out the work from the beginning to the end. Science has pushed back the darkness on every side, and it has not done that. It cannot tell a person which of their projects will succeed, whether their children will be happy, or what their life will have amounted to. Modern knowledge is a far brighter lamp. It still lights a path, and it still does not print the map. The upshot is that knowledge grows locally, and the view of the whole stays withheld.

That is the whole case on wisdom. The book trusts wisdom to see the next step and distrusts anyone who claims to see the whole. That leaves a very practical problem. Every decision a person makes is a bet on an outcome they cannot see. The book has just said the outcome is hidden even from the wise. So the book owes an answer to how a person should act at all, when they cannot know how any of it will turn out.

Part 10: Cast Thy Bread Upon the Waters

When the outcome cannot be known, the book gives a practical rule, and it is not to wait. Act, spread what you have across more than one hope, and stop watching the wind for a perfect moment. Then, whatever the hand finds to do, do it with full strength, because the grave is where work ends. Effort survives the loss of profit, and the book asks for more of it, not less.

The rule starts from a line that is quoted far more often than it is understood. "The race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise". And it goes on: "nor yet riches to men of understanding, nor yet favour to men of skill; but time and chance happeneth to them all".

The easy reading is that talent does not matter, and the race is a lottery. That cannot be what it means, because this book has already said that wisdom beats folly as light beats darkness. The swift usually do win the race. It is the point about averages, made sharp. Speed makes winning likely, and it does not make winning yours. Time and chance fall on the fast runner too, the stumble, the bad day, the storm that arrives in the one hour it matters.

The next verse adds how chance arrives. People do not know their time, the book says. They are "as the fishes that are taken in an evil net". Like birds in a snare, they are caught in an evil time that falls on them suddenly. The weight is on suddenly. The fish was swimming well a moment before. Nothing in how it swam predicted the net.

The tenth chapter gives the ordinary version, and it is worth hearing correctly. "He that diggeth a pit shall fall into it; and whoso breaketh an hedge, a serpent shall bite him". This is often heard as a proverb about villains getting what they deserve, and in context it is something plainer. The man is digging a pit because that is his job. The man breaking through a hedge is clearing ground. The next line adds that whoever splits logs may be hurt by them. Ordinary work carries ordinary risk, and no amount of skill removes it.

So the situation is this. A person must act, because the fool who folds his hands eats his own flesh. And every action is a bet on an outcome no one can see, where skill improves the odds and guarantees nothing. The eleventh chapter is the book's answer to that situation, and it comes in four short rules.

The first rule: "Cast thy bread upon the waters: for thou shalt find it after many days". The Hebrew verb is send, as in sending bread out over the water. Some readers take this as charity, and others as trade, goods sent overseas on a ship. On either reading the bread leaves your hand. It goes where you cannot follow it, and the book promises it will come back, but late, after many days. The rule is to let go of what you send, and to send it anyway.

The second rule: "Give a portion to seven, and also to eight; for thou knowest not what evil shall be upon the earth". The word portion is back, and now it is something given out. Split what you have across several hopes, because you do not know which disaster is coming. A farmer who plants one field loses everything to one flood. A person who has only one source of meaning, one job, one friend, one plan, is in the same position.

The third rule is the sharpest. "He that observeth the wind shall not sow; and he that regardeth the clouds shall not reap". A farmer who waits for the perfect sowing weather will find the wind always slightly wrong. He will keep waiting, and the season will pass, and he will have nothing.

Everyone knows a version of this person, and many people are this person about something. They are going to start the business when the market settles. They will have the child when things are more stable, and write the book when they have more time. They will say the hard thing to their father when the moment is right. The book's point is that the wind is never certain, because the whole is hidden. Waiting for certainty is waiting for a view that no one gets, and the sowing season does not wait with them.

The fourth rule gathers the others. "In the morning sow thy seed, and in the evening withhold not thine hand". The reason: "for thou knowest not whether shall prosper, either this or that, or whether they both shall be alike good". Sow early and sow late, because you cannot know in advance which sowing will come up. Perhaps both will, and perhaps neither.

The objection to all this is serious. All of this is just prudence. Spread your risks, do not wait for perfect conditions, work hard: it is sound advice, and any sensible grandmother could give it. Worse, it seems to smuggle the ledger back in. Diversifying is what investors do to maximise their returns, and the whole book was supposed to be about giving up on returns.

The first half of the objection should simply be granted. It is prudence, and the book does not mind being practical. A philosophy that cannot tell a person how to plant a field is not much use to a person with a field. The second half needs an answer, and the answer is not the not-knowing, since investors spread their risks for exactly that reason.

The difference is what the sowing is for. Two of the rules rest openly on not knowing. The first does promise a return, but after many days, at no set time, found rather than collected. The ledger sows in order to add the harvest to a total that is supposed to stay. This book sows because sowing is the work that has been handed to the person, and whatever comes back is eaten, not banked. If the bread comes back after many days, that is portion, received when it comes, and never a debt the waters owed.

Then comes the sentence that turns all of this from prudence into something closer to a creed. It is in the ninth chapter, inside the passage about bread and wine and the person you love. "Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might". And the reason: "for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest".

The reasoning there is the reverse of what a resigned person would conclude. A resigned person would say that since the grave ends all work, work does not matter, so do it half-heartedly. The book says that since the grave ends all work, the work is only available now, so do it with your whole strength. The effort is not an investment toward a later total. It is portion, like the bread, and it can only be taken while the hand can still hold it.

The difference shows in a small case. A carpenter fits a joint inside a cabinet, where no customer will ever see it. One carpenter does it roughly, because nobody will know, and nothing will come of doing it well. The other does it perfectly, for no reason except that it is the work in front of him and his hands are good at it. Neither will be remembered for the joint. Only the second one had the portion that was on offer that afternoon.

Numbered, the practical rule stands on four steps. One, skill improves the odds and guarantees nothing, because time and chance fall on everyone. Two, so a person should send out what they have, spread it, and stop waiting for certain weather. Three, what separates this from investing is its purpose: the sowing is the work in hand, and the return is eaten, not banked. Four, the grave is the reason to work with full strength now, since work exists only while life does. So effort is portion too, and the book asks for all of it.

So action no longer needs the ledger. The book gives a way to act without the ledger: send, spread, sow, and work with all your might. But there is one condition on all of it that it has left until last. The hand that finds work to do, and the eye that enjoys the light, belong to a body, and the body is wearing out. What the book does with that is the business of its final chapter.

Part 11: Before the Silver Cord Be Loosed

The book does not end on a doctrine. It ends on a body growing old, and the command to rejoice is timed against that body: now, before. This is the last and hardest blow to the ledger, because the ledger's favourite move is to put the portion off until the account is full. The book answers that the account is never full, and that the body taking the portion will not wait.

It starts from a line in the ninth chapter that sounds like a joke and is not one. "A living dog is better than a dead lion". In the Bible the dog is a despised animal, so much so that a man could call himself a dead dog to say he was nothing. The book puts a despised living thing above the proudest dead one, and it gives a reason.

"For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing, neither have they any more a reward". Knowing that you will die is presented here as an advantage the living have. The dead have no portion left, and they cannot know anything, including that they had one. The living can still take theirs. This is the book's own counterweight to its verdict that the unborn are better off. A life that can take its portion outranks the grandest life that is over.

Then, in the eleventh chapter, the eye that is never satisfied with seeing comes back one last time, and it gets a different verdict. "Truly the light is sweet, and a pleasant thing it is for the eyes to behold the sun". The eye cannot be filled, and it can still be pleased, and the book has kept those two apart from the start. Satisfaction would be profit, and sweetness is the portion, which the eye can have every morning the sun comes up.

The second command to rejoice follows. "Rejoice, O young man, in thy youth; and let thy heart cheer thee in the days of thy youth". Just before this, the book adds that even a long life should remember the days of darkness, for they will be many. Then comes the command: walk in the ways of your heart and the sight of your eyes. Then it adds a clause that seems to take it all back: "but know thou, that for all these things God will bring thee into judgment".

This clause is easy to hear as a warning to enjoy less. Read in its place, it is attached to the command, not set against it. The young man is told to rejoice, and told he will answer for how he used his days. That includes the joy he was commanded to take. The Jerusalem Talmud pushes this to its end. Every person, it says, "will have to justify himself for everything his eye saw and which he did not eat".

Then comes the line that opens the final chapter. "Remember now thy Creator in the days of thy youth, while the evil days come not". Before the years arrive, it says, "when thou shalt say, I have no pleasure in them". The key word is before. Everything that follows describes the time when pleasure is no longer possible, so that the listener will take the portion while it is.

What follows is a strange poem about old age, and its reading has to be chosen. It opens with the sun and moon and stars going dark and clouds returning after rain, as if a storm were coming over a great house. The reading followed here is the one the Talmud already gives, which hears it as a human body shutting down. The details fit a body too closely to be chance, and the poem ends at a funeral.

Here is the poem, piece by piece. "In the day when the keepers of the house shall tremble, and the strong men shall bow themselves". The frame that held the body up begins to shake and bend. "And the grinders cease because they are few". The teeth have gone. "And those that look out of the windows be darkened". The eyes are failing.

The doors to the street are shut, and the sound of the mill is low, because the ears no longer catch the noise of the day. The old man rises at the voice of a bird, because his sleep has grown thin and the first birdsong wakes him. He is afraid of heights, and of dangers in the road. The almond tree blossoms, which some readers hear as the hair going white. "The grasshopper shall be a burden, and desire shall fail". Even something as light as a grasshopper has become a weight to carry, and wanting itself runs out. Then the plain words: "because man goeth to his long home, and the mourners go about the streets".

Then the poem changes pictures, and the last images are of two things breaking. "Or ever the silver cord be loosed, or the golden bowl be broken". That is a lamp, a golden bowl of oil hanging by a silver cord. When the cord gives way, the bowl falls and the light goes out. "Or the pitcher be broken at the fountain, or the wheel broken at the cistern". That is a well: the jar that draws the water shatters, and the wheel that lowers it breaks. Light and water, the two things a household cannot live without, fail together.

"Then shall the dust return to the earth as it was: and the spirit shall return unto God who gave it". The breath that was given goes back to the giver. Honesty needs one sentence here. This line does not promise that the person continues, or that they will be rewarded. Earlier the book asked who knows whether the human spirit goes upward, and this line does not answer with a doctrine. It says only that life was given, and returns to the one who gave it.

And then the book closes its frame with the line it opened with. "Vanity of vanities, saith the preacher; all is vanity". Breath of breaths, all is breath. After the poem, the word has its full meaning. It is the last breath that leaves the body, and it is also every real and brief good the body had while it breathed.

Now the reason the timing matters so much. The ledger's favourite move is deferral. Work now, enjoy later, when the account can afford it. The big trip is for retirement, and the long dinners with friends are for when things calm down. The good wine is kept for a special occasion that keeps not quite arriving.

Hold that plan against the poem. Later is when the grinders are few and the windows are darkened. Later is when desire fails and the sweet light is harder to see. The deferred portion is not waiting safely in an account. It is being set aside for a body that will be less able to take it, and may not be there at all. The listener can finish that thought for themselves, about their own postponed things.

This is the place to set the book beside a famous modern answer to the same problem. The writer Albert Camus described what he called the absurd. In the English translation, "The absurd is born of this confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world". His image was not from this book but from Greek myth, the man Sisyphus. He was condemned to roll a rock to the top of a mountain forever, and watch it fall back each time.

The resemblance to this book is plain. Sisyphus is the sun hurrying back to where it rose, the rivers running into a sea that never fills. He is enormous effort with nothing left over. And Camus ended his essay with a famous sentence. "One must imagine Sisyphus happy". For Camus, that happiness comes from revolt, from facing the absurd with full clarity and refusing to be crushed by it.

So the two answers can be compared exactly. Both say there is no profit. Both say the effort is still worth making, and that happiness is possible inside it. The difference is where the happiness comes from. For Camus it is taken, seized in defiance of a silent world. For this book it is received. The bread is given, the power to eat it is given, and God has already approved the work.

That raises the question left open at the start, and it needs a straight answer. How much of this book's argument survives for someone who does not believe in the giver? A great deal survives. Everything argued under the sun was argued from observation, and it stands on observation. There is no profit, and the portion is real. Goodness does not pay reliably, the whole cannot be seen, and work with full strength is the only way work can be had.

Two things change without the giver. The first is small, and it concerns the power to enjoy. That power is still not under a person's control, which anyone who has sat at a good table unable to taste has learned. Without a giver, that is simply a fact about human beings, not a gift. The second is large, and it concerns approval. The book says the portion is approved, already accepted, meant for the one who eats it. There are secular cousins of this idea, and none of them says the bread is approved. Even the word portion loses something, since it means an allotted share, and a share implies someone who allots.

Without that approval, the portion becomes one of two things. It becomes Camus's happiness, a defiance, joy taken in the teeth of a world that does not care. Or it becomes gratitude with no one to thank, which many people feel and cannot explain. So the argument survives. The structure holds for everyone, and what only the believer keeps is the permission, the sense that the bread was meant for them.

One page of the book remains. The book's own argument ends with the breath going back to the giver, and the frame line repeated. But the book does not end there. A few more lines follow, spoken about the teacher rather than by him, and they give a conclusion that many readers think undoes everything before it. Those lines either take back what the book has argued, or tell the listener what to do with it. Only one of those readings fits the text.

Part 12: The Whole of a Person

The book's last lines do not take back a single thing the teacher observed. They tell the listener what to do with it all: fear God and keep his commandments, because this is the whole of a person. Many readers hear those lines as a pious patch sewn over a dangerous book. The patch reading is wrong, because fear of God was already in the body of the book, doing work. The commandments are new, and they add exactly what the body had left thin.

First, something changes in the voice. After the frame line, breath of breaths, the voice that framed the book at its start returns and talks at length about the teacher. The Preacher, it says, was wise, and taught the people, and set many proverbs in order, and looked for words that were right and pleasing. This is someone looking back on the teacher's work, perhaps a student, perhaps an editor. Nobody knows who wrote these lines, and nothing in what follows rests on knowing.

The new speaker says two things about the teacher's words, and both are exact. "The words of the wise are as goads, and as nails fastened by the masters of assemblies". A goad is the pointed stick used to drive an ox, and it hurts. A nail holds something in place. The teacher's sayings are meant to do both, to prod the listener out of the ledger and then to hold them somewhere steadier.

Then comes a warning that every reader of long books has felt. "Of making many books there is no end; and much study is a weariness of the flesh". Put that beside everything that came before, and it is the river and the sea once more. Study can become its own ledger, an account of things read and known that is supposed to add up to a total understanding. The book warns that it will not, and that the body pays for trying.

Then the conclusion. "Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments". And the reason, in the old translation: "for this is the whole duty of man". The word duty is not in the Hebrew. The translators added it. The Hebrew says, more simply and more strangely, for this is the whole of the human being. That reading, the whole of a person, answers the book's first question directly.

The book began by asking what is left over from a life. It ends by saying what a life is, whole. The ledger looked for the whole of a person in a total at the end, a surplus carried over. The conclusion puts the whole of a person somewhere else. It is in how they stand, and what they do each day with the portion they are given.

Now the old worry about contradictions. The sages who nearly suppressed the book kept it, the Talmud says, because its beginning and its end are words of Torah. That is the patch reading in its oldest form, with the edges guarding a dangerous middle. But the same rabbis also worked through the middle, telling apart the joy of doing a commandment from joy that is empty.

That second approach was the right one, and every contradiction in the book has had its reason given along the way. Each pair names two different things, and each half is true of its own thing.

And the conclusion itself was already in the middle. Fear of God appears right after the poem of times, where God does what he does so that people will fear before him. It appears in the warning about vows, where the book says plainly: fear thou God. And it appears in the advice not to be righteous over much, where the one who fears God comes out of both traps. The epilogue does not import fear of God. It picks up the thread that ran through the whole book and ties it off.

The commandments add something the body of the book had left thin. If the whole cannot be seen, a person still needs to know what to do on an ordinary Tuesday. The ledger answered that question by calculating toward the total. With the total gone, the epilogue gives a different kind of answer, a way of living that does not depend on seeing how it ends. A person does not need to know the sum to know not to steal, to keep faith, to rest, and to honour their parents.

Then there is the last sentence of the book, and it should not be smoothed. "For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil". The teacher said many times that judgment is not visible under the sun. The good are cut off and the wicked prolong their days. So it can look as though the last line contradicts him.

It does not. The book as it stands held this belief beside its observations all along. The speaker told himself that God will judge the righteous and the wicked, and told the young man he would be brought into judgment. The last line states the belief alone, and like the body of the book, it names no time and no place.

Now Tolstoy, who was left with a hidden cord and this book as his witness for despair. He did not stay there. He writes that he began to draw near to "the believers among the poor, simple, unlettered folk". He saw that their whole lives were passed in heavy labour, that they were content with life, and that they met suffering and death calmly. And he writes: "I understood that that is life itself, and that the meaning given to that life is true: and I accepted it".

Set that beside this book, and the likeness is close. The labourer whose sleep is sweet, the bread eaten with joy, the work done with full strength: the book described those people long ago. But there is a difference, and it matters. In Tolstoy's account, what the working people had was a faith that gave meaning to the whole of their lives. Their faith answered the total.

This book never answers the total. It gives the portion, the fear of God and the commandments, and it says plainly that the whole stays hidden. That is harder than what Tolstoy found, and it is also more available. It does not require a person to be sure what their life adds up to. It requires them to stand in the right place, and to take what they are given.

There is one place where this whole answer strains, and it should be named at the end. The portion assumes bread on the table. The book saw the tears of the oppressed who had no comforter, and praised the dead above the living. Its one piece of advice about the oppression of the poor is not to be surprised by it. Higher powers, it says, watch over the high ones. It does not say how to end oppression, or even that anyone should try.

What its own terms support is smaller than a plan. It grieved that the oppressed had no comforter. It said two are better than one, because one lifts the other when he falls. A person with bread and a companion can be the comforter the oppressed lacked. The book never makes that a command, and it would not end oppression if it did. That is where the answer strains, and it is also the one step its words leave open to anyone listening.

Here is the argument of the whole book, as plain steps. One, the question is what is left over from a life, the part that lasts and is still yours. Two, nothing is both lasting and yours, and nothing fills even while you live. Three, the richest possible test found joy that was real and nothing left over. Four, what a person gets is portion, given, brief, and real, and even the power to enjoy it is given. Five, the times are given, the whole is withheld, and the good are not reliably paid. Six, so goodness asks nothing back, work is done with full strength, and bets are spread. Seven, the body taking the portion will not wait, so the portion is now. And eight, a person stands on the near side of the line, and that standing is the whole of them.

So the account can be closed. It was never going to show a profit, and it was keeping score of the wrong column all along. What remains is smaller than a total and much more real. Tomorrow morning there will be bread, and whatever work the hand finds, and probably someone to eat with. None of it will be left over. All of it is the portion, and the book already said what to do with it. Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy.
