Cold open

One of the oldest long stories we have is about a man who finds out he is going to die.1 He tries every way out that people still try today. It is called the Epic of Gilgamesh, and it was written on clay tablets in what is now Iraq.2 Its claim is hard and simple. Death cannot be beaten, outrun or explained away. Each of the usual answers fails as a way out, at an exact point the story shows you. What is left standing at the end is not a way out. It is a man who stops trying to keep himself and starts handing things on. He leaves the walls of a city, and a written account of his hardships, sealed in a box for a stranger to open.

Most people already have a sensible view of death, and it is worth stating at full strength. Everyone dies, and everyone knows it. The sane responses are well known. Make a life that matters, and leave something behind, so that some part of you lasts. Enjoy the days you have, because they are all there is. Fear of death is mostly confusion, since when death arrives you will not be there to suffer it. And if all else fails, medicine keeps adding years.

People who have just buried someone tend to find that none of this touches the thing that keeps them awake. The epic explains why, and it does it by letting its hero try each answer in turn. He goes after a name that will outlast him, and it holds only while death belongs to other people. He refuses to let his dead friend go, and a worm ends the refusal. He is told to eat, wash, dance and hold his child, and the advice is true and does not reach his fear. He finds the one man who never died, and learns that the gift was a single accident that will never be repeated. He is challenged to stay awake for a week, and seven loaves of bread show what sleep took from him. He is handed a plant that makes the old young again, and a snake eats it while he bathes.

Then he walks home to his city and shows a boatman its walls. That ending can be read as bitter, a man left with nothing but bricks. Read with the opening lines beside it, it says something else, and that something else is the epic's answer.

Every one of those answers depends on a difference most people never notice until it is forced on them. There is knowing that everyone dies, and there is knowing that you will. The king at the start of the story has the first kind of knowledge and none of the second, which is where most of us live.

Part 1: The King Who Saw Everything

The epic opens on a man who knows that people die, the way most of us know it, as a general fact that costs him nothing. And its very first lines tell you, before anything happens, that the story is the record of how that changed. Both halves of that claim are in the text. The second is easy to miss, because it sits in the part of an old poem that most readers skip.

First, one fact about the object itself. The story was copied and reworked on clay for well over a thousand years, so it survives in more than one version. The fullest is a later standard version on eleven tablets, and its opening and its ending were built to answer each other.3 A few famous lines survive only in an older Babylonian copy, and when one of those comes up, it is called the older copy.4

Notice what that instruction does not say. It does not say read out his victories. It says read out his misfortunes, his sufferings, the things that went wrong. The story is offered to you as a record of hardship, kept in a locked box, to be opened by a stranger who was not there. Hold on to that box. The poem's last lines send the listener back to it, and nobody at the start yet knows why.

So the frame already makes a claim. Something happened to this man that changed what he knew, and it cost him. What he ended with was a written account that other people could read, and his own survival was no part of it. The rest of the epic is the road between the first line and the box.

Then the story proper begins, and the man it shows you is not wise at all. He is, in the poem's own words, "two-thirds of him god but a third of him human".12 He is tall, beautiful, strong beyond anyone in the city, and he has no equal.13 And the people of Uruk are suffering under him.

So the first thing that ever stands in Gilgamesh's way is another person. Keep that in view. The second thing that stands in his way is death, and the epic ties the two together so tightly that neither makes sense alone.

Here is the distinction the rest of the epic turns on, and it needs two cases before it gets a name. A man reads in the newspaper that thousands of people died on the roads last year. He believes it. He may even know the number is true. And then he drives home a little too fast, as he always does, and nothing in him has changed. A second man gets a phone call from a doctor about a scan. The doctor uses a calm voice and a long word. Nothing in the call is news, in one sense, since he already knew that people die of exactly this. But he puts the phone down and the kitchen looks different.

Both men know that people die. What differs is the person it is about. The first man knows it about everyone. The second man knows it about himself. Those are the two kinds of knowing that the epic separates, and their plain names will do: knowing it about everyone, and knowing it about yourself.

The first man could pass any exam on mortality, so the difference cannot be in the information. It lies in what the knowledge is allowed to touch. Knowing it about everyone sits in the mind like a statistic, true and inert. Knowing it about yourself reaches into what you do on an ordinary afternoon. It changes the order of things you care about, sometimes in a single phone call.

Most people, most of the time, live in the first kind. That is close to a condition of getting through a day. Nobody can plan a career, cook dinner and pay a mortgage with the second kind fully switched on. So nearly everyone switches it off, and calls that sanity, which it partly is. The king at the start of the epic is the extreme case of that ordinary state.

Does the text earn that? The poem never says, in so many words, that Gilgamesh thinks he will live forever. What it shows is a man who acts as if no limit applies to him. The evidence that he knows about death in general comes later, in his own mouth. Before his first great adventure, he will say out loud that people's days are numbered and that only the gods last. He knows the fact perfectly well. It simply has not been allowed near him. That is knowing it about everyone, in its purest form. A man can state the truth about mortality in a speech, and then go off to make himself famous with it.

There is a serious case against reading the poem this way. It says that this is an ancient hero tale, and that ancient heroes do not have inner lives. Gilgamesh is a type, the mighty king, and nothing inside him changes. Reading a change of knowledge into him is modern psychology laid over an old adventure story. He fights, he travels, he comes home, and the poem's interest is in the deeds.

The answer is in the frame, and it is strong. The poem itself says, before the first deed, what its interest is. Its first word for him is the one who saw, where strong or royal or great might have been expected. Its summary of his life is not a list of conquests but a road that made him weary and then gave him rest. And its instruction to the reader is to read out his misfortunes. A poem that wanted only deeds would open with deeds. This one opens with knowledge, cost and a written record, which is to say with the inner change the objection says is not there. The deeds are real, and the poem enjoys them, but they are told inside a frame that tells you they are the road to something else.

A second objection is smaller but worth settling. If he is two-thirds god, perhaps the problem of death is not quite his. Perhaps a part-divine king is a special case, and the epic is not about ordinary mortality at all.

The answer is that the epic uses the fraction to make exactly the opposite point. Two-thirds god buys him strength, beauty and a long reign. It does not buy him one day of life beyond his end. The third of him that is human decides everything that matters. A man who is mostly divine and still has to die is the clearest possible demonstration that nothing short of being wholly a god is enough. The fraction is there to close the loophole, not to open it. If being two-thirds god does not save you, being rich, being famous or being loved will not save you either.

So the epic does something careful in its first tablet. It sets the frame: a man who saw something, at a cost, and left a written account for others. Then it shows the starting point: a king with no limit, who knows the general fact of death and lives as if it were somebody else's. And it supplies the first limit, which is not death but a person, made from clay to be his equal.

There is one more thing in the prologue that will matter later, so it is worth placing now. Among the things the poem says Gilgamesh did is that he restored "the cult-centres that the Deluge destroyed". The Deluge is the great flood. The prologue also says, in passing, that he reached a man far away who survived that flood.20 Nobody hearing it yet knows why that matters. It will turn out that this is the only man who ever escaped death, and that Gilgamesh's long road ends at his door. The prologue has already told you where the road goes, the way you mention a town on a map.

What has been established is this. There are two ways of knowing that people die, and they are different in what they touch, not in what they contain. The king begins with the first and none of the second. The frame of the poem promises that by the end he will have paid for something and left it written down. The gods' first move is to give him an equal, and an equal is the one thing that could make death his own business. So the next question is what that equal is, and why the epic spends so long making him human before it lets anything happen to him.

Part 2: The Man From the Grass

Before the epic shows anyone die, it builds the thing that death will take. It takes a creature who has nobody, makes him human, and gives him a friend. Every step of that making is also a step toward being able to lose something. The gift and the exposure arrive together, often in the same line, and the poem is careful to show it.

Enkidu begins in the wild, made from a pinch of clay. He is covered in hair, he does not know people or any country, and he lives with the herds. The poem gives one picture of him that sums up the whole life: he is "feeding on grass with the very gazelles". He drinks at the water-holes with the animals, and he is happy there.21 He also frees animals from the traps that people set, which is how he first comes into the story at all.

So the creature made to stop Gilgamesh becomes the person he loves most. And the king, who had no equal, now has one. The people of Uruk are rested, as the gods intended, because the king's force now has somewhere to go that is not into them. But something else has happened that nobody asked for. For the first time in his life, Gilgamesh has someone particular.

To see why that matters for death, compare two lives that differ on that one point. Enkidu among the gazelles has company all the time. He eats with them, drinks with them, runs with them, and he is never alone. But the herd is a herd. If a lion takes one gazelle, the others drink at the same water-hole the next morning. Nothing in the poem suggests that Enkidu's life among them was built around any one of them.

Enkidu after Shamhat, and after the fight in the doorway, also has company. But now one person in the world is not interchangeable with any other. There is a face that, if it were gone, would leave a gap the shape of that face. Nothing else in his life has changed on this point. He was fed before and he is fed now. He had companions before and he has them now. The difference is that now he has someone.

Call that a bond: one person who cannot be replaced by any other. The herd gave Enkidu company without a bond. The friendship gives each of the two men a bond. With it comes, for the first time, a death that could happen to them without happening to their own body.

Run the same thing on a barer case, with the epic's particulars removed. A person who has nobody can still fear their own death. But there is a whole second kind of loss they are safe from, which is watching someone else's. The day they come to love one person in particular, they become exposed to it. It cannot be otherwise, because what makes the person precious is exactly what makes their death a wound. The bread, the herd and the doorway are replaceable details. What is not replaceable is that the capacity to have someone and the capacity to lose someone are one capacity, seen from two ends.

That is the principle, and it is the case of Enkidu with the details stripped off. The epic builds it in the order that a person lives it. First the understanding, then the wish for a friend, then the friend. And it has not yet shown a single death.

The obvious objection is that this reads too much into a story about civilisation. On this view, the Enkidu tablets are about the city against the wild. They show a savage tamed by sex, bread and clothes, they celebrate what cities give, and they have nothing to do with mortality. The friendship is a plot device to send two heroes on adventures together.

There is truth in that, and it should be kept. The poem does celebrate the city, and it does enjoy the taming. But the objection misses how the poem itself uses these scenes later. When Enkidu lies dying, he curses the woman who brought him out of the wild, and the answer he gets goes straight back to these scenes. The poem returns to the civilising at the moment of death, and reads it as the goods of a life that is ending. So the civilisation reading is right about what these scenes contain and wrong about what they are for. They are the inventory of what death will be measured against.

A second objection is quieter. Perhaps Enkidu was better off with the gazelles. He had no fear and no grief, and nobody he could lose. The poem itself shows that the wild life was easy and the human life will end in suffering. If attachment is the price of loss, the sensible choice would seem to be never to pay it.

That objection is exactly the one Enkidu will raise himself, in a fever, near the end of his life. He will wish he had never left the herd. So the epic does not dismiss it. It gives it to the character with the most right to make it, and then it answers it, at his deathbed. For now it is enough to notice that the objection is real, and that the epic knows it is real.

What has been established is this. Becoming human, in this poem, means gaining understanding and losing the easy life of the herd, and the two arrive in one line. The first thing understanding wants is another person, and it gets one, in a doorway, after a fight. From that moment both men have a bond, and with it a second way to be harmed by death, through someone else's. Gilgamesh now has someone to lose. The question is what he does with death while it still belongs to other people. His first answer is the oldest one there is.

Those few lines are an argument, and like any argument they can be laid out as steps. One. Only the gods live forever. Two. A man's days are numbered, and whatever he does blows away like wind. Three. But a name can outlast the man who made it. So, four. The sensible response to death is to risk your life for a name, the one thing you do that is not wind.

That is a real argument, and a strong one. The first two steps are true, and the epic will spend its whole second half confirming them. The third step is also true, as far as it goes. Names do outlast men, and this one was copied for well over a thousand years and is read again today. If any argument about death could be called common sense, it is this one. It is also the answer behind a great deal of modern advice, which tells you to leave a legacy, build something that lasts, and be remembered.

Now listen to who is afraid in that speech. Gilgamesh states the whole truth about mortality, in four lines, and then accuses Enkidu of fearing death. The man who has just said that every human life is wind is not frightened at all. He is excited. Mortality, for him, is a premise in an argument for doing what he already wanted to do. At this point a sceptic has a powerful reply. It says that the first answer did not fail at all. Gilgamesh wanted a name that lasts, and he got one. His name was copied on clay for well over a thousand years, and it is still read today. Whatever else is true, the plan in that speech succeeded beyond anything he could have hoped. The epic itself is proof that a name can outlast death.

All of that is true, and the epic never denies it. It never shows the name fading. What it shows is something different, and it shows it twice. First, a name can be taken. When Enkidu is dying, he will say of the great cedar door he made that some later king will put his own name on it. A name lives in other people's hands, and other people can change it.

Second, and this is the deeper point, a name that lasts does not answer the question the fear is asking. Lay out the argument again and the gap shows. The fear says: I will die. The answer says: your name will not. For that to be comfort, the thing that fears and the thing that lasts would have to be the same thing, and they are not. Gilgamesh the name has outlived him by thousands of years. Gilgamesh the man got nothing out of any of those years, because he was not in them.

So the ruling on the objection is this. Fame works perfectly as an answer to the question, will something of me last? The answer is yes, sometimes, for a while. But fame cannot answer the question, will I last, and that is the only question being asked by someone who is afraid. Step three of the argument is true, and the step from three to four quietly swaps one question for the other.

Why does nobody notice the swap? Because, while death is somebody else's, the two questions feel the same. If you have never really felt that you will end, then a name going on after you feels like you going on. The first answer is honest, and it only fits the first kind of knowing. It is built for knowing it about everyone, and it gives way the moment someone knows it about himself.

Two cases make the point sharp. A man in good health writes his will, picks the charity that will carry his name, and feels pleased and calm. The same man, years later, in a hospital bed, is told he has weeks. Nothing about the will has changed. The charity will still carry his name. But he does not lie in that bed thinking of the plaque. He thinks about his daughter's face, about one more summer, about whether it will hurt. The legacy is exactly as real as it was, and it has become irrelevant to the thing he feels.

That is the shape of the first answer's failure, and the epic is about to act it out. So far it has established that fame is a real good, that it can be won, and that it can last a very long time. It has also shown that fame is bought with other people's deaths. And it feels like an answer only to someone for whom death is still general. So the question is what happens when death stops being general. The epic's answer starts with a dream, in which the gods sit in council and decide that one of the two friends must die.

So what changed? Not his situation. What changed is which way he was reading his life. The curse read it backwards from the deathbed, so that every good thing was only a step toward the end. Shamash makes him read it forwards, item by item, as it was lived. Read forwards, the bread was bread, eaten and enjoyed, and the friendship was a friendship, real for every day it lasted.

Put as an argument, it runs like this. The curse says the end makes the goods worthless. But the goods happened. The bread was eaten, the drink was drunk and the friend was had. The end can stop more goods from happening, and that is a real loss. But it cannot reach back and make the past goods not have happened. So the end is a loss of future goods, and it is not a cancellation of past ones. The life is not made bad by its ending. It is made shorter.

There is a hard case against this, and it should be tested. Some endings really do seem to change what came before. A woman learns, after thirty years, that her husband deceived her the whole time. The marriage she remembers as happy now looks like something else, and it feels as if the ending reached back and spoiled it.

But consider what did the spoiling. It was not the ending as such. It was new information about the past, the discovery that the goods she thought she had were not what she thought. Death brings no information of that kind. Enkidu's bread was real bread, his friend a real friend, and nothing about the fever shows otherwise. An ending can expose a past that was false. It cannot falsify a past that was true, and that is the only kind of reaching back the curse needed.

Why did this death do it, when the epic is full of deaths? Gilgamesh has seen men die. He killed Humbaba with his own hands and watched him beg. None of that turned the general fact into his own. The answer is in the very first thing the gods said about Enkidu. They made him as a match for Gilgamesh, his equal, his double. So when Gilgamesh asks, "shall I not then be like Enkidu?", the question has already been answered by the way Enkidu was made. That is the mechanism, laid bare. You can watch strangers die and keep death general, because you are not like them in any way that counts. But a death that happens to someone you have made part of yourself happens, in part, to you. The bond from the man from the grass is the road the knowledge travels on. The same tie that made Enkidu precious makes his death a demonstration of your own.

Thousands of years later, a Russian novelist found the same seam and described it from the other side.85 In Tolstoy's story The Death of Ivan Ilych, a judge falls ill and slowly understands that he is dying. Long ago he had learned a textbook syllogism: "Caius is a man, men are mortal, therefore Caius is mortal". Tolstoy writes that the syllogism "had always seemed to him correct as applied to Caius, but certainly not as applied to himself".86 Caius was a man in general, and it was right for him to die. For himself, Ivan thinks, "with all my thoughts and emotions, it's altogether a different matter".87

The parallel maps on one point and breaks on another, and both are worth seeing. It maps on the gap itself. Ivan, like the young Gilgamesh, could state the general truth perfectly and never once applied it to himself. The syllogism is the name speech in a classroom. Where it breaks is the road the knowledge takes. Ivan learns his death from his own body, from a pain in his side. Gilgamesh learns his from his friend's body. And that difference is the epic's own contribution. The first real lesson in your own death can come through someone else's, by way of the person you are most like.

Run the case once more with the epic's details stripped away. The particulars, the maggot, the seven days, the friend made of clay, can all be replaced. A woman sorts her father's things after the funeral and finds his reading glasses folded on a book, a page marked halfway through. She knew he was dead. She was at the funeral. But the half-read book does what the funeral could not, because it is a plain physical fact that cannot be argued with. He will not finish it. And then, nearly always, the second thought arrives, which is that one day there will be a half-read book of hers.

Said generally, then. Knowing it about everyone becomes knowing it about yourself through someone you are bound to. Their death reaches you as a fact too plain to keep at a distance. That is the maggot, and it is the reading glasses, and they are the same thing with different details.

The obvious objection is that this is psychology, not philosophy. Grief is grief, and every culture has stories of people who would not let the dead go. On this view it has nothing to do with an argument about death.

The answer is that everything that follows in the epic depends on it, and the first answer to death has just been undone by it. Remember the gap in the name speech. Fame answered the question, will something of me last, and it felt like an answer only while death stayed general. Now death is no longer general. The statue of gold is standing, finished and beautiful, and Gilgamesh walks away from it into the wild. He does not go looking for a bigger statue. That is the first answer refuted by the man who made it, not with an argument, but with his feet.

That completes the case against the first answer. Fame answered a question that only someone who knows death about everyone would ask. And the greatest name-maker in the story has just walked out on his own monument.

There is one more thing in the tablet's opening line worth hearing. "I became afraid of death, so go roaming the wild". The fear is his reason for leaving home. Gilgamesh does not respond to his own death by sitting still with it. He responds by running at it, looking for a way out, and the wild he runs into is the place Enkidu came from.

So, in sum. The turn from knowing death about everyone to knowing it about yourself is made by a plain fact about someone you are bound to. For Gilgamesh, the fact was a maggot on his double's face, and it undid the first answer at a stroke. He has stopped asking whether his name will last and started asking whether he can. The next question is what a man does once he knows. His first move is to refuse. The refusal takes the shape of a long journey that turns him into the very thing his friend once was.

Part 6: The Advice at the Edge of the World

The second answer to death is the one most sensible people give today: enjoy the days you have, because they are all there is. The epic puts this answer in the mouth of a woman who keeps a tavern at the edge of the world, and it is true. Gilgamesh refuses it, and he is right to refuse it as an answer to his fear, and wrong to refuse the good things it points to. The poem itself passes no verdict on her speech, so that verdict is a reading, and the rest of the story is its evidence.

It is tempting to say he is simply wrong. On this view grief has made him deaf to good sense, and the tavern-keeper is the voice of the poem. Read on its own, this passage looks exactly like the moral of the epic. If that reading were right, everything after this scene would be a mistake, so it has to be answered on its merits.

Begin with two cases. A man is told that the factory where he has worked for twenty years will close in a year. He goes to a friend and asks how to stop the closure. The friend says: enjoy the year, spend it with the people you like there, do good work while you can. That advice may be excellent. But it does not answer his question. He asked how to stop the closure, and he was told how to spend the time before it.

A second man is told the same news. He asks his friend what he should do with this year, given that the factory is closing. The friend gives the same advice, word for word. This time it is an answer, and a good one. Nothing about the advice has changed. What changed is the question it was given to.

Those two cases mark the difference between two questions. One asks how not to lose a thing. The other asks how to live, given that you will lose it. The tavern-keeper's advice is a superb answer to the second question, and Gilgamesh, standing at her door in a lion skin, is asking the first.

That is why he is right to refuse it as an answer. He asked, can I escape death, and he was told how to spend the life that ends in it. It is the same swap the first answer made, a different question quietly put in place of the one he asked. Fame swapped will I last for will something of me last. Enjoyment swaps will I last for how should I live meanwhile. To a man who is still asking whether he can last, both answers sound like a change of subject.

But there is a deeper reason why enjoyment cannot answer the fear, and it is not about the question at all. The list contains food, celebration, clean clothes, a child's hand, a wife's embrace. Those are exactly the things death takes. The fear of death, in a man who has a child and a wife, is very largely the fear of losing those. So telling a frightened man to enjoy them more does not reduce what he fears to lose. It points straight at it.

Put plainly, the tavern-keeper says that since you will die, you should enjoy the goods of a human life. But those goods are what death takes, and a man afraid of death is afraid, among other things, of losing them. So the advice cannot remove his fear, because it directs him to the very things the fear is about. Enjoyment is what death takes, which is why it cannot be the answer to death.

And yet he is wrong to refuse the goods, and the poem shows it in his own body. He is starving, unwashed and dressed in skins. Every item on her list, clean clothes, washing, food, is something he has stopped doing. So the third answer fails at its first step. Gilgamesh came looking for a way out, on the reasoning that if one man escaped, there is a way. But the one man did not escape by a way. He escaped by an accident, inside a catastrophe, through a trick. The poem lets the reasoning fail by telling the truth about the only case there is.

There is a modern form of this answer, and it is worth hearing at full strength, because it turns the question around. It says that the real problem is not whether immortality can be had, but whether it would be any good. Live long enough, the argument runs, and you will have done everything your character cares about. You will have read every book you would want, loved in every way you are capable of, and seen every kind of day. A person who stays recognisably themselves forever would run out of reasons to go on, and endless life would end in endless boredom. On this view, death is not an enemy but the thing that keeps a life worth having.

There is a strong reply. Some goods do not wear out with repetition. A good meal is not spoiled by the memory of a thousand earlier meals. Nobody tires of a child's hand in theirs because they have held it before. A life built around goods like that, the reply says, could go on and on without going stale. And the reply has named a particular set of goods. They are the tavern-keeper's list: food, drink, washing, a child's hand, an embrace.

The epic does not settle this debate directly, but it shows something both sides miss. Its one immortal is not shown bored. He is shown removed. He lives at the mouth of the rivers, beyond the Waters of Death, where no one comes except a boatman. His city is gone, turned to clay in the flood. His people are gone. He has life and his wife and bread to bake, and he has no city and none of the people who lived in it.

What the epic shows is that the only endless life on offer is a life taken out of the human world. The goods that make a life worth living, the ones that do not wear out, are goods you have among other people. They need a city, a child and a table. This is a serious argument, and it does not rest on any belief about an afterlife. It is also behind one of the sensible beliefs about death, that fearing it is confusion, since nobody will be there to suffer it. It deserves an answer on its own terms, and the epic, centuries before it was made, supplies the case that answers it.

Now see the sleep through Epicurus's eyes. For six days Gilgamesh felt nothing. By the first step, nothing bad happened to him during those days, since there was nobody there to feel it. Whatever the loaves record, it is nothing to him.

And yet nobody, hearing the story, believes that. Gilgamesh lost six days. He did not suffer them, but he lost them. Whatever he could have done with them, said to his boatman, seen of the far shore, thought about his friend, he did not do. The loaves record days that were his to have, and that went by without him.

A second case, with the epic's details stripped off, makes the same point harder. A woman falls into a coma for a year. She feels nothing for the whole of it, not even boredom. While she is out, her daughter takes her first steps, says her first word and has her first birthday. When the woman wakes and is told, the question is whether she lost anything.

Nearly everyone says yes, and the reason cannot be that she suffered, because she did not. It is that there was a year of her daughter's life she would have had, and did not have. She was harmed without feeling the harm, and the harm is a gap between the life she got and the life she would otherwise have had.

Call that the harm of deprivation: being bad for you by taking away goods you would have had, whether or not you ever feel it. Once the coma case is on the table, the first step of the Epicurean argument is in trouble. Something can be bad for a person without their feeling it, if what it does is take away. And death is the purest case of taking away there is. It takes away everything a person would have had after it.

Now the strongest Epicurean comeback. In the coma case, the woman wakes up. There is a subject left to have been harmed. In death there is not. So it is hard to say who, exactly, is harmed by death, and when. Before it, nothing bad has happened yet, and after it there is nobody there to be harmed.

That is a real puzzle, and there is an answer that holds. The harm of death is not an event that happens to someone at a moment. It is a fact about a whole life, compared with the life it would have been. A person who died at forty instead of eighty had a shorter life than they would have had. That is true of them, the person who lived, whether or not anyone is around afterwards to feel it. The loaves are not death, since Gilgamesh wakes up and the dead do not. But they show the shape of the loss: days that belonged to a life, gone by with no one there to have them.

And the mirror argument fails at the same point, which a plain case shows. A man born in 1950 cannot sensibly wish he had been born in 1850. A person born then, to other parents, would not have been him. The years before his birth were never years his life could have filled. But he can sensibly wish he had lived to ninety instead of dying at sixty, because that person would have been him, with thirty more years. The time after death is different in exactly that way. It is time your life could have run on into, and the loaves are made of it. The two stretches of nothing are not mirror images, because only one of them is cut out of a life that was going on.

So the ruling on the oldest argument is this. Epicurus is right that you will not suffer being dead. He is wrong that death is therefore nothing to you. Death harms by deprivation, and a harm by deprivation does not need to be felt to be real. The sensible belief that fear of death is mostly confusion can now be split. It is right that there will be no pain in being dead. It is wrong that there is nothing to fear, because what there is to fear is the loss, and the loss is real.

The epic adds one thing that the philosophical debate tends to leave out, and it is in Gilgamesh's last line. He does not say: being dead will hurt. He says death is in his bedroom, and wherever he turns his face. The fear the loaves teach is the knowledge that the counting is already happening, that every night of ordinary sleep is a small loaf going stale. What frightens him is the counting, and a feeling has nothing to do with it.

One limit on this should be said plainly. Showing that death is a real loss does not, by itself, show that fear is the right response to it. A loss that is certain might call for grief, or for calm, as easily as for fear. What the argument shows is narrower: the fear is not a mistake about the facts.

That changes what an answer to the fear would have to look like. If the fear were confusion, the cure would be an argument, and Epicurus's argument would do. But the fear is accurate about its object. There is a real loss coming, and it is already being counted out in days. So no argument will make it go away, and the epic does not try to make it go away. The question it has left is narrower and harder. The loss is real, it is being counted, and it cannot be stopped. What is a person to do with the days that are not yet stale?

Gilgamesh does not ask that question yet. He sits in the immortal man's house with the row of loaves in front of him, and he has failed the test. Uta-napishti sends him away. But first he has the boatman wash him and throw away his lion skin, and dress him in a royal robe for the journey home. And then, as he is leaving, there is one more offer, smaller than immortality and smaller than the test. It is a way back to being young.

Hear the question he is asking. Not, how can I escape, and not, will my name last. For whom. He has just discovered that all his labour, the whole journey, the three hundred poles, the dive with stones on his feet, went to someone else. And he has to ask, for the first time, whom it was ever for. He means that nothing came to him, and that is a complaint. Taking the question outward, to whom the labour could be for, goes further than he does, and it is the reading the ending will support.

Now the answer itself, weighed properly. More time is the answer that modern medicine and the modern dream of life extension give, and it deserves a serious hearing. The argument of the loaves showed that death harms by taking away the goods a person would have had. If that is right, then more time is not a trivial good. Every extra year is a year of goods that death would otherwise have taken. A plant that turns an old man young is, on the epic's own reckoning, a real gift, and Uta-napishti gives it as one.

So the epic does not sneer at the plant, and neither should anyone hearing it. The years that medicine has added to human lives are years of children's hands and full tables. Those are the tavern-keeper's goods, and nothing here says they are worth less than they seem.

Even in the best case, the plant is a specific thing. Its name is not "the man who never dies". It is "the old man has grown young". It turns an old man into a young one, and a young man, as the immortal said, is still a reed in the reed bed. The snake that eats it sheds its skin and is young again, and snakes still die. Even if Gilgamesh had carried the plant home and eaten it, the clock would only have been set back, and it would have kept running. The end would have been moved, not removed.

That leaves the hard step, and it needs going slowly. The loaves showed that the fear of death is a fear of the counting, the days running out. But listen to what Gilgamesh actually weeps about at the pool. He does not only say he has lost some years. He says his labour went for nothing, to a snake, and that for himself he gained nothing. The fear of the counting turns out to have two halves.

One half is that the days will run out. That half has no cure, and more time only postpones it. The other half is that the days will have been spent for nothing, that when the counting stops, there will be nothing they were for. More time does not touch that half either, since a longer life spent for nothing is only a longer nothing. But that half, unlike the first, is a question that can be answered, and it is the question he has just asked: for whom. Enkidu's deathbed answered it for a life already lived, by counting what was real. A person with days still ahead is asking something else, what to spend them on, and that is where for whom comes in.

Two cases make the second half concrete. A man of eighty learns a new treatment will give him ten more years. He is overjoyed, and he should be. Then, a week later, he lies awake with the same question he had before the news. He wonders what all this is for, and whether it will have been enough. The ten years changed the date on the question and nothing else.

Now take someone with no new treatment, at forty, who has worked out what their days are for, who they are spending them on and why. They are not less mortal than the man of eighty. But the question that kept him awake has an answer for them, and the answer does not depend on how many years are left. The difference between the two is not the amount of time. It is whether the time has a purpose that reaches past the one who is spending it.

That is where the plant leaves Gilgamesh. He did not lose immortality at the pool. He never had it. He lost some years, and he is grief-stricken, and he is right to be. But in the same moment he asked for whom, and that question is worth more to him than the plant was. The plant only moved the end, and the question points at what to do before it.

There is a strong objection to all of this, and it should be heard. It says that the epic is being too clever about the plant. Suppose medicine could add ten thousand years, or renew the body indefinitely. Then the difference between more time and no end would vanish in practice. The counting would go on, but so slowly that it would never be felt. On that view, the plant was the right answer after all, and Gilgamesh was simply unlucky.

The reply has two parts. The first is that this is the immortal man's case again, by another route. Endless renewal is the life the epic has already examined in Uta-napishti. The cost it showed was removal, a life kept while the world it was for is lost. Anyone who outlives everyone they love, again and again, has the same problem in slow motion. The second is simpler. Whatever medicine might do, it has not done it, and the person lying awake tonight is lying awake now, with the years they actually have. The question of what those years are for does not wait for the technology.

So the last answer comes out like the others. More time is a real good, and the epic gives it as a gift, not a trick. But it only moves the end, and it leaves untouched the half of the fear that asks what the days are for. And the epic takes the plant away by accident, in the middle of a bath. No amount of time a person wins is ever safe from the ordinary world.

Gilgamesh gets up from the pool. He has failed at fame and refused the tavern-keeper's advice. He has been turned away by the immortal, failed the test of sleep, and lost the plant to a snake. Every answer to death he could find has closed. And he has asked, for the first time, for whom. He and the boatman go on, and they reach Uruk. What he does there, in the last lines of the epic, can be read as the answer to that question.

Part 11: What the Tablet Is For

Put together, the epic's answer to death is this. Death cannot be beaten, and every attempt to beat it fails at a point you can name. What survives is the fear split in two, will I last, which has only one answer, and what are the days for, which has many. That is the complete philosophy. It is worth laying out in one place, testing against what most people believe, and asking what it does to someone lying awake tonight.

Here is the whole argument as plain steps. One. There are two ways to know that people die: knowing it about everyone, and knowing it about yourself. They contain the same fact and differ in what the fact is allowed to touch. Two. The first answer, fame, says make a name that lasts. It answers the question, will something of me last, and it only feels like an answer while death is known about everyone. Three. The road from the first kind of knowing to the second runs through a bond. The capacity to have someone and the capacity to lose them are one capacity. The death of someone you are bound to is how your own death becomes real.

Four. An ending does not cancel a life. Read forwards, as it was lived, a life's goods were real when they happened. Death takes away what would have come next, and leaves what came before. Five. The second answer, enjoy the days you have, is a true list of the goods of a human life. It cannot answer the fear, because what it points at is exactly what death takes. Six. The third answer, escape, was never available. The only case of it was a one-time accident, and it cost the escaper the human world.

Seven. The oldest argument against fear, that death cannot hurt you because you will not be there, is wrong. Death harms by taking away, and a harm by taking away does not need to be felt. The fear is accurate about what it fears, which is the counting. Eight. The fourth answer, more time, is a real good, and it only moves the end. It leaves untouched the question of what the counted days are for. Nine. The fear has two halves: that the days will run out, and that they will have been for nothing. The first has no cure, and the second is answered by asking for whom. The epic answers it with a wall that shelters a city, and a written account of Gilgamesh's hardships, left for a stranger.

That is the epic, with the stories removed and the argument left standing. It is the maggot, the loaves and the snake said in general words, and the general words are the same claims the scenes were making.

Now test it against the sensible view of death from the very beginning, point by point, and say what is kept and what is thrown out. The first belief was: make a life that matters, and leave something behind, so that some part of you lasts. Keep the first half. Making something that lasts is exactly what the epic ends on. Throw out the second half, the so that. What you leave does not keep any part of you. It lasts for the people who come after, and the point of it is them. That one change of purpose is the difference between the hunt for a name and the wall.

The second belief was: enjoy the days you have, because they are all there is. Keep it as a description of what is good. The epic agrees, across its versions, and washes and dresses its hero in it before sending him home. Throw it out as a cure for the fear. It points at the very things you are afraid to lose, so it cannot make the fear go away. Anyone who says it should has misunderstood what the fear is about.

The third belief was: fear of death is mostly confusion, since when death arrives you will not be there to suffer it. Keep the fact inside it. There is no suffering in being dead. Throw out the conclusion. The fear is tracking a real loss, the days that will go on without you, and the loaves on the table show its shape.

The fourth belief was: medicine keeps adding years. Keep it, gratefully. Those years are real goods, and the epic gives its hero the plant as a gift. Throw it out as an answer. Ten years or a hundred move the date, and the question of what the years are for arrives on the new date unchanged.

So each sensible belief fails as a way out, and survives cut down to its true part. What is thrown away in every case is the same thing. It is the hope that any of them would make a person stop being someone who will die. That hope is the thing the epic exists to break.

The hardest objection to the whole philosophy goes like this. It says that this is resignation dressed up as wisdom. A man tries everything, fails at everything, and then calls his failure a lesson. Telling a frightened person that the answer is to make things for others is cold comfort. A woman dying in a hospital bed does not care about a city wall. And the cleverness about changing the question avoids the only question that matters to her. She wants to know whether she has to die, and the answer is yes.

Part of this is right, and it should be granted plainly. The epic is not comfort. It never says Gilgamesh stopped being afraid. It never promises anything after death except a house of dust. And the answer to whether she has to die is yes, and the epic says so without flinching.

But resignation is the wrong word for what happens at the end, and the difference can be stated exactly. Resignation is what you do when you stop acting because nothing will work. Gilgamesh does not stop acting. He comes home, stands on his wall and shows it to someone else. As the opening says, he sets down his labours on a stone, and his hardships are kept in a box for whoever opens it. What he stops doing is one particular thing. He stops spending his strength on keeping himself.

Look at what the wish to keep himself made him do while it was driving. Before he was afraid, it sent him to kill a guardian for a name. Once he was afraid, it made him sit with a corpse for seven days. It made him throw away his clothes and his city, smash the one safe way across the sea, and burn three hundred poles. Every action it produced was spent on the impossible task of keeping himself, and every one of them failed. The prologue's summary of that road is that he was weary.

And then it says he was granted rest. Rest does not mean the fear was cured. It means it stopped being in charge. The man on the wall at the end is still mortal, still knows it about himself, and perhaps still lies awake. But his strength is no longer going into the search. It is going into a city and a story, things that are for other people and that do not need him to last.

As for the woman in the hospital bed, the epic has one more thing to say to her, and it is not about walls. It is what Shamash said to Enkidu at his deathbed. Read the life forwards. The bread was eaten, the friend was had, the child's hand was held. None of it is cancelled by the ending. It is Enkidu's consolation, and it holds for the same reason it held for him.

Another objection comes from a different direction. Not everyone has a city or a child. A person who lives alone, with no family and no great work, might hear all this as one more thing they do not have.

The epic's own answer is in the box. The tablet is left for whoever finds it, a stranger, someone he will never meet. The handing on the epic praises does not need a family. It needs only that what a person has learned, or built, or mended, is put where someone else can use it. A nurse trains the nurse after her. A man writes down what he found out the hard way. A woman keeps a garden the whole street walks past. Each of them is standing on a wall and pointing.

There is one last turn in the argument, and it involves the listener. The prologue does not just say that the story was written down. It tells you to open the box and read it out. And in the whole epic, one thing turned knowing it about everyone into knowing it about yourself. It was watching death happen to someone you are like, your double, your equal.

So, on this reading, the epic is built to do to its reader what Enkidu's death did to Gilgamesh. It gives you a man made to be like you, the one-third human, and lets you watch him find out. It cannot give you the maggot. But it can bring the general fact a little closer to your own name than a newspaper ever could, without anyone having to die for it. That is what the misfortunes in the box are for. They are his toil handed on. Someone else can know a little sooner, and at less cost, what it cost him seven days beside a body to learn.

Which means that anyone who has lain awake after a funeral is in the position the epic was written for. The person they lost was their Enkidu, and the knowledge has already arrived. The epic cannot take it back, and it does not try. What it offers is the road Gilgamesh took after the knowledge arrived, with every dead end marked. The reader does not have to walk each one to find out where it stops.

So, finally, what has been established. Death cannot be beaten, outrun or explained away, and every answer that promises otherwise breaks at a point the epic names. The fear of death is accurate about its object, and no argument removes it. What changes is that the fear is split in two. Will I last has only one answer, and were the days for anything is answered by asking for whom. Gilgamesh's answers were a wall and a box, one for the people inside the city and one for strangers. The fear did not leave him at the end. It stopped running his life. And the poem that records that change is still being opened and read out by people who will die, four thousand years on.156
