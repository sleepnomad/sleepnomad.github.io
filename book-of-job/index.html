Cold open

A woman is standing in a hospital corridor at four in the morning. Her son is behind the door, and he is not going to live. A relative she loves takes her hand and says the sentence people say. Everything happens for a reason. She nods, because there is nothing else to do with her face. And something in her goes cold, and she cannot say why.

There is a book in the Bible written to say why. It is the Book of Job, and most people remember it wrong. They remember a patient man who lost everything, kept his faith, and got it all back. That is two chapters out of forty-two.1 In the other forty, the least patient man in scripture shouts at God.2 Beside him sit three decent friends telling him, at length and beautifully, that everything happens for a reason.3 At the end, God speaks. He does not tell Job the reason.4 He tells the friends they have not spoken the truth about him, and he tells them the angry man did.5

So the sentence in the corridor is the one sentence this book exists to refute, and it refutes it in God's own voice. That is the claim, and it is stronger than it sounds. The friends are not villains. They hold the view that most of the Bible holds6, that most religions hold, that karma holds, that the self-help shelf holds under other names. The world keeps accounts. Goodness is paid, wrongdoing is charged, and where a loss looks unearned there is a reason we cannot see yet.7 It is an orderly view and a hopeful one. Put one plainly innocent sufferer in front of it, and it has exactly two exits. Either he was not innocent, or the loss was useful. The book walks the friends through both doors and shows what is on the other side.

Part 1: The Man Who Had Everything

The Book of Job is usually read as a book about why the innocent suffer. Its first two chapters ask a different question, and the whole book is the working out of that one instead. The question is whether goodness can exist at all if it is always paid for. That claim needs proving, and the proof is in how the story is built.

The book insists on one thing before anything happens. The first sentence introduces a man in the land of Uz. The narrator calls him blameless and upright, a man who feared God and shunned evil.12 A narrator can be wrong, so the book does not leave it there. In heaven, God says the same words to the Adversary, and adds that there is no one like him on earth. Then, after the first round of losses, God says it a third time, word for word, blameless and upright. Three times, and twice in God's own mouth. This matters because the entire tradition of reading Job depends on it. Every reader who later finds a hidden fault in Job, some pride, some secret sin that explains what happens to him, is reading against the text. The book does not present Job's innocence as a claim Job makes. It presents it as a fact God states, and it does so three times so that nobody can miss it. Whatever happens next happens to a man who did nothing to earn it, and the book wants that settled before the first camel is stolen.

Heard as a sneer, that is easy to dismiss. Heard as an argument it is very hard to answer, and the book treats it as an argument. Here is its shape. Say the world works exactly as the pious say it works. The good are blessed, the wicked fall, and the accounts are settled in this life. Then the best man in the world is a very particular kind of man. He is a man who has always been paid for being good. You cannot tell, from the outside or from the inside, whether he loves God or loves the fence. A man who is honest because honesty pays is not dishonest. But nobody would call him honest either, in the sense that matters, and he could not know himself which one he was. So if the accounting picture of the world is true all the way down, then goodness is invisible. Not rare. Invisible, because it can never be told apart from good sense.

That is the trap the Adversary has set, and it does not catch Job. It catches the accounting picture itself. The only way to find out whether goodness for its own sake exists is to remove the pay and see what is left standing. The book is an experiment with exactly that design. A man whose goodness is not in question loses everything, and the book watches.

What is taken is worth hearing in the order the text gives it, because the order is doing work. Four messengers arrive, and each one interrupts the last. Raiders from the south attacked, carried off the oxen and the donkeys, and put the boys to the sword.17 Then God's fire fell from heaven and burned up the sheep and the shepherds. Then raiders from the east took the camels and killed the boys who kept them. Then a great wind came from the wilderness and struck the four corners of the house where his children were feasting. The messenger calls the fire God's fire19, and even if that is only the old word for lightning, the book lets it stand. That detail will matter later. Part 3: Why We Blame the Wounded

The friends' move from comfort to accusation belongs to no age and no religion. It is a default of the human mind when it is shown an innocent person suffering and cannot make it stop. The experiment was run in the middle of the nineteen sixties, and it is simple enough to describe in a minute. A young woman is brought into a room and told she is taking part in a study of learning.53 A group of other young women watch her through a screen. She is asked questions. Part 4: Job's Case Against God

Job puts God on trial more fully than anyone else in the Bible, and the book presents this as piety rather than sin. That runs against everything the word patience suggests, and it needs showing. The friends offered Job two exits from his situation, and he refuses both. He will not say he deserved it, and he will not say God had nothing to do with it. He holds both facts at once, his innocence and God's responsibility, and then he does the only thing a man can do who holds both. He sues.

Hear first how far he goes, because the sanitised memory of Job hides it. In his reply to Bildad he says that God destroys the blameless and the guilty alike, and that it is all one.76 When a scourge brings sudden death, he says, God mocks as the innocent fail.77 The earth is handed over to the wicked, and God covers the eyes of its judges.78 And then a sentence with a challenge in it. If it is not he, then who? Job is not describing an absent God, or a God whose ways are hidden. He is describing a God who is present, powerful, and unjust, and he is saying it to God's friends, in God's hearing, as an accusation. The friends said that God does not pervert justice. Job's answer is that he is looking at a perverted judgement, and that if God did not do it, nobody did.

Part 5: The Best Bad Answer

Suffering teaches. That is the most attractive version of the friends' view, and it is the version that survived. A fourth speaker in the Book of Job says it. It cannot be teaching Job's children. They are dead. Whatever the roof falling in was for, it was not for their moral growth, because they had none left to do. It cannot be teaching the shepherds burned with the sheep, or the boys put to the sword with the camels. They are dead too. The only person in the story with any understanding left to open is Job. So the theory has to say that ten young people and a household of servants died for a purpose. The purpose was to open the understanding of the one man who survived them. There is one reply left to it, and it should be named. The dead are not forgotten, the reply goes, because their making continues beyond death. That reply needs an afterlife to work, and it asks the dead to be repaid later for a price they did not agree to now. Without it, the theory forgets the dead. It looks at a catastrophe, finds the one survivor, and calls what he learns the point.

Take the particulars out and the shape is the same in the corridor. A mother is told she will grow from this. Perhaps she will. But if her growth is the reason it happened, then her son's life was the fee for her growth. Nobody in the world would accept that trade if it were offered in advance. Offer any parent the deal in plain words. Your child dies, and you become wiser and more compassionate. There is not a parent alive who would take it, and the theory says God took it for them. A lesson you would refuse to be taught, at a price you would never agree to pay, is not a lesson. It is a price. Calling it a lesson afterwards is only a way of making the survivor carry the bill as if it were a gift.

Here is the remarkable thing, and it is why this theory deserves respect even as it fails. The theologian who built the modern version saw exactly this, and said so. He asks the question himself. To further a supposed purpose of soul-making, need the world contain as much pain as it does. Need the programme include the more extreme forms of torture, whether inflicted by man or by disease. Need there be unendurable agony, protracted to the point where the sufferer is no longer recognisably human.129 And his answer, in his own words, is a frank appeal to the positive value of mystery. Such suffering, he writes, remains unjust and inexplicable, haphazard and cruelly excessive. It challenges faith with its utterly baffling, alien, destructive meaninglessness.130 That is the most careful defender of God in a century. He has arrived by his own road at the ash heap where Job is sitting, and conceded that the worst cases have no lesson in them.

Part 6: The Voice From the Storm

When God finally answers Job, he says neither of the two things everyone is waiting for. He never says Job deserved it, and he never says there was a hidden reason. What he says is that the world was never built as a court, and that Job's whole case assumed it was. That needs defending. The speech has been heard as everything from the highest poetry in the Bible to a bully shouting down a bereaved man. Both of those readings will get their turn. Which leaves the question of what Job says to a God who has answered him like this, and what God then says about it. Job answers in six verses, and then God gives the verdict of the whole book.

Part 7: Dust and Ashes

The Book of Job gives two verdicts at its end, and they are on two different questions. To the question why the innocent suffer, its answer is a refusal, and the reason is withheld with it. To the question how a suffering person should speak about God, its answer is one word. Truthfully. Even when the truth is an accusation, and even when the alternative is piety. Both halves come from the text's own last chapter.

Job's reply to the storm is six verses long, and the first thing to notice is what he concedes. I know that you can do everything, he says, that nothing you propose is impossible for you. I spoke without understanding, of things beyond me, which I did not know.158 Then the line that carries the chapter. I had heard you with my ears, but now I see you with my eyes.159 He concedes ignorance. He does not concede guilt. In six verses there is no confession of sin, no withdrawal of his claim to innocence, no admission that the friends were right about anything.160 What he takes back is his picture of the world, the court he demanded and the books he assumed were being kept. He had heard of God, from the tradition that gave the friends their theology. Now he has seen. What he has seen is the world of the storm speech, with the sea and the ostrich and the monster in it. A man who has seen that world cannot go on suing for a balanced account, because he has seen that there is no account. So he stops.

There is a philosopher who saw this more clearly than almost anyone. His reading is worth having on the table, because it turns the book's verdict into a rule. That is the whole book. Philosophers have had it in their hands for two thousand years. For most of that time they read it the way the friends would have wanted. In the last three centuries they tried to do better, and to do it properly, with arguments. What they built, and where it stands now, is the next thing to look at. The best of them, it turns out, arrived where the storm speech was already standing.

Part 9: What the Philosophers Built on the Ruins

For three centuries, professional philosophy fought the friends' battle again, on both sides, with better tools. The defenders re-ran the friends' speeches in formal dress, and the one strand still standing is the one that says least. That strand is the storm speech in a syllogism, and it has a price the storm speech does not pay.

The word for the whole enterprise was coined in 1710. Leibniz published a book with the word in its title, and the word stuck to the project.196 A theodicy is a justification of God, an account of why a good and all-powerful God permits the evil there is. The book's own answer is the friends' answer at cosmic scale. God, being supremely wise and infinitely good, cannot but have chosen the best. So this world, with everything in it, is the best of all possible worlds.197 Every evil in it is there because removing it would have made the whole worse. There is a reason for the roof falling in, even if no one on earth can see it. That is the friends' doctrine, translated into Latin and given a system.

And here is the answer that survives, the one this part has been walking toward. It was given in 1984 by Stephen Wykstra, and it is the storm speech in a syllogism. Wykstra asked what entitles anyone to say that something appears to be the case. You are entitled to say it appears that there is no reason for this suffering only on one condition. It must be reasonable to think that, if there were a reason, you would be able to see it.214 Then the comparison that carries the whole position. The good that would justify the fawn's suffering, if there is one, is a good purposed by the maker of everything, whose wisdom exceeds ours. By how much? He proposes, modestly, that it exceeds ours roughly as an adult's exceeds a one-month-old infant's. And an infant discerning most of its parents' purposes for the pains they allow it to suffer is, he says, not likely at all.215 So nobody is in a position to say the fawn's suffering appears pointless, because in this comparison we are the infant. We would not see the reason if there were one, so our not seeing it is not evidence of anything.

Part 10: What Would Actually Settle It

Every answer the philosophers gave was doing the same arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the reason none of them reached Job. The best of all possible worlds, the defence built on free choice, the fawn in the fire, the infant who cannot read the parent's mind. Each of them weighs an evil against a good somewhere in the whole, and asks whether the whole comes out ahead. That is a question about a total. It was never a question about the man on the ash heap. One position, set out in 1989, saw that and built on it. It names what Job got. And it comes with a price, and the price is where the book and the philosophy part company for the last time.

The book has already run the arithmetic once. At the end, God doubles Job's sheep and camels and gives him ten new children, and the world's ledger, taken as a total, comes out ahead. That scene was ruled on already: a total is not a repayment. The dead children are not in the total, and the total does not reach them. One philosopher, writing in 1989, took that exact distinction and made it the centre of the whole problem. An evil can be balanced off, she said, when some good somewhere outweighs it and the books close positive. That is what the doubled sheep do. Or an evil can be defeated, which is a different thing entirely. An evil is defeated when it is taken up into a whole that is better for containing it. A dark passage in a piece of music is like that. The rest of the piece does not cancel it; the rest of the piece is what it comes to mean. Balancing off is done on a sum. Defeat is done inside a life.219

Part 11: The Obscenity of Explanation

There is a position that says theodicy does not merely fail. It says the attempt is itself a wrong. To stand beside a suffering person and explain their suffering is the first immoral act, from which the others follow. The Book of Job states that position long before any philosopher did, and it holds it with an edge the modern versions lack. It bans the answer and not the question. And it insists that the question be put to God, out loud, in the second person.

The position is easy to caricature and deserves better, so it gets stated first. Put the friends' seven silent days beside their charge sheet, and put the laboratory beside both. Then draw the conclusion they point to. The moment you explain someone's pain, you have taken a side, and it is not theirs. You have said that their pain fits. That it belongs in the world for a reason, that the world is in order with it in. And the person in pain hears exactly that. They hear that the world is in order and their child is dead, and that these two things are compatible. And they hear that you are at peace with it. On this view, the wrong of theodicy lies in the act of making the arguments in the presence of the sufferer, whatever their strength. The act is a betrayal. Explanation is the comforter changing sides.

The most famous statement of this is in a novel rather than a work of philosophy. The philosopher of the burning fawn sends his readers to it in a footnote, so it is fair to follow him there.232 A young Russian intellectual sits in a tavern with his brother, who is a novice monk, and describes what is done to children.233 He has collected the cases from newspapers and books.234 Then he says what he will and will not accept. He does not deny God. He says so in as many words. It is not God that I do not accept, he tells his brother, only I most respectfully return him the ticket.235 The ticket is his admission to the harmony, the final state in which every tear will be explained and every sufferer will forgive. He does not want it. If the harmony costs the tears of one tortured child, then he hastens, as he puts it, to give back his entrance ticket.236 He would rather remain with his unavenged suffering and his unsatisfied indignation, even if he is wrong.237 The novel does not give him the last word, but the argument has stood on its own since.

Part 12: The Fence and the Fee

The Adversary's question was an empirical one, and nobody in the book noticed. Does Job fear God for nothing. Strip the sneer off it and what is left is a claim about what payment does to a good act. Read as logic, the wager says that if goodness is always paid, goodness cannot be told from good sense. That step is true, and the whole book stands on it. But it says less than the studies found. The step is about what can be seen. The Adversary's bet rests on what payment does to the thing itself, and for fifty years people have been measuring exactly that. What they found is that payment does not hide goodness. It converts it, and the conversion happens in people who never chose it and cannot feel it happening.

Start with the smallest case, because it is the cleanest. In 1973 a nursery school let some psychologists watch its children draw. The children were chosen because they already drew for pleasure, in free play, with nobody asking. Then, one at a time, they were invited to draw for a few minutes. A third of them were promised a certificate before they began, and they got it.243 A third were promised nothing and were handed the same certificate afterwards, as a surprise. A third were promised nothing and got nothing. Anyone would guess that the reward, however it arrived, would make the drawing dearer to the child. A week or two later the observers came back, put the pens out in free play, and counted. The children who had been promised the certificate and paid it now chose to draw about half as often as the others.244 The children who had been surprised with it drew as much as ever. So did the children who got nothing. The reward had not added to the drawing. It had bought it. A thing the child did for nothing had become a thing the child did for a certificate. When the certificate stopped, half the drawing went with it.

Put the three results together and the Adversary's argument is stronger than the book gave him. The wager, read as logic, makes goodness invisible in a paid world. The measurements say something worse. In a paid world goodness is spent. The certificate did not hide the drawing so that nobody could see it. It turned the drawing into a fee, and when the fee stopped there was less drawing than there had been. The fine cashed out the parents' obligation, and the cash did not turn back into obligation in the weeks anyone was counting. The accounting picture of the world, if it were true, would not merely make goodness unreadable. It would be a machine for turning goodness into commerce, one transaction at a time. And notice how it does it, because the shape is one the book has shown before. Nobody in the nursery decided to draw less. No parent decided to become a customer. The conversion happened underneath, in what the act had come to mean, without asking anyone. At the other end of the ash heap, the friends' minds did the same thing. They turned an innocent man into a debtor without their permission, because the belief in a fair world needed him to be one. What the measurements establish is narrower than a rule and enough for the argument. Payment is not neutral to the thing it pays for. The fence changes what grows inside it.

The measurements cannot show that he did not. They start with children who already loved drawing, and they say what a promise does to a goodness that exists; they cannot say whether one existed. What they can say is what the pay stopping should have done to Job if his goodness was a fee: thinned it. The friends offered him, for thirty chapters, the words a thinning goodness reaches for. He never said them. Which leaves the question the book actually set out to answer, and it was never Job's. Everything since chapter two has been evidence in the Adversary's trial, and the measurements say what the evidence is worth. The verdict is the last thing left to give.

Part 13: For Nothing

The Book of Job answers a question, and it is the Adversary's, not Job's. Does Job fear God for nothing.253 The answer is yes, and it is the only kind of evidence there could be. If goodness were always paid, nobody could ever know it was real. Job's unpaid goodness is the demonstration, and it cost what it cost.

So the Adversary loses, and it is worth being exact about what he loses. He said Job's goodness was the fence. The fence came down and the goodness stayed. It stayed in the form of a man who would not lie about God to save himself, and would not stop addressing him even in fury. That is goodness with the pay removed, and it is the only kind that can be seen, because it leaves no receipt. The test cannot show that his goodness was pure, since nobody can see all the way into a motive. What it shows is narrower and enough: whatever Job's goodness was, it was not for the fence. The book could not have shown even that any other way. A Job who kept his herds would show nothing, and a Job who cursed God would show the Adversary right.

A philosopher said the same thing in 1785 without mentioning Job, and it is the same thought. A good will, he wrote, is good not because of what it performs or effects, and not by its aptness for attaining some proposed end. It is good simply by virtue of the willing. Even if such a will achieved nothing at all, he said, it would still shine by its own light. Like a jewel, as something that has its full worth in itself.260 That is the Adversary's question, answered in the abstract. A goodness whose worth lay in what it obtained would be the fence. The jewel is the goodness that shines when the fence is gone. The philosopher stated the principle in a sentence. The Book of Job ran the experiment over forty chapters, on a man in ashes who would not lie.

One objection remains, and the whole argument has to survive it. Job did not know this. He was never told about the wager or the reason, and he died without learning that his suffering had proved anything to anyone. He was given God's presence, the one thing he demanded, and none of the words he demanded with it. So what good is a proof to the person who is the proof. The honest answer is that, as a proof, it did him no good at all. The book does not say Job's suffering was worth it to Job. It does not say it was worth it at all. It says God brought the evil, and it says the children stayed dead. It puts those two facts in the last chapter so they cannot be missed. The demonstration is real and the price was not agreed to. Both things are true, and the book refuses to make either of them smaller so that the other can be comfortable. There is a harder edge to this, and it should be named once. The book itself has a purpose served by the deaths, the wager, and that is the very shape the lesson theory failed on. The difference is that the book never offers the purpose to Job as a reason. It never says the purpose made the deaths worth it, and it names the price as unagreed. Whether that difference is enough is the honest doubt the book leaves in the reader's hands.

Now what a person carries out of this, because the book was written for the person in the corridor and not for the seminar. First, what to say beside the bed, and it is almost nothing. The friends were right for seven days and wrong the moment they explained.261 The bereaved parents said the same thing. A hand on the arm, a connection without words, the dead child's name spoken.262 Not a reason. Anyone who offers a reason has changed sides, whether they meant to or not. Second, what may be said to God, for anyone who has a God to say it to. Anything true. That this is unjust. That he did it. That the innocent are dying and the wicked are at their tables with grandchildren. The book puts every one of those sentences in the mouth of the man God calls his servant. It puts the pious explanations in the mouths of the men God sends to sacrifice for their lie. The anger a grieving believer is ashamed of is what the book puts in the mouth of the man God vindicates. Third, what never to accept, from anyone, in any dress. That you deserved it. That it was sent to teach you. That there is a reason you cannot see, and that your accusation therefore has no ground. The first is the friends. The second is Elihu, and soul-making theology whenever it is applied to a particular loss. The third is the infant argument, the most sophisticated philosophy of the last forty years. It survives every argument against it by making every accusation groundless, Job's included. The first two were tried in this book, in front of one innocent man, and failed, and God said so. The third the book answers with its verdict, which is the one thing that philosophy cannot absorb: God calling the accuser right.

The argument, then, as steps, so it can be carried. One. The accounting view says suffering is deserved or useful, and it is the view of most of scripture and most of common sense. Two. Faced with one plainly innocent sufferer it has two exits, invent the crime or call the loss a lesson. Both turn the comforter into a prosecutor. Three. The prosecution is the mind protecting its belief in a fair world, and it has been measured in people with no theology at all. Four. The honest alternative is Job's, to hold innocence and God's responsibility together and demand a hearing. Five. God grants the hearing, gives no reason, and shows a world that was never built as a court. Six. God then calls the accuser right and the defenders wrong, so that in this book truth-telling about God outranks piety about him. Seven. It was bought at a price nobody agreed to.

That last step is where the book leaves everyone who reads it honestly, and it does not soften it. The woman in the corridor was told that everything happens for a reason. The oldest and hardest book on the subject says that the people who told her that were not speaking the truth about God. It says God said so. It says the sentences she is ashamed of are the ones God called true when Job said them. It says the world was not built to pay her, which is different from saying her loss was owed. And it says that the part of her that stays good when the fence is gone is the only goodness there is evidence for. The evidence cost her son, and the book knew that. That is why it doubled the sheep and left the children at ten, and why it let the LORD's name stay on the deed.264 It is the most honest book in the Bible, and honesty is the one thing in it that God rewards.
