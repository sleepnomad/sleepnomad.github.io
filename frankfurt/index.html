Cold open

You chose your job, your phone, the shows you watch, the opinions you hold. Nobody makes you do any of it. And still there is a feeling, most nights, that your life is being run by something you cannot point at. When you try to say what is wrong, you sound ungrateful, or paranoid. So you stop trying, and you open the phone. The strange part is that you are not even unhappy, only never sure that any of it is yours.

That feeling has a shape, and a group of thinkers spent fifty years drawing it. Their answer is that being unfree today does not mean somebody forces you. It means you live inside a kind of reason that can only ask how to get a thing, and never what the thing is for. That kind of reason built the modern world, and it also builds your wants. So the freedom you feel is real, and you really do choose. And the choosing is the form the unfreedom takes. Everything you can choose between was cut to shape before you arrived. The question of whether to want any of it was taken out of the room.

If that is right, four things have to be true, and each of them can be tested. The tests have been run, and what they found is less than either side wants. First, wanting can be manufactured, in a person who believes they want freely. In Germany, in 1933, millions had voted, with enthusiasm and before anyone forced them, for the government that would destroy the country. The working class, with unions and parties and newspapers of its own, held every right opinion and watched. The watching is the puzzle, not the vote. Second, the manufacturing is done by no liar and no villain, but by an interest that never needed a plan. It is done by reason itself, the same reason that got us out of superstition, once it is cut down to one function. Third, it happens to you after work, not at work, which you already expect to belong to someone else. Your evenings are where the argument says you are made, and that is the claim the tests reach least. Fourth, your own test of freedom, "nobody forces me", is a test this arrangement passes without anyone having built it for that purpose. That is what makes the arrangement so hard to see from inside it. A system that gets what it needs from your own wants has no reason to reach for force. So the absence of force tells you nothing about whether your wants are yours.

Those four claims are what the parts that follow have to earn, one at a time. The men who made them disagreed with each other about almost everything else, including whether there is any way out. One of them said there is no right way to live in a wrong world. Another said the way out has been sitting inside every argument you have ever had. The argument between them is not settled by choosing a side. It is settled by testing both against the one machine the older of them never saw, the one in your hand.

Part 1: The Question They Were Built For

In 1933 a working class with its own unions, parties and newspapers watched a government come to power on a promise to destroy it. The working class did almost nothing. The Frankfurt School, a research institute in Frankfurt whose members called it the Institute, became what it is because of those two sentences. The puzzle is not the vote, whose mass base was the lower middle class. The silence. Fear and lies do not explain that silence. Something inside the person explains it, and that something was formed before the person held a single opinion. And it can be measured, badly at first and then better.

Start with what everyone expected, because the expectation is the problem. Marx had written that it is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness.1 Plainly: change the conditions and the minds follow. Put people in factories, take their tools and their land, pay them by the hour. They will come to see the arrangement for what it is, and end it. Marx's own theory asked for organisation and struggle to get from the first thing to the second, and never for a reflex. But the crude reading was the one everyone in 1930 was holding, and it is the crude reading that broke. Germany had done everything the theory asked. The factories were there. The crash had thrown millions out of work. The conditions had arrived, in full, on schedule. And the consciousness that was supposed to follow them did not come, then or later.

What actually happened is more awkward than the legend, and the school's own question gets sharper when you tell it straight. The unemployed workers did radicalise. They moved left, to the Communists, not to the Nazis.2 The Nazi vote was built from the lower middle class.3 And from 1930 the best predictor of who voted for the Nazis was not his job or his wage. It was whether he was Protestant or Catholic.4 So the workers did not choose the disaster. They watched it. They held the right opinions, and when the moment came the opinions turned out to weigh nothing. That is the puzzle a theory of conditions cannot touch. Between the conditions and the act there is a person, and the person did not do what the conditions said.

A young psychoanalyst there, Erich Fromm, had already gone looking inside that person before the collapse. In 1929 he sent a long questionnaire to German workers and clerks, and hundreds of them answered. The political questions were the easy part. The interesting questions were the private ones. How a child should be punished, whether women should work, what the man thought of his boss, what he did with his evenings. The idea was simple and, at the time, strange. A political opinion is a sentence a person says. A character is what the person will actually do when the sentence costs something. Fromm wanted to know how often the two matched. The method was crude: a questionnaire sent out to volunteers, with open answers coded by the team that had written the questions. No clean result from it can be quoted here. What the study was reaching for is the thing that matters. It was the first attempt to measure the gap between what a person believes and what a person is. It did not close that gap. A later study, in another country, measured the disposition, and measuring it is not the same as closing the gap.

With the German particulars removed, the question is this. A man says he wants the boss gone. The same man wants his son beaten for talking back, and wants his wife at home. He admires the foreman for being hard, and spends his Sunday obeying a marching band. Either the sentence is his politics, or the rest of his answers are. The school's answer is the founding move of everything that follows. The rest is the politics, and the sentence is a costume. When the costume was tested against the street in 1933, the street won.

That answer forced a change in what the school had to be. If the person is where the theory broke, then the person has to be studied. In 1930 one body of thought had been built to study how a person is made before they know it, and that was Freud's. So the Institute did a thing nobody had done. It put Marx and Freud in the same building. Marx to say what the conditions are. Freud to say how the conditions get inside a child, through the father, the punishment, the shame. There they become a character that will later vote, or not vote, or stay home. The family is where the economy is turned into a person. The father's authority at home is the boss's authority rehearsed, and the child learns to obey before it learns to vote. That is the school's founding assumption, and every part of this argument assumes it.

A historian of the period has a shorter answer, and it deserves stating at its full weight. The left was divided against itself, and its two halves spent more of their strength on each other than on the enemy. A general strike is hard to call when millions are already out of work and glad of any wage at all. And a state that arrests its opponents first does not have to persuade anyone of anything. All of that is true, and none of it needs a single word about character. That much is granted here. The school never claimed that character explains why the government came to power. The claim underneath is narrower and much harder to get rid of. It explains the distance between the opinion a person holds and what that person does when holding it starts to cost.

There is an objection here that anyone sensible raises. Character is a story. You can explain any outcome with it after the fact. If the workers had risen, character would have explained that too. A claim that fits every result is not a claim. The reply is a piece of history, and it decides whether any of this can be trusted, so it goes slowly.

After the war, in America, the Institute tried to turn the character idea into a number. They wrote a scale, a list of statements a person agrees or disagrees with. The statements were about obedience, toughness, sex, punishment, and the world being a dangerous place. The scale had one purpose, stated on its first page. To find the person whose makeup makes him particularly susceptible to antidemocratic propaganda.5 Before anyone was a Nazi, the theory said, you could find the person who was ready to be one. The scale was built and given to large numbers of Americans, and for a while it looked like the number the school had been missing.

Then it was caught. Every one of the statements was written so that agreeing meant authoritarian. Not one was phrased the other way round. So a person who simply tends to agree with whatever is put in front of them, a yes-sayer, scored as a fascist.6 The scale could not tell an authoritarian from someone polite. That is a real flaw, and for a while it looked like the whole idea died with it. What happened next is the part that answers the objection. A Canadian psychologist rebuilt the scale so that half the statements ran the other way. To score high you now had to disagree half the time.7 He gave the repaired version to people for decades. The finding held. The people who scored high were still the same people. They disliked so many kinds of outsider at once that he called them equal opportunity bigots.8

Compare that against what a story would have done. A story cannot be caught by a yes-sayer. A story does not have a flaw in its wording that a stranger can find, fix, and re-test. The character claim was stated precisely enough to be wrong, was found to be wrong in one specific way, and survived the repair. That is what a real claim looks like from the outside. Whether it is the whole explanation of 1933 is a separate question. The electoral record says it is not, because religion first and class after it did most of the predicting. What the character work explains is narrower and, for this argument, more important. The school took the gap to mean that the opinion sits in the sentence while the obedience sits in the person. On that reading, when the two disagree the person decides and the sentence loses. That reading goes a good deal further than the measurement underneath it.

What that test shows and what it does not show need separating here. The scale puts sentences in front of a person and counts which ones are agreed with, then counts which other sentences are agreed with. That is one set of agreed statements predicting another set of agreed statements. What survived the repair is that a stable disposition exists, and that it predicts hostility to outsiders. Whether it predicts what a person does when acting costs them something was never the measured result. The repaired scale kept the finding and did not need the Freudian story to hold it up. The disposition is real, and where it comes from is not carried by the numbers. So the Freud the school added to Marx stands as an argument and not as evidence.

There is a second objection, aimed not at the finding but at the whole way of working. It needs answering here, because it will be raised again against everything that follows. This is not science. You decided the workers should have revolted, and then you went looking for a reason they did not. Your theory is a politics wearing a lab coat.

Max Horkheimer, who ran the Institute, answered this by describing two kinds of thinking. The difference between them is the difference between this school and ordinary social science. Take two people looking at a fever. The first builds a better thermometer. The second asks why this society measures fevers so carefully and hunger not at all. The first takes the world as given: here are the facts, here is the instrument, make the instrument better. The second says the facts were made. The hospital, the thermometer, the decision that a temperature is worth a number and a hungry child is not. All of it was built by people, for reasons, and it could have been built otherwise. Horkheimer writes that the facts as they reach our senses are socially pre-formed twice over. The thing we look at is a product of history, and so is the eye that looks.9 The first kind of thinking takes what it is handed, and the second asks who handed it over.

So the second kind is biased. Horkheimer says yes, and then says the thing that turns the objection around. There is no theory of society, he writes, not even the theory of the generalising sociologist, that does not contain political interests. The generalising sociologist is the one who only counts and averages. The thermometer-builder has a politics too. His politics is that the hospital should keep running as it is, and he calls that having no politics. The difference is not that one side has an interest and the other does not. It is that one side names its interest and the other hides it inside the word neutral. That answer is a counter-charge, though, and a counter-charge is not a defence. That the thermometer-builder has a politics does nothing to show that the school's own claims can be checked, and a thermometer really can be checked. The school earned the right to be called testable in the scale, not in the essay. The scale was written down, caught out, repaired by a stranger, and tested again. The school is not exempt from the charge of bias, and never claimed to be. It claims the charge cuts both ways, and that a theory which admits its interest can at least be argued with.

The whole of it comes down to five steps. One: the theory said conditions make consciousness. Two: the conditions arrived and the consciousness did not. Three: so something between them, the person, has to be studied. Four: the person is made in the family before they hold an opinion, so Freud had to be added to Marx. Five: a stable disposition exists, it predicts hostility, and it survived being caught and repaired. What the measurements hold is this much: a disposition can be measured, and it is not the same thing as an opinion. That this disposition is what decides action, when acting costs something, is the school's hypothesis. Every part after this one assumes it on those terms and not as a result.

How a wanting gets made has not been reached at all. If a person's wanting is manufactured, and by nothing as crude as a lie, then something in ordinary modern life is doing the manufacturing. Fromm's answer was that the first suspect is the thing the person is proudest of. That thing is their freedom, and what a person is doing with it is the question the argument has to take up next.

Part 2: Freedom as a Burden

Fromm's answer to the question of how wanting gets made is that freedom itself does part of the work. It does that work not by being false, but by being unbearable in one specific way. Freedom from the old bonds arrives as loneliness long before it arrives as power, and people trade it back to feel held. The old trades were obvious: a leader, an enemy. The modern trade is quiet. You become what is expected of you, and you experience it as your own choice. That last clause is the whole trick, which Fromm found a way to show happening in a single room.

Fromm's demonstration begins with two men, five centuries apart, alike in every way but one. The first makes chairs in a town with a guild. He cannot leave his trade, cannot set his own price, cannot move to the next town and set up shop. His father made chairs and his son will. But if he makes good chairs, Fromm writes, he has done all that is needed to live safely at the level his position assigns him.12 The guild is built on mutual help.13 He has no freedom in our sense, and he also, which is the point, has company at every hour.14 The security is relative, and it depends on his staying exactly where he was put. Fromm is careful about that limit, and his care with it matters to the argument. A critic would attack the romance of the old town, and the argument does not need that romance.

Fromm's names for the two sides of that difference are freedom from and freedom to, and he says the two are not the same thing.16 Freedom from is the cut: the bond, the guild, the church, the father, removed. Freedom to is what a person can then actually do with a self that is now on its own. His historical claim is that the gap between the two of them has grown.17 A child shows the same shape in small, on a scale anyone can check. The child gets freer of the ties that held it back, and at the same moment freer of the world that made it feel safe.18 Every step out of the family is a gain and a loss in one movement. Modern history, on his account, is that step taken by whole populations, with no one to walk back to.

This is more than a mood, and the reason is in one line of Fromm's. His sentence is that to feel completely alone leads to mental disintegration, the way starvation leads to death.19 Not sadness. Disintegration. If that is true, then isolation is not a condition a person can wait out. It is a pressure that has to be discharged somewhere, the way hunger has to be. The plumbing in that image is Freud's, and nothing here depends on the plumbing, only on the pattern it describes. A pressure that has to go somewhere will go to whatever is offered. He blames both, the bonds that were cut and the market that made sure nothing grew back in their place.20 Capitalism, on his account, made the individual more alone and gave him a feeling of insignificance and powerlessness.

So what does a person do with a pressure they cannot bear and cannot name. Fromm's chapter on this describes three escapes from it. One: find someone to submit to, or someone to command, so that the self is fused to something larger and the loneliness stops. Two: destroy what is outside, since a threat you have removed can no longer make you feel small.21 Those two are the ones the 1930s ran on, and everyone can see them. The third is the one he says most normal people in a modern society actually take, and almost nobody sees, because it looks like health.

Fromm's name for the person who takes the third escape is the automaton. What he writes about that person runs like this. The individual ceases to be himself. He adopts entirely the kind of personality offered to him by cultural patterns.22 He therefore becomes exactly as all others are and as they expect him to be. The loneliness goes, because there is no longer a separate self to be lonely. The price is the self. And the reason the price is not felt is that the person is convinced the whole time that the adopted self is theirs, chosen, original. To break that conviction Fromm reached for a demonstration rather than an argument.

Part 3: Reason That Can Only Count

The kind of reason that got us out of superstition can only answer one question, how. It has no way to ask the other one, what for. And by succeeding completely it turns back into the thing it escaped. That is the centre of everything the school wrote, and the last of those three sentences sounds like a paradox. It is a mechanism with four steps. By the end of this part it should be visible in a hospital corridor.

Horkheimer and Adorno wrote the book that carries this claim, Dialectic of Enlightenment, in exile in California in 1944. Their stated purpose was to explain why humanity, instead of entering a truly human state, was sinking into a new kind of barbarism.33 Notice what the sentence assumes. The barbarism was no relapse into the past, but a product of the most educated, most scientific, most administered society on earth. The book's wager is that it came out of it because of those things, not despite them.

Start with two cases, and they are an illustration built for this argument, not a scene from the book. A priest in an old city kills a goat and reads its liver. The liver says the harvest will fail, so the city stores grain. Nobody asks the priest why a liver should know about rain. He is the one who reads; they are the ones who act. An analyst in a modern firm opens a dashboard. The number says the quarter will miss, so the firm cuts a department. Nobody in the meeting asks what the number is for, or whether a quarter is a thing worth being measured by. He is the one who reads; they are the ones who act. Everyone can feel the difference, and it is real. The dashboard works. The liver did not. What the two rooms share is easy to miss. In both, there is a power nobody in the room made, that nobody in the room questions, that hands down a verdict, and the room obeys. The first room calls it the gods, and the second calls it the numbers. Applied to those two rooms, the book's claim is that the second is the first with the names changed. The working dashboard is exactly what makes the likeness hard to see.

To see why, the word reason has to be split in two, and Horkheimer did the splitting. One kind of reason is concerned with means and ends, in his phrase. It asks whether a procedure fits a purpose that is more or less taken for granted.34 Call it the counting reason. It can tell you the fastest route, the cheapest supplier, the dose that kills the infection. It cannot tell you whether to go, whether to buy, or whether the patient's life is worth saving, because those are not questions about means. The other kind is older, so call it the old reason, the one that claimed to know the order of things. It held that the world had a structure of its own, and a life could be measured against it. The degree of reasonableness of a man's life, Horkheimer writes, could be determined according to its harmony with this totality.35 That kind of reason asked what for. It also underwrote the priest, and every tyranny that ever claimed the same knowledge. The enlightenment's achievement was to kill the old reason. Its cost is that only the counting reason was left, and counting cannot ask what the old reason asked. Horkheimer's sentence for the result is short. Having given up autonomy, reason has become an instrument.36

Those four steps are the hard part, where the claim gets earned. Step one: to master nature you have to be able to compare things. To compare unlike things you have to turn them into amounts of the same thing. A forest becomes so many tons of timber, and an hour of a nurse's attention becomes a unit of care. The book's sentence is that this society is ruled by equivalence: it makes dissimilar things comparable by reducing them to abstract quantities.37 Nothing sinister is intended by it, and you cannot build a bridge without it. Step two: once that is the standard, anything that will not become an amount looks like a defect. For enlightenment, they write, anything which does not conform to the standard of calculability and utility must be viewed with suspicion.38 Grief, a view from a window, a Sunday with nothing in it. None of those is a quantity of anything, so none of them can be defended in the room where the decisions are made. Step three, and this is where it turns: the person doing the mastering is also made of the stuff being mastered. He has a body that tires, wants that wander, a childhood that is still in him. To master the forest he has to master those too: sleep on schedule, want on schedule, treat his own hours as units. The book says humanity had to inflict terrible injuries on itself before the self was created. Something of this process, it adds, is repeated in every childhood.39 Step four: the self that survives that treatment is a thing that manages itself. And the book's darkest sentence is about what that costs: self-preservation, it says, destroys the very thing which is to be preserved.40 The living person was the point of all the mastering. What the managing self manages is that living body, so the preserving spends exactly what it was built to keep.

Part 7: Thinking That Only Says No

The ground that can only say no belongs to one man, and he spent his life defending it. Adorno's most quoted sentence is usually heard as despair, and the book it comes from opens by naming its subject. Minima Moralia opens with a dedication. The book relates, he writes there, to a realm which has counted, since time immemorial, as the authentic one of philosophy. The dedication mourns what became of that realm, and then it names the realm.120 The teaching of the good life. A man who wrote a whole book about the good life did not think there was nothing to say about it. He thought there was only one honest way left to say it. His position comes in two halves. You do not need a picture of the right life to know a wrong one. And any picture you draw is drawn in the wrong life's colours, so the honest thinker refuses to draw it and says no instead. Whether saying no is enough is what this part has to rule on.

Part 8: The Rescue: Reason That Talks

Habermas's claim is that the first generation made one mistake, and that the mistake was about reason. They treated the counting kind as if it were the only kind. He says there is another, and it is neither the counting reason nor the old reason that claimed to know the order of things. It lives in the act of arguing, between people, and it carries a standard nobody can argue without using. Call it the reason that talks. If he is right, the machine from the third part is not all there is to reason. There is a ground to stand on, and Adorno was standing on it the whole time he said there was none. That is the rescue, and how far it reaches turns out to matter more than that it exists.

Two people who want different things from the same situation, and two ways of settling it. The first way: one of them has the money, or the strength, or the position, and the other gives in. The dispute ends, and nothing has been agreed. Habermas marks the difference himself. Agreement can indeed be objectively obtained by force. But what comes to pass manifestly through outside influence or the use of violence cannot count subjectively as agreement. The loser complies, and he does not agree, and he knows the difference, and so does the winner. The second way: one of them gives a reason. We should do it my way, she says, because the last time we did it yours, this happened. The moment that sentence is spoken, something has changed that neither person chose. She has invited the other to check the reason, and staked the outcome on whether the reason is good. She has, without saying so, agreed that if the other has a better one, the better one should win. Those two ways are the counting reason and the reason that talks. More exactly, they are two attitudes a person can take. One is aimed at success, and treats the other person as an obstacle or an instrument. The other is aimed at reaching understanding, and treats the other person as someone who can say yes or no to what you have said. Reaching understanding, he writes, is the inherent telos of human speech, and telos means the built-in purpose, the thing the activity is for.130 People do not always argue honestly, and the claim does not need them to. When they argue at all, they have already accepted a standard the counting reason does not contain.

Part 9: The Test: The Feed

The present is the best case the school ever had for its diagnosis, and the hardest for either cure. Adorno never saw the object in your hand, and Habermas wrote about it only late in life. Neither the diagnosis nor the rescue was built with it in view. That is what makes it a fair test, because nobody built it to prove a theory. It was built to hit a number.

Begin with what the machine is, without the mystique. Several billion people produce more than anyone could look at, and something has to decide what each person sees first. The something is a ranking system, trained on what people do. Did they stop, tap, share, reply, stay. Whatever they engaged with, show more of it, to them and to people like them. Other signals are added beside that one, including, on some platforms, surveys asking a sample of users what they wanted to have seen. How much each signal weighs is not published. The machine does not manufacture what you see, because the users make all of it themselves. Benjamin's hope that every reader would turn into a writer came true here, literally, and it is the business model. Habermas, in his nineties, said the same thing about the platforms in his own words. They create a space of communication in which readers, listeners and viewers can spontaneously assume the role of authors.147 The sorting still happens upstream, so the schema survived the very thing that was supposed to abolish it. The fourth part hung its mechanism on a broadcast schedule, and the feed has no schedule at all. The shape survives that, because the sorting is still done before you arrive, whichever hour you pick.

Part 10: What Is This For

The only practical thing the school has to offer is a habit, and it had better be stated exactly. The freedom left to you is the habit of asking, of any want or evening, the question the counting reason cannot ask. What is this for. It works, to the extent it works, because it is the one act that cannot be done for you. A machine can sort your world in advance, and it cannot ask on your behalf whether the sorting was worth having. Where that question has been given a slot, nobody outside can see what it weighs. Whatever the answer to what is this for turns out to be, it is not the thing a platform counts as engagement.

Advice is what the culture industry sells, and the school was never in that business. Fromm's test of a want asked not where it came from but whether it was the result of your own activity.159 Applied to the evening, the question is whether this want was the result of anything you did, or whether it arrived already sorted. Sometimes you look for who put the want there and find no hypnotist, only a shape that fitted.

Adorno's image for how this kind of seeing works is the mote, the splinter, in your own eye. It is the least despairing thing he wrote. The mote in your own eye is the best magnifying glass.160 That is an instruction and not a consolation, because the flaw is the instrument and you do not remove it before looking. There is a place where your own life does not fit the arrangement, where the want feels wrong or the reward feels empty. That place is the one point from which the arrangement is visible, because everywhere else it fits, and what fits cannot be seen. The child who became a name is the case in point. The part of him that never fitted the name was the one place the name could be seen from, and no teacher stood there.

Habermas adds the outward version of the same habit, and it is a demand rather than a question. Whenever something is done to you by a system, ask for the reason. The system will not give one to you, and a court can make it give one, slowly, over years. But the moment you ask, you have located the place where a claim should have been and was not. The ranking never said "this is worth your attention", and the target never said "this patient is better". When you ask them what for and get silence, the silence is the finding. You have found the seam, in his word. The line between the part of life that runs on agreement and the part that runs on a medium that needs none.161 Knowing where the seam is does not move it, and it does tell you what you are standing on. It tells you which of your evening's wants were reached by agreement and which by a number. The faculty that asks what for is the reason that talks, turned around on your own wants. It settles how an answer has to be reached, between people, and it supplies none. The debt stays half paid, and you carry the other half. The question goes to another person and comes back answered with reasons they can push against. That is why the habit is a conversation and not an hour alone with yourself. Planted wants come with their own reasons, so the test is never whether you have one. The test is whether your reason survives another person's questions. One that arrives without effort and collapses when pushed is the manuscript's.

Marcuse's contribution has to be handled with the most care, because it comes closest to a programme and the programme failed. He called for a great refusal, and his definition of it was the protest against that which is.162 That is a stance before it is a policy, and as a stance it survives everything the sixth part said. What the sixth part rejected was where his politics actually went, the withdrawal of tolerance from one side of the argument. Someone has to be appointed to say which side a movement is on, and that is the committee again. As a habit for one person the refusal is exactly right, and it is close to the same habit. Marcuse did not expect even that much to be safe. His own verdict on his own slogan sits in the same chapter, where he is describing the closing of the second dimension. The politics is left where Marcuse dropped it. What survives is the habit of refusing one thing you can name, on your own account, after asking what it was for.

The whole argument, once, in order, because a person should be able to carry it out of the room. One, the theory said conditions make consciousness, and the conditions arrived while the consciousness did not, so the person had to be studied. The school assumed the person is made in the family before they hold an opinion. Two, freedom from the old bonds arrives as loneliness, and the loneliness has to be discharged somewhere. The modern discharge is to become what is expected and to call it choice. Three, the reason that beat superstition can only ask how and never what for, so it turns everything into amounts, including the person. It turns back into the power it escaped, and the name for a thing that becomes its opposite by succeeding is dialectic. Four, the place this reason gets inside you is not work but leisure, where the sorting is done upstream and the product is you. That leisure trains the attention the work needs is the mechanism this argument holds as a hypothesis. Five, the machine does not decide, and whoever owns the machine decides, working with what people can be made to want. No villain and still an owner, an interest doing the work a plan would. Six, a society can be unfree while satisfying its members, and the loss is the ability to ask whether instead of which. Nobody can judge your wants for you, and a real rest from a want is what raises the flag. Seven, you do not need a picture of the right life to know a wrong one, and every picture so far has been sold back. So a standard of that kind can only say what is wrong, and it cannot say what right action would be. That leaves a person who has to act with nothing to act on, and the price was chosen knowingly. Eight, there is a reason that talks, alive in the act of arguing, and it carries a standard nobody can argue without. It binds people who can be asked to answer, it reaches a company through a court, and none of it reaches your evening. Habermas never claimed otherwise, and the route he named, law, stops short of the evening, and that is the whole of the complaint. Nine, the feed is the counting reason at its largest, tilting what its users see without anyone intending it. The question of what is worth a person's attention has a slot in there, and the weight of that slot is a secret. Four weeks away from it lowered how much people valued it, the valuation stayed large, and that is the flag. Ten, what is left is the question, asked by you, of your own wants, to someone who can answer. Nothing in the machine can ask it for you.

One objection has been left standing through all of this, and it deserves its own best words rather than a summary. Nothing in this argument can say what a free want would look like. Every test in it can cast suspicion on a want. Only one of them could clear a want: a rest that ends with the person wanting the thing more. In the study, nobody came back wanting more, so nothing was cleared. A liberal can answer that freedom is the absence of force, a door you can leave by, and a voice that can be heard. That is a standard a person can apply on a Tuesday, to a law or a workplace, and this argument has none. The reply is not a rescue, because the argument never claimed a standard of freedom in the first place. What it claimed is narrower and worse, that the liberal's standard is the one an administered life passes by design. No force is used, the door is open, the voice is heard, and the wants were shaped before any of that was tested. A feeling of being run is evidence against nothing in that standard, which is why it could be dismissed as ingratitude every time. So the word for the condition is the one Part 3 used for the society, administered. Unfree has been used here in that sense and no larger one. There is a larger concession to make in the same breath. That wanting is manufactured at all is the school's hypothesis, and it has never been a finding. The repaired scale, the feed experiment and the four weeks off the platform are consistent with it and confirm none of it. The man with the manuscript is an image of the claim and never was its proof. Without the hypothesis the rest of it still holds. The counting reason is real and measured, and the schema is real and measured. And the question what is this for is still the one thing none of the machines can ask. There is a residue after that, and it cannot be talked away. The feeling all this began with may not be the splinter at all. It may be what living among billions of other people's choices feels like from inside one life, and nothing here rules that out. What the argument can do is say what to do with the feeling either way, which is to ask the question and watch.

There is one more turn, the dialectic applied to everything said so far, and leaving it out would be cheating. Anything that can be wanted can be sold, and the wish to be free of the machine is a want, so it will be sold. It already is. The retreat, the programme, the app that limits the other apps, the book telling you to put down the phone, bought on the phone. The question what is this for can itself be turned into a product with a lock of hair on it. The person who buys it will feel they chose. Benjamin put the general form of it about history itself. There has never been a document of culture, which is not simultaneously one of barbarism.164 Every critique of the arrangement is printed and sold by the arrangement, so it carries the mark of what it condemns. That is what his sentence means here. The critique is made inside the arrangement, printed by its presses, sold in its shops, and this argument is not exempt. It is a product. You are engaging with it. Somewhere a number just went up.

So what is the defence, if the critique itself is for sale. There is only one and it is small, which is knowing that. The person who knows the wish to be free is being sold to them can still buy it, and often should. They can ask what it is for while paying. That is the difference between the man with the manuscript and a person who has heard this out. The second person may want exactly the same things as the first. The second person cannot see the join either, and knows there is one. The sign of it is a reason that cost nothing to find. Of the thinker who keeps doing this, Adorno wrote that he is in truth the one who does not give in. Not the one who wins, which is a different thing and was never on offer. One person can ask what tonight is for, tonight, and get an answer they did not have this morning. Nothing at the platform moves when they do, and that is the whole size of what is being offered.

Go back to the sentence at the start. You chose your job, your phone, the shows you watch, the opinions you hold. Nobody makes you do any of it. And still there is a feeling that your life is being run by something you cannot point at. You can point at it now. It is a way of thinking that only asks how, built into everything you own, including your wants. It runs most strongly in the hours you are sure are yours. The fourth thing claimed at the start was that "nobody forces me" is the exact test this arrangement passes. The evening and the feed show the shape of the arrangement passing that test. The tests reach the feed, and they barely reach the evening. The feeling was not ungrateful and it was not paranoid. Whether it was the mote or only the noise of living among billions of choices, do the same thing with it. Only you can start it, and you cannot finish it alone. What it cannot tell you is what to want instead. Nobody can, and everybody who says they can is selling something, and this is where the school stops and you go on. The question that was taken out of the room has been handed back. It is yours, and the only way to lose it again is to let something else ask it for you.
