Cold open

You have spent years getting better. Therapy, the gym, the books, the habits, the early mornings, and it worked, and you are better than you were. And some mornings you still wake up wanting something you cannot name. Getting the thing you thought it was does not touch it. The stranger part is that the better you get, the more you are the one who is getting better. Being that person all day is exhausting, and you have started to notice that you are watching yourself be good.

A thousand years ago, inside Islam, a line of people studied exactly that condition, on themselves, for a lifetime each. They are called Sufis, and what they left behind is not a mood or a poetry but an argument with a diagnosis in it.1 The one who is doing the improving is the illness. The self you experience as "me", the one that owns your body, your actions and your virtues, does not own any of it. It is a claim, and the claim is what stands between you and the thing you wake up wanting. Because the hunger is real, and it has one object, and that object is not among the things you can get. Every promotion, every person, every retreat was a substitute, which is why each one worked for a while and none of them held.

If that is right, the way out was never trying harder, because trying is the claim at work. The method the Sufis built does not kill the self. It sees through the claim in stages, by one practice that starves it and by one act in which the self steps aside on its own. The practice is remembrance and the act is love, and both get their full mechanism here. The test of arrival is not bliss. It is a person who keeps every duty they had before and acts without a rival inside.

Five things have to be true for that to hold, and each of them can be tested. The hunger must be pointing somewhere, or the right answer is to manage it and stop hoping. The "me" that collects your acts must be a habit and not the owner. Knowing that must change nothing on its own, or you would be cured by the end of this sentence. The path must have steps a person can actually take. And whoever is left at the end must still keep their promises, or this is dissolution sold as freedom. The Sufis argued every one of these, against each other and against their critics, and they did not always win. The argument breaks in two places, and both get their full weight here. One is the moment the intoxicated saints said too much. The other is the point where the whole structure asks for a God you may not believe in, and that one gets a ruling.

Part 1: The Hunger That No Object Ends

The wanting under all your wants has no object among the things you can get, and that is why it survives every success. You are not broken and the world is not withholding anything. The first thing to establish is what the wanting is tied to, since it is not tied to things, and the evidence is your own.

Three cases, and you have lived at least one of them. The promotion. For two years it was the thing, the one change that would settle whether you were doing well. It came, and there was a week, maybe a month, of the feeling you had been promised. Then one morning you were at the same desk with a new title and the same weather inside. The wanting had moved to the next rung without asking you. The person. You met someone and the hunger went quiet, completely, for a season, not managed and not distracted but gone. Then, without anything going wrong between you, it came back, and it came back inside the relationship. That felt like a betrayal of something, and you could not have said of what. The retreat. Ten days of silence with a phone in a locked box, and by the sixth day something opened that you had not felt since childhood. You came home and it held for a fortnight, and then the old weather returned, and now you want the retreat.

What the three have in common is not that they failed. Each one worked, and each one quieted the hunger for a time. Each time, the hunger came back to the same place and pointed at a new object. You could run the experiment a hundred more times, and you already know the result, because you have run it.

Here is what anyone would guess before looking. People who come into a great deal of money in one stroke should be much happier afterwards, and should stay that way. The one obstacle everyone names is gone. That has been asked, of people who had won large prizes, followed for years against people like them who had not. The winners were more satisfied with their lives afterwards, and the gain held for over a decade. It was also small.2 On happiness itself, the day-to-day feeling, the difference was smaller still, too small to be sure of. So the thing everyone is sure would end the wanting moves it a little, and leaves it where it was.

So here is the account most intelligent people hold, and here it is at its strongest. The wanting is a spur. It was built into us by a long history in which the animals that stayed hungry left more descendants than the animals that were content. It has no object, because it was never about any object. It is a pressure, and objects are only where the pressure happens to point. On this account the grown-up response is management. Lower the expectations, practise gratitude, and learn to sit with the pressure so that it does not run you. Stop expecting anything to end it, because nothing can. This is a serious position and a great deal of it is right. The wanting is real, and it is not cured by getting things. The person who keeps expecting the next thing to cure it is going to be disappointed on schedule.

But look at what the spur account has to explain, because the evidence is yours. The promotion bought a month and the person bought a season. And there was a third kind of moment, which bought no time at all and was worth all the rest while it lasted. That moment was not the retreat itself but something inside it. Inside the retreat, or the music, or the hour with the person, there came a stretch when you forgot yourself entirely. In that moment the hunger did not go quiet so much as vanish, because the one who does the wanting was not there. So the wanting is tied to one thing, and that thing is not any object but the self. When the self is present the hunger is present, whatever you have. When the self is absent the hunger is absent, whatever you lack. A promotion is about you, so it buys a month. A person takes you out of yourself for hours at a time, so it buys a season. The forgetting takes you out entirely, so for as long as it lasts there is nothing to want with. The spur account can say this too, and it says it well. The hunger is an alarm, and when nobody is home to hear it, it stops. The Sufi account says something different about the same fact, and the difference is the fork this argument ends on. On the Sufi account the self is not the one at home hearing an alarm; it is the wall between the hunger and what it wants. When it steps aside the hunger is not switched off but, for a moment, answered. Both accounts fit the evidence so far, and what separates them gets its ruling near the end. What can be settled here is smaller and firm. The hunger is not for things, it is tied to the self, and everything that follows works on either account until the ruling.

Here is the Sufi account in its own image, which is older than the study. The most famous poem of the tradition opens with a reed flute, and the flute is complaining. It was cut from a reed bed, and everything it sings is the sound of that cut. In the translation the scholars use, one line carries the whole idea. "Every one who is left far from his source wishes back the time when he was united with it".3 The poet is Rumi, and the argument returns to him, so keep the name. What the reed wants is the bed it was cut from, and no better bed would do. The song is the wanting, and the wanting is the memory of a joining. Nothing the reed can reach from where it is will ever be that bed. Read as a claim rather than a mood, the image says exactly what the Sufi account says. The hunger has an object, the object is not a thing, and the self is the distance from it. The image does not prove that. It gives the account a shape you can hold, and the shape is homesickness rather than appetite.

One more case, and it is the case that shows the hunger surviving knowledge. In the year 1095 one of the most famous scholars in the Islamic world was teaching in Baghdad, at the top of his profession. Then he stopped being able to speak.5 His name was al-Ghazali, and he wrote down what happened, years later, in his own words. For months, he says, he had been tossed about between worldly desires and the pull of what he calls eternal life. Then, he writes, "God caused my tongue to dry up so that I was prevented from lecturing".6 He could not swallow, and the doctors gave up.7 Their verdict, as he reports it, was that the trouble came from the heart. No treatment would work until whatever had come over the heart was allayed.8 Consider what this man knew. He had read the Sufis' books. He knew their teaching on the self, on the hunger and on the return better than almost anyone alive.9 He could have lectured on it that morning, if his tongue had let him, and knowing everything about the cure had not cured him. He says as much. What the Sufis had, he writes, "cannot be apprehended by study, but only by immediate experience".10 Hold that sentence, because a great deal turns on it later. It is also why hearing the diagnosis you are hearing now will change nothing by itself.

So here is what to keep of the management account and what to drop. Keep this: the lack is real, it is not a defect of character, and it is not fixed by getting things. Drop this: that its object is among the things you can get. The evidence, your own and the scholar's, says a third thing. The hunger is tied to the self, and the nearest you have come to its end is every moment in which you were not there.

What the three cases established. The wanting survives every success because every success is a substitute. It is tied to the self and not to any object: present when you are, absent when you are not. Whether that absence is an alarm switched off or a hunger answered is the fork, and it stays open until the end. And knowing all this does not end it, because the most learned man of his age knew it and lost his voice anyway. The next question is who is doing the wanting, and why it keeps reaching for the wrong things.

Part 2: The One Who Wants

The self you experience as "me" is a claim of ownership, not a fact about who you are. It claims your body, your actions and your virtues, and it is the claiming that hides the object of the hunger. That is the whole of this part, and it is going to be earned on two people giving away the same money.

Two people, the same street, the same stranger asking for help, the same sum handed over. The first one gives it and walks on, and by the next corner has half forgotten it. The second one gives it and walks on carrying something. A small warmth, a sense of having been the kind of person who gives. By the next corner the second one has told the story to themselves twice. Every outward fact is identical. The stranger cannot tell them apart, and a camera could not. The difference is in who owns the act afterwards. In the first case nobody does; the money moved and that was the end of it. In the second case someone stepped forward to collect, and what they collected was not the money but the giving, and they are still holding it. Give it a week and the camera can tell them apart after all, because the second one mentions it.

Run it once more with the particulars changed, and watch the structure hold. Two people apologise, in the same words, to the same friend. One apologises and it is over. The other apologises and is now the person who was big enough to apologise. They are a little pleased about it, and they notice the friend has not thanked them for it. Same words, same friend, and the second apology has an owner and the first does not. And the bare form. In every act you do, there is the act, and there is the one who steps forward afterwards to say that it was theirs. That one is not the act, and it is not the body that did the act, and it is not the friend or the stranger. It is a claimant, and it arrives late, and it collects.

The Qur'an has a name for that claimant, and the Sufis built their whole account of a person on it. In one verse a man who has just been cleared of a false charge refuses to take credit for his own innocence. In the translation used throughout here, he says: "I do not seek to free myself from blame, for indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil".11 The word rendered "soul" there is the word the Sufis mean by the self. The Arabic phrase is sharper than the translation: the self that commands to evil, and the Sufis shortened it to the commanding self.12 The self that gives orders. The one that steps forward to collect is the name used here for the same thing. The name is chosen because collecting is how it gives orders in a life like yours. Two other verses name two other conditions of the same thing. One swears "by the self-reproaching soul", the self that has begun to catch itself.13 One addresses, at the end of a life, "O tranquil soul! Return to your Lord, well pleased and well pleasing".14 The Sufis read those three as three states of one self and not three selves. Fairness requires one clause here. The verses are scattered, one inside a story and one inside an oath, and the ladder of three is the tradition's construction from them.15 The book itself sets out no such ladder. But it is the construction the argument runs on, and it earns its place, because it says the claimant is not fixed. It commands, then it reproaches, then it rests, and the whole path is the movement between those.

So here is the mechanism, which you already live inside. The commanding self does not only feed on vice. It feeds on virtue at least as well, and probably better, because virtue is a more respectable thing to own. The person who gave the money and is still holding the giving has not done a bad thing. They have done a good thing and let the claimant collect it, and now the good thing belongs to the self and enlarges it. Every improvement you have made in ten years worked the same way. You became more disciplined, and the claimant now owns discipline. You became kinder, and the claimant owns kindness. You learned to be humble about all of it, and the claimant owns the humility, which is the best possession of the lot. This is why ten years of getting better produce a bigger self and not a quieter one. The improver was collecting the whole time. You said it yourself: you are watching yourself be good. The seeing is not the problem. The one who steps forward when something good is seen, to say that it is theirs, is the problem.

The Sufis did not arrive at this by theory. They arrived at it by catching the claimant in the act, in themselves. The most famous catch is the one al-Ghazali made in the middle of his breakdown. He had been teaching religion, at the top of the profession, for years. Then he looked at why. "I examined my motive in my work of teaching", he writes, "and realized that it was not a pure desire for the things of God". What moved him instead, he says, was "the desire for an influential position and public recognition".16 Consider what that confession costs. The act was the best act available to him: teaching the truth. The claimant had taken it over completely, and he had not noticed for years. It had grown fat on the holiest work in the city. Nothing in his conduct would have shown it. Only the motive was wrong, and the motive is exactly the part the claimant runs.

Two words the rest of the argument turns on, one at a time. The Sufis say a person has a heart and has a self. They do not mean the organ, and they do not mean two creatures living inside you. Ghazali is careful about this. The word for self, he says, is also used for the whole person, heart included, and that is not the sense meant here.17 In the sense the Sufis fight, it means the part that "must be striven against and broken", the anger and the appetite.18 The claimant, the collector, is the name used here for that part, seen from the outside, in what it does. The heart is what knows. "It is the heart that knows God", he writes, "and works for God, and strives toward God, and draws near to Him".19 Put the theology aside for one minute and keep only the structure, which is two acts and not two people. There is seeing, and there is claiming, and you do both. So whenever "the heart" is said to want or choose or do something from here on, that is shorthand for you, acting without the claimant. You already tell the two acts apart, every day, without the names. When you say "I caught myself doing it again", the catching and the doing were two acts of one person, and you know which was which. The Sufis call the one who sees the heart and the one who claims the self. They mean by that a habit of yours and not a tenant.

Part 3: Nothing Is Real by Itself

Islam has one sentence at its centre, and it is short: there is no god but God. Most people, inside the faith and outside it, hear it as a headcount, one god rather than many. The Sufis hear it as a claim about being. Nothing exists by itself. Everything other than the one thing exists on loan, and a thing that exists on loan cannot own anything, including itself. If that is right, the self's claim of ownership is a mistake in principle, and not only in the cases where you happened to catch it.

The light in a room at night is where al-Ghazali starts. There is light on the floor. You ask where it comes from, and it comes from the wall, where a patch is lit. The wall is lit by a mirror hung opposite the window. The mirror is lit by the moon, and the moon by the sun. In his words, "the light upon the floor is owed to that upon the wall, and the light on the wall to that in the mirror".22 And so on up, the mirror to the moon and the moon to the sun. The only question that matters is this. Does the floor have any light of its own. It is lit. You can read by it. But every particle of the light on the floor is on loan from the wall. The wall borrowed it from the mirror, and the mirror from the moon. Take the sun away and there is nothing on the floor at all, and there never was anything that was the floor's. Here is the stripped version, with the particulars named as replaceable. A thing that shines by another's light is really shining. The shining is real, and you can use it. But the thing that shines made none of it, and that is the whole point. And the general form, which is the first two with the details removed. Whatever exists through another exists, really, but has nothing that is its own.

Part 4: Knowing It and Tasting It

There are two ways to know the same truth, and only one of them changes a person. The first is knowing by report, the way you know the boiling point of water. The second is knowing by taste, the way you know that you have been burned. The first cannot be turned into the second by adding more of the first, however much you add. That is why everything said so far has left you exactly where you were, and why it was always going to.

You have just heard that the self is a claim and that the claim is false in principle. You followed the argument. You may even have agreed with it. And this morning the claimant will get up with you, put on your clothes, and go to work, and nothing you heard will slow it down. That is no failure of the argument but its own prediction, and the tradition made the prediction first.

Two cases, and the difference between them is the whole point. A doctor knows what health is. He can define it, list its causes, name every remedy that restores it, and teach it to a room of students. Then he falls ill, and the knowledge sits inside him, complete and useless, while he lies in bed. Al-Ghazali uses exactly this case. The doctor, "when he is himself ill, knows the definition and causes of health and the remedies which restore it, and yet is lacking in health".40 Second case, his as well. There is a man who has studied drunkenness, who can tell you what it does to the body, and has never been drunk. And there is a drunk, who could not define it to save his life.41 Which of them knows drunkenness. Both do. And they do not know the same thing, and only one of them is staggering. Ghazali puts it as an exclamation, because by the time he wrote it he had lived on both sides. "What a difference there is between knowing the definition of health and satiety", he writes, "and being healthy and satisfied".42 The tradition's word for the second kind of knowing is a word for tasting.43 You taste a thing when it is in your mouth and not when it is on the menu.

The knowledge that was supposed to dissolve the claimant has become the claimant's favourite possession. It was handed to you, and the one who received it was the one it was about.

Taste is different in kind, not in degree, and the difference is who is present. When the doctor is healthy, there is no fact he possesses about health. There is just the walking and the appetite. When the drunk is drunk, he is not holding drunkenness; it is holding him. Taste is knowledge in which the knower is changed rather than enlarged. That is why it cannot be collected. There is no item to add to the shelf, because the shelf itself is what moved. Ghazali sat at the top of the profession that deals in knowledge by report. That is why his knowledge could not reach the thing that was wrong with him. What the Sufis had, in the sentence you were told to hold, could not be had by study, only by that experience. He did not lack a book. He lacked the taste, and no book would give it to him, because a book is exactly a report.

Here the philosopher stands up, and the objection is good, and the tradition felt its force before any modern critic did. Taste, the philosopher says, is a feeling, and feelings are unreliable. A feeling of certainty proves nothing except that you feel certain. The drunk is a perfect example: he knows drunkenness by taste and his judgement is worth nothing while he does. Private conviction has been the engine of every delusion in history, and each of the deluded would have told you it tasted like truth. And the deeper problem is privacy. When two people reason, their results can be compared and they converge. When two mystics taste, they diverge, and the tradition's own history is the proof, since its saints contradicted each other in public. So if the Sufi path ends in taste, it ends in a feeling. A feeling is not knowledge, and the whole edifice rests on nothing that can be checked.

Ghazali's answer comes in two parts, and it is worth taking slowly because he came to it by nearly losing his mind. Before the breakdown described earlier there was an earlier crisis, when he was young, and it was a crisis of exactly this kind. He asked what he could be certain of. He turned first on the senses and found they lie. The eye reports a star the size of a coin, and geometry proves it larger than the earth.44 So he fell back on reason, and then asked the question that broke him. What guarantees reason. Reason had exposed the senses; what would expose reason. He could find nothing, because any proof of reason's reliability would have to use reason, and would prove nothing. He says he was stuck there for two months, unable to trust anything.45 When the doubt lifted, it was not because he had found the proof. In his words, the recovery came "not by systematic demonstration or marshalled argument, but by a light which God most high cast into my breast".46 Here the philosopher pounces, and should. That is exactly the appeal to a private feeling that was just objected to. But look at what Ghazali does with it, because it is not what it sounds like. The light is what ended his doubt, and he does not offer it to anyone else as a proof. What he offers is a point about first principles, and it is a point the philosopher already accepts. "First principles are not sought", he writes, "since they are present and to hand".47 That is the whole answer. The philosopher trusts reason. The philosopher cannot prove reason, and has never been able to, and does not therefore stop trusting it. You cannot check that your eyes work without using your eyes, and you do not conclude that you are blind. Reason is trusted because it is what you are doing when you doubt it, and because it holds together and bears fruit. It is not trusted on a prior proof, since there is none. The Sufi says only this. The same standing you give to reason, on the same grounds, taste asks for. Not on a private feeling of certainty, which is worthless and which Ghazali knew was worthless. On the same two tests reason passes: whether it coheres, and what it produces. And the privacy objection gets the answer a trained palate gets. A chef's taste is private, and it is still trained, still checked against other cooks, and still wrong when the dish is bad. Tastes diverge, and the tradition's answer to the divergence was not to trust them but to build checks, which is the next part of the path. And the philosopher is right about one thing, which is kept for the ending. What taste reaches is a change in the self. It does not reach the claim about the world, that nothing is anything by itself, and that claim stays a claim.

Part 5: Stations You Earn, States You Are Given

The change the Sufis describe has a structure, and the structure has one rule at its centre. Some steps can be taken, and some can only happen. Confusing the two is the commonest error on the path, and the commonest error in your life as well. It is the reason your best efforts have produced the least.

Two people, and both of them want to feel grateful. The first decides to feel it. Every morning they sit down and try to be grateful, on schedule, and what arrives is a list. Health, family, a roof. They read the list and feel nothing, and then feel bad about feeling nothing, and the claimant collects that as well. The second person does not try to feel anything. They keep a practice of thanking. They say thank you to the person who made the coffee, and to the driver. At the end of the day they name three things that went right, and they feel nothing while doing it, for weeks. And one morning, walking to the car, gratitude arrives, uninvited, for no reason, so strong they have to stop. It stays for an hour and goes. Same goal. The first person aimed at it directly and got a list, and the second aimed at something else and was hit by the thing itself. That is the structure, not luck, and the Sufis named it a thousand years ago. It is also what "never try harder" meant at the very start: the trying that fails is the trying aimed at the feeling.

The two names, one at a time. A station is what the practice of thanking was. In one of the tradition's oldest handbooks, a station is what a person realises through their own discipline, by what they attain to.49 There is a rule attached: nobody moves to the next station without completing the one they are in.50 A station is earned, and once earned it is kept, the way a habit is kept. A state is what arrived in the car park. The same handbook defines it as a meaning that comes upon the heart with no intention on the person's part. No drawing it on, and no acquiring it.51 A second handbook from the same century says it more sharply. A state descends into a person's heart "without his being able to repel it when it comes, or to attract it when it goes".52 And the first handbook gives the rule in four words, which in English come out as: states are gifts, stations are earnings.53

The stations themselves are not a list of virtues. They are a sequence of subtractions, and each one takes away one thing the claimant had been holding. The handbooks list them in different orders and argue about the order, so the order here is the order the subtraction runs in.54 The first station is turning back, and in the tradition's own language the word means return. It is the first halting-place of everyone who sets out, and what it removes is the direction.55 Until now the hunger has been pointed outward, at the next thing. Turning back points it the other way, toward the source, and nothing else on the path is possible before that. The second is patience, and the definition given in the handbook is a physical one: to swallow bitterness without making a face.56 Another master in the same passage calls it standing with affliction with good manners. What patience removes is the demand that the hard thing stop.57 Not the hard thing, which stays. The demand. The third is trust, and its definition is the one that unsettles people. The first station of trust, one master says, is to be in God's hands like a corpse in the hands of the washer.58 What it removes is management, the constant background labour of arranging outcomes, which is most of what the claimant does all day. The fourth is contentment, and the handbook is careful to say what it is not. Contentment is not that you do not feel the blow. And the fifth is poverty, and it is not about being poor. Its definition is to need nothing except God. The handbook adds that the outward sign is having no means to lean on, and the inward one is the one that counts.61 What it removes is the last possession, which is the sense of having anything at all to fall back on. Direction, demand, management, objection, possession. Each station takes one of them away, and each one was something the claimant was holding. Part 7: The Candle and the Road

A path that drops the law produces the self in a saint's clothes. A law that has no path produces the self in a judge's clothes. For centuries the tradition's central argument was between people who had noticed one of those failures and not the other. It ended with a ruling, and the ruling is not clean, and both halves of that are told here. The ruling is the answer to the question left standing: what holds a person to right action once the claimant that feared punishment is gone.

Rumi gives the map in the preface to one of his books, and it has three words.95 The religious law, he writes, "is like a candle showing the way".96 Then: "when you have come on to the way, your wayfaring is the Path; and when you have reached the journey's end, that is the Truth".97 Law, path, truth. He compresses it to one line: "The Law is knowledge, the Path action, the Truth attainment unto God".98 The candle does not stop being needed when you are on the road. It is what shows the road. One of the eleventh-century handbooks makes the same point without the picture, and names the two ways of getting it wrong. Two parties err, its author writes.99 First "the formal theologians, who assert that there is no distinction" between law and truth. For them the rules are the whole thing and the path is superstition.100 Second, some "who hold that it is possible for one of these things to subsist without the other". These declare, he says, "that when the Truth is revealed the Law is abolished".101 His ruling: the law "cannot possibly be maintained" without the truth, and the truth cannot be maintained "without observance" of the law.102 Their relation, he says, is like that of body and spirit.103 A body with no spirit is a corpse. A spirit with no body is a ghost. The judge is the corpse, and the saint who has dropped the law is the ghost, and the tradition wanted neither.

The outside critic's charge is answered in the person who keeps the law without fear. And Rabi'a's prayer leaves the question everything so far has been circling. Love has to be accounted for as a method, because the tradition put it exactly where knowledge and discipline had failed.

Part 8: Love Is the Method, Not the Reward

The self cannot be argued out of its claim, and it cannot be disciplined out of it, because arguing and disciplining are things the self does. There is one act in which you leave the claimant behind on purpose, and that act is love. So love is not the prize at the end of the path. It is the method, and everything earlier, the remembrance and the stations and the return, was running on it.

Begin with what you already know, because you have been in it. A child is sick for a week, and you sit up with them, night after night. Nothing about it is an emergency; there is no alarm, no adrenaline, nothing that switches the self off from outside. You chose it, and you keep choosing it at three in the morning. And for hours at a time there is no one in the room but the child. Your whole concern has gone across, and the claimant, which survived every program you ever ran against it, is simply not there. Nobody fought it and nothing switched it off. It was left, the way a room is left when someone walks out of it to go where they are needed. That is the fact everything here is built on. Love is the one way you can leave the claimant behind on purpose, without a fight and without an alarm. And what you leave it for is not a task but a person, who is still there when the fever breaks.

Two cases before the distinction, because the word covers two opposite motions. The first person loves someone and wants to keep them. The love is real and the wanting is real, and the beloved is, at bottom, a possession, the most precious one, guarded and feared for. When the beloved's good and the lover's having conflict, the having wins, and the lover calls that love. The second person loves someone and wants to disappear into them. The beloved's good is what the lover wants. When it conflicts with the lover's having, the good wins, and the lover does not experience that as a loss. Same word, opposite direction. The first love is the claimant using the beloved. The second love is the claimant spent. The test for which one you are in is simple and painful. When the beloved's good and your having them come apart, which one do you choose, and how does it feel.

The argument is short. Think back over every method that has come before. Asceticism is something the self does. It fasts, and it becomes an ascetic self, proud of its fasting, and the scholar who lost his voice caught himself doing exactly that. Knowledge is something the self holds. It learns that the self is a claim and becomes a knowing self, attached to being the one who knows. Even remembrance, the engine of the path, is a practice the self can sit down to and collect: I am someone who does remembrance. Every method is done by the self, and what the self does, it owns, and what it owns feeds it. Except one. Love is the only act whose success is the self's absence. You cannot love well while watching yourself love for an audience; that watching is the failure. So love is the one method the claimant cannot collect, because the moment it collects it, the love has become the first kind and stopped working. A careful listener will have noticed something, and it should be met. Love begins with a choice, and a choice has a chooser. The chooser is you, acting without the claimant, which is what the heart has meant all along. What is absent at three in the morning is the claimant, and you are what walked into the room.

The tradition says this in its own words, and its definition of love is not a sentiment; it is a mechanism. Love, one of the handbooks says, quoting an earlier master, "is the effacement of the lover's attributes and the establishment of the Beloved's essence".132 Effacement. The word the tradition uses for annihilation, applied to the lover. And the handbook's own gloss, which is the claim here word for word: "thus in love for his Friend he becomes an enemy to himself".133 The self is not defeated in love. It changes sides. Rumi says why no argument can do this job. "Love is the astrolabe of the mysteries of God", he writes, the instrument that finds what reason cannot sight.134 A few lines on, having tried to explain love, he gives up in a line the tradition loves. The intellect, trying to expound it, lay down like a donkey in the mud. It was love alone that explained love.135 That is the doctor and the drunk again, from the earlier argument about the two knowledges. Love is knowledge by taste, in the purest form there is, and it cannot be handed over as a report.

Part 9: Can It Be True Without God

Everything the argument has said about a person can be lifted out of the religion it came from and tested on a person, and it survives. The one thing that cannot be lifted out is the object of the hunger. Take that out and the diagnosis changes, not only the wrapper. What follows says which claims lift, which do not, what reading the argument holds, and how far it can carry someone who does not share it.

Here is the secular reading as its best defender would put it, because most people watching hold it. Sufism is a psychology in a theological wrapper. The self is a construct, and the Sufis saw it a thousand years before anyone had the word. Remembrance is attention training, and it works for the reason any attention training works. Annihilation is what happens when self-referring thought stops, and people get there on a cushion with no God in the room. Love as the displacing act is a fact about human beings, and every parent knows it. All of that is usable, and all of it can be kept, and the God part is a historical wrapper that can be peeled off. On this reading the Sufis were excellent psychologists who mistook a feature of the mind for a feature of the universe.

Take the reading seriously, and see how much it is right about, because the list is long. The self as a claim rather than an owner: a claim about a person, run on the street, and it held. The two knowledges, report and taste: a claim about a person, run at the doctor's bedside, and it held. Stations earned and states given: a claim about a person, and you can run the gratitude case in a month. Annihilation and the return: a claim about a person, with the musician and the parent as its evidence. Love as the act in which the claimant leaves: a claim about a person, and the sick room had a child in it, not a God. Every one of those was argued here without asking you to believe in anything, and every one survives the lift. A person who does not believe in God can take all five home tonight, and the argument has been built so that they can.

Now what does not survive, and it is one thing, and it sits at the root. The argument began with a hunger tied to the self, absent whenever the self is absent, and two readings of that fact were left open. On one, the self is the reader of an alarm, and when it is switched off the alarm stops. On the other, the self is the distance from the one thing the hunger wants. When it steps aside the hunger is, for a moment, answered. The secular reading takes the first. It can have everything else, including the moment on the cushion when the self is gone, and it reads that moment as silence. The Sufi reading reads the same moment as arrival. That is the whole difference, and it is not small: on the first reading the reed is singing about a bed that does not exist. All five of the lifted claims still work on the first reading, and what they produce is a person who is quieter. They do not produce a person who is fed, because on that reading there is nothing to be fed by.

Rumi has one image for the difference, and it is the mirage. "Although the thirsty man is delighted by the mirage", he writes, "he runs away when he comes up to it, and seeks for water".146 The mirage is every object the hunger reached for and dropped. The secular reading says the whole desert is mirage, that there is no water, and that the wise thing is to stop running. The Sufi reading says the mirage proves the water, in this sense: nothing would be delighted by a mirage that was not built for water. Rumi puts it as a reversal. If the thirsty seek water, then "water too seeks in the world them that are thirsty". The thirst is the water's pull on you, felt from your end, and not only yours.147 That is the whole Sufi claim about the hunger, and it is beautiful, and it now has to face the objection that cuts deepest.

The objection, in the plainest form. A felt need is not evidence that its object exists. Thirst shows that you have a body built to drink. It does not show that there is water within reach, or that there is water at all. People have died of thirst in deserts, and their thirst was as real as anyone's. A hunger that nothing on earth satisfies might mean you were made for something beyond the earth. It might equally mean that you were made by a process that never promised satisfaction, and left you wanting because wanting kept your ancestors alive. The argument from the hunger to its object proves, at most, that you are the kind of creature that hungers. It proves nothing about what is out there. That is right, and it is accepted here without a fight. The hunger is evidence that you are built to be fed, and it is not evidence that there is food. The tradition's real position was never that this argument works as a proof. A Sufi who offered it as one would be corrected by the handbooks.

So what was the tradition's position, if not a proof. It was a wager, tested from inside. Here is the shape of it. Two readings of the same absence are on the table, silence and arrival, and neither can be settled by argument, as the objection just showed. But they predict different things, and the predictions are about you. If the absence is silence, then with practice the wanting fades and you will not miss it. The best days are the ones with least of it. If the absence is arrival, then with practice the wanting does not fade; it changes into something you would not give up. Rumi has the case. A thirsty man on a high wall above a stream cannot get down. So he tears bricks from the wall and throws them into the water, to hear the splash.148 "The more thirsty any one on the top of the wall is, the more quickly does he tear off the bricks".149 The bricks are the practice, and every one he tears off brings the water nearer and the sound louder. On this reading practice does not quiet the thirst; it turns it into the thing he would not give up. Those are different outcomes, and the person best placed to read which one arrived is the one whose hunger it is.

The critic has one more move, and it lands. A test only the tester can read is not a test. It is exactly the self-certification the tradition forbids: the person decides for themselves that they have arrived, and calls the deciding evidence. Two answers, and the second gives ground. First, the tradition never lets the inside reading stand alone. The outcome shows in conduct, sooner or later, and conduct is public. The tradition's whole apparatus of company and guidance exists to check the inside reading against the outside one. A person who says the hunger has changed and is harder to live with has failed the test, whatever they feel. And what the tradition forbids you to certify alone is arrival. What your hunger is doing this month is not arrival, and you are the only one who can report it. Second, the ground given. The deepest part of the outcome, what the hunger is doing now, can only be read from inside, and that is a limit. It is the same limit every account of what a life feels like has to live under, including the secular one. Nobody can prove to you from outside that the management worked either.

The ruling. The argument holds the psychology to be true: the five claims just listed, argued on cases, standing whether or not there is a God. The metaphysics is the claim that the absence is arrival and the hunger has a source. The argument holds that to be a claim you can test and that nothing said here can prove. It is not held as fact, and it is not dismissed as wrapper. The handbooks built stages and tests around it rather than proofs, and that is what a wager looks like from inside. And here is what the secular practitioner should expect, said plainly, so that nobody is sold anything. You can take the five claims and practise them without the object. You will get quieter, the claimant will get thinner, and your life will be better. That is real and it is not nothing. What you will not get, on your own reading, is fed, because your reading says there is nothing to be fed by. So quiet is the ceiling, by your own account and not by the tradition's prediction. Whether that ceiling is the real one, or only the ceiling of the reading, is the thing you would have to find out. No one can find it out for you.

The desert, then. Every claim about the thirsty person lifts out and holds. The claim about the water does not lift out, and the argument from the thirst to the water fails as a proof. What remains is two readings of the same absence, silence and arrival, and two predictions about what practice does to the thirst. Only the thirsty can read the result, and it is checked against how they then live. Which means the argument ends where the tradition always ended, in practice. The next question is the practical one. What, exactly, does a person do tomorrow morning with no master and no monastery. And which parts of the method does the tradition say cannot be done that way at all.

Part 10: The Practice Without a Monastery

The method's units are small and specific, and most of them need no belief. But the tradition is blunt about one thing, and it is the thing you most want to hear the opposite of. The whole of it cannot be done alone. What can be done alone is set out here, unit by unit, with what each one removes. Then the thing that cannot, and why, and what the tradition offers in its place.

The first unit is the accounting, and it is for tonight. Ghazali built a whole book of his great work around it.150 The picture he opens with is a merchant at the end of a trading day. Merchants, he writes, when they "settle accounts they seek a healthy profit".151 The intellect, he says, does the same with a life. He gives the practice six steps, in order. In the morning you set the terms: what today is for. Through the day, you watch. At night, the accounting; then a penalty for what it found; then renewed effort; then, last, a word of reproach to yourself. He quotes a saying handed down from the second caliph: "Call yourselves to account before being called to account".152 Here is what the practice does, in the terms used so far. The claimant writes a version of the day, all day, in which it comes out well. The accounting checks that version against the day. You go through the acts one by one and ask of each: who owned that. The gift on the street, the apology, the hour of work, the thing you said at dinner. Most of them, you will find, had an owner who stepped forward afterwards, and the finding is the practice. You are not asked to punish yourself, and Ghazali's penalties belong to his world, not yours. You are asked to see, and the seer is the heart. Every night you do it, the seer gets a little steadier and the claimant a little more visible.

The second unit is remembrance, and it was given in full earlier. One name or one phrase, returned to, for a fixed time, and then in the gaps of the day. What it removes is the claimant's food, which is attention. A person who does not believe in God can do it with any word that is not about themselves. The tradition would say they are doing something real and something less, and both halves of that are true.

The third unit is service, and the tradition ranks it higher than you would expect. One of the earliest written rules for a Sufi house, drawn up by a master in eastern Iran a thousand years ago, has ten articles.153 One says: "Let them welcome the poor and needy and all who join their company, and let them bear patiently the trouble of waiting upon them".154 Another: "Let them not eat anything save in participation with one another".155 What service removes is the self's position at the centre of its own attention, by putting someone else's need there. It is the unit hardest to counterfeit, because the need is someone else's and is met whether or not anyone collects. The collecting can spoil the giver, as it did on the street, but it cannot unwash the dish. The same master is reported to have said what the whole path produces, and the sentence is the ending state. "The true saint goes in and out amongst the people and eats and sleeps with them". He "buys and sells in the market and marries and takes part in social intercourse". And he "never forgets God for a single moment".156 In the market. Not in the cave.

The fourth unit is retreat, and here the tradition disagrees with itself, and the disagreement is instructive. The school of Bayazid, the drunk saint, enjoined retirement from the world on all its disciples.157 The handbook that reports that goes the other way, and quotes a saying with a warning in it. "Let none imagine, however, that solitude merely consists in living alone".159 As long as the thought of other people is in your heart, it says, you are not in solitude. It does not matter what room you are in. And the reverse: "the true solitary is not disturbed by society".160 The preoccupied man, it goes on, "seeks in vain to acquire freedom from thought by secluding himself".161 So the retreat, in the tradition's own ruling, is not a place. It is the absence of the audience in your head, and you can have that in a crowd or fail to have it in a cell. The retreat you went on, and came back from wanting another, was a cell with the audience still in it. The unit that matters is not ten days of silence. The fifth unit is the one the tradition says cannot be skipped and you cannot supply. Company. The handbook is not gentle about it. "Solitude is fatal to the novice", it says.162 It gives the reason in a saying: Satan is with the solitary, and farther from two who are together.163 Why fatal. Here is the problem with that, and it is the reason most people who get this far stop. The tradition says you need a guide, because the self appoints itself the judge of its own progress. The modern viewer has no guide and every reason to distrust the ones on offer. And the reason is not squeamishness. Submission to a guide is exactly where the abuses happen, movement after movement, and you know the cases. So the tradition seems to say: the one unit you cannot do alone is the one that is not safe to do with anyone. The tradition's answer is not to drop the requirement. It is to make the tests for a guide stricter than trust, and to say that the abuses are what happens when the tests are skipped. The handbook gives two tests, and three more follow from the account of the claimant. From the handbook: a guide who has trained under no one. And a guide who "takes pains to obtain publicity", who is "led astray by self-conceit".167 From the claimant: a guide who says he has arrived or is past the law, because that was the disguise that ends the audit. A guide who profits, because the claimant collects. And a guide who cannot be left, because the guide is meant to be the direction of prayer for a while, and nobody worships the compass. Two things must be said against the tests, and both are true. A careful abuser can pass the first four. The fifth can only be run by leaving, which is the one test you can run early, and should. And no test certifies that a guide has arrived, since arrival cannot be certified by anyone, and the tradition knew it. A guide is a bet on a person with checks attached, never a certificate. That does not solve the problem of finding one. It means the person with no guide is better off than the person with a bad one. And far better off than the person who did not know the tests.

So the ruling on doing it alone, plainly. What the tradition allows: the stations, which are habits and can be kept; the accounting; remembrance; service. Those are the units, and they can all be done tomorrow, and the tradition says they are real. The solitude the handbook calls fatal is retreat from company, not a quarter of an hour alone at night. The ending state, called back by its own words: the saint in the market, and the true solitary who is not disturbed by society. Put it beside where you started. You were exhausted by being the one who gets better, and watching yourself be good, and wanting something no success touched. The diagnosis was that the seeing was never the problem; the one who stepped forward to collect was. The cure was never to try harder, because trying was the claim at work. It was to see the claim, and starve it, and let love empty the room. Then to come back to the market with the same duties and no rival inside. That is the person the whole method was for. It is a person, with a name and a job, who does not talk about their retreat.

The whole argument, once, as steps. One: the hunger under your wants is not for things; it is tied to the self, and absent when the self is. Two: the one who wants is a claimant, not an owner, and you can catch it, because claiming and seeing are two acts. Three: nothing exists by itself, so the claim is mistaken in principle, and exercise survives while ownership goes. Four: knowing that changes nothing, because knowledge by report is a possession, and only taste reaches the claimant. Five: taste has a path, stations you earn and states you are given, driven by remembrance, which starves what lives on attention. Six: the end is the extinction of the claim and the return of the person, and the return is higher than the trance. Seven: the returned person keeps the law, because "I am beyond it" is the claimant's last disguise. Eight: the engine is love, the one act in which you leave the claimant behind on purpose, for a person and not a task. Nine: all of that lifts out and holds without God. Whether the absence is silence or arrival does not lift out, and that is a wager only you can test. Ten: the units are small, the accounting first, and the one thing you may not do alone is certify the result.

One thing to do tonight, and it needs no belief. Before you sleep, take the day, and go through what you did in it, act by act. For each one ask a single question, and answer it honestly, and do nothing else about it. The question is: who owned that.
