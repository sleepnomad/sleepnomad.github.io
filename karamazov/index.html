Everyone knows one line from The Brothers Karamazov. If there is no God, everything is permitted. The line travels without the book, and it travels wrong. The line is not said that way. It is not said by Ivan, not at first. What most people take from the book is this, and it is the smart view, not the lazy one. Dostoevsky wrote the best case against God that any novel contains. A child of five locked in a privy overnight, her face smeared with her own filth, praying.5 A boy torn apart by dogs in front of his mother.6 A cardinal who tells Christ to his face that people cannot bear freedom, and that the Church has fixed his mistake.7 And then, having written that, Dostoevsky answers it with a monk's memoirs and a kiss. Neither is an argument. So the book is a masterpiece with a hole where the answer should be, and the author's own letters admit he was worried.8

So the book concedes the argument from the children. No God may pay for harmony with a child's tears, and nobody in the book argues that one may. What it denies is that the man who won that argument can live on what follows from it. And what it puts in the place of belief is not a better belief. It is a practice, which the monk calls active love, and its only evidence is a life.13 That is why the book ends at a child's funeral, with a dozen boys cheering, and calls that hope.14 What follows is the argument that gets you there, one step at a time, starting with a father nobody could love.

Part 1: A Father Nobody Could Love

The book's question is not whether God exists. Its question is whether a person can live without loving anyone. It tests that on the hardest case it can build, a father who has earned nothing. That is the claim of this part, and the rest of it is the case.

Fyodor Pavlovitch Karamazov is introduced in the first sentence of the book as a type, abject and vicious and at the same time senseless.15 He is a small landowner who got his money by marrying it and keeping it.16 He ran to dine at other men's tables and fastened on them as a toady.17 He drank, he kept women in the house while his wife was alive, and he cried in public about his grievances while he did it.18 He had three sons by two wives, Dmitri by the first, Ivan and Alyosha by the second, and he lost all three the same way.19 The narrator is exact about the first one. Fyodor abandoned him not from malice, and not because of anything the mother had done, but simply because he forgot him.20 A servant took the three year old in, because otherwise there would have been no one even to change the child's shirt.21 The two younger boys were forgotten in the same way and raised in the same servant's cottage.22 Their father held his parties in the house across the yard.23

Hold on to how ordinary that is. A general who sets dogs on a child is a monster; Fyodor is something more common. He is a man who never once noticed that anyone else was real, and who has arranged his life so that he never has to. He is also, in a way the book insists on, funny. He clowns. He lies for pleasure. He tells stories against himself before anyone else can, so that nothing anyone says about him can land.

The book opens the real argument by putting this man in a room with the one person who can see through him. The family goes to the monastery to bring a money quarrel between Fyodor and Dmitri, the eldest, before a holy man.24 They put it in front of the elder, Zosima, who is old and dying and treated by the town as a saint.25 Fyodor arrives and performs. He kneels, he babbles, he tells a false story about a saint carrying his own severed head, he calls himself a buffoon in earnest.26 And Zosima, who has said almost nothing, tells him the one thing nobody has ever told him. Do not be so ashamed of yourself, for that is at the root of it all. And then, above all, don't lie to yourself.27 The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie, he says, comes to such a state. He can no longer tell the truth inside him from the truth around him. And so he loses all respect for himself and for others.28

Then Zosima names the thing the whole book will turn on. There are two kinds of love, and they look alike from the outside. Love in dreams is greedy for immediate action, rapidly performed, in the sight of all.38 It wants to be seen, it wants to be done with, and it wants to be thanked. There is an obvious objection. The Inquisitor is right. Look at any election, any crowd, any market. People choose bread over freedom every time they are offered the choice. A poem that calls that weakness is sneering at the human race from a cell it has never been locked in.

The book concedes most of that, and the concession is this. It agrees that people mostly choose bread; the whole plot is people choosing bread, money, drink, revenge, whatever is nearest. The disagreement is narrower. It is about what follows from that fact. The Inquisitor says: therefore rule them, lovingly, with lies. The book says: a rule built on contempt for the ruled ends in that cell. It ends with a man burning the one person he could not lie to. And the man building it knows. That is the part the objection cannot absorb. The Inquisitor is not shown to be wrong about people. On this reading, he is shown unable to look at the prisoner, sending him away rather than killing him, and keeping the kiss. A man whose premise was true would have lit the fire. If people really were sheep and the prisoner only a nuisance, burning him would have cost nothing. Opening the door is the admission that the old man cannot.

Where that leaves the argument. The Inquisitor's case is that people cannot bear freedom and must be governed by loving lies, and the book grants that people mostly choose bread. The poem is a self-portrait. Its author has just confessed that the old man does not believe, and has taken the charge as his own. The old man is what the returned ticket becomes with power behind it. The reply is a kiss, on this reading, because any argument would have been another authority, and the book does not pretend the kiss converts anyone. That leaves a question the book cannot dodge. If the answer to Ivan cannot be an argument, what can it be. The book's next move is to stop the story and hand over the life of an old man who is about to die.

Part 5: The Answer That Is Not an Argument

The book's reply to Ivan is the life of an old man, told after his death by the one person who loved him. It is offered as a reply, the only kind the Inquisitor chapter left open, and it is not a proof. The reply holds, as far as the book goes, and it holds by changing what a reply is.

The book has set itself a problem. Three times now it has declined to argue: in the elder's cell, in the poem, and at the tavern table. If the book is going to say anything back, it cannot be a chain of reasons. A chain of reasons is the thing Ivan is best at, and the thing the Inquisitor said people cannot live without. So the book stops. The plot halts for a whole section, and Alyosha writes down, from memory, what the dying elder said about his own life. It never argues with Ivan, and all of it bears on him.

Part 11: The Ruling

Here is what the book argued, in four steps, said once as steps. Then the three strongest objections to it, each answered or conceded.

Step one, which is Ivan's. Against a God who buys harmony with a child's tears, Ivan is right, and nobody in the book argues otherwise. He granted God, granted the harmony, and refused to consent to the price. Alyosha names a forgiver, and nobody in the novel argues that the forgiving makes the price acceptable. The elder's whole account of suffering is consolation, not justification, and the reading here follows him. The argument from the children is not answered, because it is correct.

Step two, which is the plot's. All things are lawful cannot be lived by the one who states it, and the book showed this by letting the idea walk. It reached a cook who had no reason to refuse it, and he took it as permission.335 He asked for one more thing, a departure, and got it.336 Then it came back to Ivan as a poor relation on his sofa, reciting his schoolboy anecdote.337 The conscience he said would go with the belief did not go when he dropped the belief. It lost its voice and kept its grip, which is worse. One limit on that step, conceded: it is one man, with blood on his hands. A seminarian in the book denies Ivan's whole thesis, says mankind can be virtuous without immortality, and lives comfortably on it.338 The book despises him, never breaks him, and never tests him.

Set that against Ivan, because this is where the book gives him the only reply it has, in the way it said it would. Ivan's children were cases. He kept cuttings; he read them out; he built an argument on them and returned a ticket. Every one of those children was real, and every one was, in Ivan's hands, an item. They go in, and come out with something they will carry. That is active love, from the widow's chapter, run to its end. Slow, unpaid, unwatched, spent on the person in front of you, and remembered by someone who was not the one you spent it on.

And Kolya's question is the widow's question, asked by a boy who has been told there is no one to ask it of. Can it be true. Alyosha does not prove it. That is the elder's move with the widow: something to do and someone to trust, handed over in place of a proof. What that does to a life can be said in four sentences.

The person you cannot love is the test, and there is no other test. Not humanity, but the man in the chair, the father who forgot you, the boy who bit you. The act that counts is small, and it holds only while it is not hoarded, and the instant you say mine and kick it breaks. And one thing in this book is never lost, not to the corpse, not to the court, not to the fever. It is a scene someone made for someone else without knowing it would be kept. Every good person the book shows was given one, so the only instruction the book gives is to make one. For someone who cannot pay you back, and who may not understand it, and who will remember it in a room you will never see.

The funeral and the cheer happen in the same minute. The book is not softening the death with the cheer. It is saying that a boy is dead, and that a dozen boys stood at his stone. Both of those are true, and the second is the only reply to the first that anyone has ever managed. That is the most that the book will say, and the book can afford it, because it never once claimed to have anything else.

Part 13: Where the Book Breaks

Three places where the argument is thinner than the plot, and then what the book leaves a person with anyway. A case that hides its weak points is asking to be trusted, and this book, of all books, does not ask for that.

The first break is Alyosha. The book we have is not the book Dostoevsky meant to write. In the preface he says so. There is one life story and two novels, and the main novel is the second one, set in the present day. The one in our hands is almost not a novel at all, only a moment from the hero's early youth thirteen years before.376 He died a few months after finishing it, and the second novel was never written.377 That matters for the argument, because Alyosha is the person the argument is supposed to prove itself on. He is the one who leaves the monastery to practise active love in the world.378 The book we have gives him one real test of his own, the boys and Ilyusha, and it goes well. His losses are all inflicted from outside, in one autumn. He never has to spend years on a person who does not improve. The elder said love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing compared with love in dreams.379 The book shows the dreadful part on the elder, and on Grushenka, and on the captain, and never on its hero. The resolute champion who rises from the earth at Cana is untried, and the author planned more, and died.380

Not one of them is helped by an argument, and the book contains one of the strongest arguments ever put in a novel. Every one of them is changed, or not, by one act that one other person did or failed to do for them. That is the whole of it, and the book spent its whole length finding out whether the elder was right to say so.

Here is the question the book hands you, and it is the one it was built to ask. Not whether God exists. Not whether the ticket should be returned; that one, Ivan may keep. The question is the one from the elder's cell, put to a man who had just said nothing is immoral. Which of these can you live in. The book's honest report is that the argument cannot be lived in by the man who made it. The practice can be, by ordinary and unreliable people, for as long as it is not hoarded. And the practice starts small. One person, who cannot pay you back, who may not understand, and who is in the room. An onion, not hoarded, not claimed, and remembered by someone else in a room you will never see. That is the whole of the book's instruction. It is the only part of the book that does not need to be believed, because it only needs to be done.
