Cold open

The three lines people quote most as Shakespeare's wisdom are spoken by a spy, a poser and a murderer. To thine own self be true is the last of a dozen tips a father gives his son about clothes and lending money.1 The father dies behind a curtain, listening at a door.2 All the world's a stage is spoken by a man who does nothing all play but stand aside and sneer.3 The moment he finishes, a starving old servant is carried in to be fed.4 A tale told by an idiot, signifying nothing, is spoken by a king who murdered his way to an empty room.5 He has just been told his wife is dead.6 Shakespeare never speaks in his own voice. There is no page anywhere where he tells you what he thinks. Every idea belongs to a person on a stage, and the play then does something to that person.

So it is easy to conclude he had no philosophy, and most people who love him say exactly that. He gave every side its best voice and never chose. That conclusion is wrong, and the evidence against it is the plots. The same thing goes wrong the same way across twenty years of plays. A man demands proof of something that cannot be proved. A wife's love, a daughter's love, his own real self, the right to the crown on his head. The demand destroys the thing he wanted proved. And in play after play, the one act that ever turns a man the other way is the same act. He stops demanding, and accepts what he already knew. It does not always save him. It is the only thing that ever does.

That is the philosophy, and it can be said in one sentence. People are not ruined by what they do not know. They are ruined by refusing to accept what they already know, about other people, about themselves, about power, and about death. The cure the plays show is acceptance without proof. In the tragedies the plays call it readiness. In the late plays they call it grace, and they give it to human beings to perform, and thank the gods afterwards.

What follows proves that sentence the only way it can be proved, by watching what the plays do to the ideas spoken in them. First the method, which is a kind of experiment. Then the experiment run on the self, on love, on what things are worth, on kings, on nature, and on death. And finally on the hardest case, forgiving a man who never says sorry.

Part 1: The Man Who Never Speaks

Shakespeare holds no doctrine, and every line people quote as his wisdom belongs to a character the play is busy judging. That is where this begins, and there is a second half that matters more. The plays do not mark those lines false. They mark what each one costs, and who pays.

Start with the most quoted advice in the language. A father is seeing his son off on a ship. The boy is late for his ship, and the father holds him back to give him rules for living. Dress as well as you can afford but not showily, because clothes announce the man.7 Do not borrow and do not lend, because a loan loses the money and the friend.8 And then the line that sits on mugs and in wedding speeches. This above all, to thine own self be true. And it must follow, as the night the day, thou canst not then be false to any man.9 The speaker is Polonius. He is the king's counsellor in Hamlet, and the rest of his life in the play is spent hiding and listening.10 He sends a servant to spy on that same son abroad.11 He hides behind a curtain to overhear the prince talking to the prince's mother. Hamlet hears a noise, stabs through the cloth, and Polonius dies with the words, O, I am slain.12 Hamlet, seeing who it is, says over the body, thou wretched, rash, intruding fool, farewell.13 The man who said be true to yourself and you cannot be false to anyone died being false to the people around him. The play arranged that.

The third line is the hardest, and it is the one people quote when they want to sound unflinching. Life is a tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing. The speaker is Macbeth.20 He is besieged in his castle at the end of the play. He has murdered a king who trusted him, a friend, and a household of children. A cry of women is heard. A servant goes to find out and returns. The queen, my lord, is dead. Macbeth's first response is that she has died at an inconvenient time. She should have died hereafter. Then the speech about tomorrow and tomorrow, the brief candle, the poor player who struts and frets his hour upon the stage, and the idiot's tale. Here a distinction is needed. Nobody on stage answers Macbeth. The play does not follow this speech with a starving servant being fed. It does not say life means something. What it does is show, over five acts, how this man emptied his life of meaning with his own hands. So when he says life is empty, the audience knows whose life he is describing, and why. The line is located rather than refuted. It is true of a life lived like his, and the play has shown what that life was.

So far the pattern looks simple. A character says something quotable, and the play shows him up. If that were the whole method, it would be a cheap trick. It would also be a philosophy of a kind, the sneering kind, where every belief is a fool's belief. It is not the whole method, and the same fat knight is the proof.

Before that battle, alone, Falstaff talks himself out of honour. Can honour set to a leg?. No. Or an arm?. No. Or take away the grief of a wound?. No. Honour hath no skill in surgery then?. No. What is honour?. A word. He asks who has it, and answers, he that died on Wednesday, and asks whether that man feels it, and answers, no. Therefore, I'll none of it.25 The play then does something particular with this speech. The other great figure of that play is a young nobleman who lives for honour and talks about almost nothing else. In the battle that follows, he dies on the field. It is the prince who kills him, in single combat, and says a few generous words over the body.26 Then Falstaff, who faked his own death a few yards away, gets up, stabs the corpse, and carries it off to claim the credit.27 The play does not refute the speech against honour. It stages the very world the speech describes. The man who scorned honour is alive and the man who loved it is dead. The one is carrying the other's body off for a reward, and the prince who actually did the killing lets him. The prince holds honour without ever making a speech about it. That is a third answer to the question of honour, and the quietest one in the play.

Here is the truer version of the rule, and it is better than the simple one. The plays do not mark a maxim false. They mark what it costs and who pays the bill. Falstaff is right that honour cannot set a leg. The price of being right about that is the man he is. At the end of the next play the prince he loves becomes king and, in front of everyone, refuses to know him. Polonius is not wrong that clothes announce the man or that loans lose friends. The price of a life run on tips is that there is nobody home to be true to. Jaques is not wrong that people age and lose their teeth. The price of standing outside life to describe it is that you stand outside it. Every one of these lines is a bill, and the play shows you who pays it.

This is where the strongest objection arrives, and it deserves its full weight. If every view in the plays is spoken by someone, and every someone is shown paying for it, then Shakespeare is a relativist. He gives the villain great lines and the saint great lines. He writes both from the inside with equal care. A man who can do that has no position. His greatness is that he refused to choose, and the phrase Shakespeare's philosophy is a contradiction in terms, like the weather's opinion. Many of the people who love him most believe this, and it has a distinguished history. It is also the most comfortable belief a reader can hold, because it makes every line free for the taking. Whichever quotation fits your mood today is Shakespeare's wisdom today.

The answer begins with a fact about how plays work that the objection ignores. A play has two ways to pass a verdict. A character can say something, and the author can arrange what happens next. Shakespeare almost never uses the first in his own voice. When someone on stage passes judgement, it is a character with a stake in the scene. A queen who is bored, a servant who is frightened, someone inside the story with something to lose. The verdicts that count are the second kind. The verdict on Polonius is a sword through a curtain. The verdict on Jaques is an old man carried in to be fed. The verdict on Falstaff's speech about honour is the young nobleman's corpse and Falstaff standing over it, alive and lying. None of those verdicts are spoken. All of them are chosen. Someone decided that Orlando enters at that line and not ten lines later. Someone decided that Polonius dies spying and not in bed. A writer with no views does not need to decide those things one way rather than another. The choices are the opinions.

That is only the beginning of the answer, because it opens a harder question. If the verdicts are in the plots, then reading the philosophy means reading what the plots do, not what the characters say. And plots can be read in more than one way too. A method is needed, and it has to be one that anyone can check. Otherwise the whole enterprise is just another person's mood dressed as Shakespeare's.

So far, then. The famous lines are views given to people, and each person is then shown paying for the view they hold. The plays do not refute a line by having a narrator contradict it. They refute, or price, a line by what they make happen. So there is a method here, and it is not an absence of opinion. What that method is, whether it can be checked, and what it finds when it is run on the largest questions, comes next.

Part 2: Thinking in Cases

Shakespeare's philosophy is a method before it is a set of results. The method is this. Take a view and state it in its strongest form, in the mouth of the person best placed to believe it. Give that person a life. Then run the whole world of the play against the view and watch what it costs. Philosophers have a name for this kind of thing. They call it a thought experiment, and they use it whenever a principle cannot be tested in a laboratory. You build a case, you put the principle inside it, and you see what breaks. The plays were doing this before the name existed, and they were doing it with a rigour that a viewer can check.

Here is the method at full stretch. An army has been camped outside a walled city for seven years and has nothing to show for it. Their generals meet to ask why. A commander called Ulysses stands and gives the most complete speech about political order anywhere in the plays. The heavens themselves, the planets, and this centre observe degree, priority and place. This centre is the earth, and degree is rank.30 Everything in the universe has a rank and keeps it, he says, and that is why the universe holds together. Take but degree away, untune that string, and hark what discord follows. Remove rank, and the rude son strikes his father dead, appetite becomes a universal wolf, and the world eats itself.3132 The army is failing, he says, because its greatest fighter will not obey and the chain of command has gone slack.33 Heard on its own, this is a philosophy. It has the grandeur of one, and heard out of the play it sounds like a view a man might hold.

Now the objection that a serious reader will already have made. The reader supplies the philosophy. Hand the same play to a priest, a revolutionary and a psychoanalyst, and each will walk out with a different Shakespeare who agrees with them. That has been happening for four hundred years, and it never stops. So when someone claims to have read the philosophy off the plot, they have read their own mind off a mirror. The answer is the constraint the objection forgets. A reader can reinterpret what a character says. A reader cannot reinterpret who dies. The old counsellor is stabbed behind the curtain in every reading of Hamlet that has ever existed. Ulysses rigs the lottery in every reading of his play. Part 3: Nothing Inside

The plays deny that there is a findable true self to be true to. Most people will resist that, so it needs saying carefully. The plays are full of inner life, grief that cannot be spoken, love that will not fit in a mouth. What they deny is something more specific. An essence, separate from what a person does and how they are seen, that could be found and shown and held up as the real one. Every character who goes looking for that finds nothing, and says so. The search is staged again and again, in kings, in soldiers, in a prince, and the result is the same each time.

Begin with the man who had the most to find. Richard the Second is a king who believes that his kingship is part of his body. His cousin raises an army and takes the country, and Richard is brought to a hall to give up the crown in public. He asks for a mirror. He looks into it, and the speech he makes is a search. Was this the face that every day under his household roof did keep ten thousand men?46. Was this the face that like the sun did make beholders wink?. He is looking for the king in the face, and he cannot find him. A brittle glory shineth in this face. He smashes the glass on the floor.47 Then he says his grief lies all within, that the substance is inside and the face was only its shadow.48 That is the claim of an inner self, made by the man who has just failed to find one in the glass. The substance he names is grief, and grief is a feeling, not a self. Grief is something a man has. A self would be the thing that has it, and that is what he cannot find or name. He says it is there. And in the same scene he says the strangest thing a king can say. I have no name, no title, and know not now what name to call myself.49 Take the crown off, and he does not know what to call the thing that is left. He has not forgotten his name. The name was the crown, and he had believed there was something under it.

The argument, as steps. One, in the plays, what a person is, is what they do and how they are seen. Two, several characters go looking for a self beneath that. Three, each one reports that they found roles and a blank. Four, the characters who demand an essence end badly, every one. Accepting the roles guarantees nothing, and the comedies have their own casualties, a steward shut up in the dark, a moneylender stripped in court. But the ruin in the tragedies always begins with the demand. Coriolanus dies the moment he yields to his mother. What put him at the gates with an army was years of refusing to be anyone's son. So the plays rule that demanding the inner self is the disease, and not only that the search comes up empty.

This changes the famous advice. To thine own self be true assumes there is a self in there, separate from what you do. Your actions can be true or false to it. The plays say the actions are the self. Be true to yourself, in their world, can only mean, do the things a person you would respect does, and let that be what you are. That is smaller advice, and much harder.

So there is no findable self beneath the roles, and the search for one ends in a mirror smashed on the floor. A person is what they do and how they are seen. Which raises the next question immediately, and it is the one the tragedies are built on. If I am what I do and how I am seen, then what is it to know another person?. What can I know of a wife, or a daughter, beyond what she does and how she seems?. The tragedies are about the man who demands more, and the next part is his.

Part 4: Ocular Proof

The tragedies of jealousy and love are not caused by ignorance. Othello, and the jealous king of The Winter's Tale, and old Lear with his daughters, are not men who knew too little. They are men who demanded certainty about another person's heart, which is a demand for a kind of thing that does not exist. And the demand produces the very thing it fears. The proof of that claim is three men, and one of them had no villain whispering in his ear at all.

Start with the one who did. Othello is a general who has married a senator's daughter against her father's wishes, and she loves him. The play gives him everything he could need to know it. She defied her family for him and followed him to a war, and none of that is in dispute when his officer Iago begins to hint. Othello does what a reasonable man does with a hint, and asks for evidence. Villain, be sure thou prove my love a whore. Be sure of it. Give me the ocular proof.71 He wants to see it with his eyes, and that sounds like the opposite of jealousy, like a man refusing to be jealous without grounds. Then, a few lines later, the sentence the whole tragedy sits on. I think my wife be honest, and think she is not. I think that thou art just, and think thou art not. I'll have some proof.72 Two beliefs at once, and he cannot bear to live between them, so he needs the space closed. And notice the form of the demand. He asks for proof that she is false, not proof that she is true. He has already decided which certainty would settle him.

The ruling, then. Another person's love is not a fact that evidence can settle. It is something you accept or refuse, on grounds that will never be proof. The men who demanded proof got a handkerchief, a spider in a cup, and a daughter's honest nothing. Each destroyed the thing he was trying to be sure of. Refusing to accept was not safer than accepting. In every play where the demand was made of a heart, it was fatal.

So the tragedies of love are caused by a demand for certainty about a heart. The demand is the disease, not a defence against one. Philosophy has a version of this problem, and it is the one modern philosophy started from. How can I be certain of anything outside my own head, another person's mind included. The plays diagnosed that demand, and treated it, before the philosopher who made it famous had written a word of it. What the plays' treatment is, and whether it is any good, comes next.

Part 5: The Disease of Certainty

The plays diagnosed the modern demand for certainty before the philosophers did, and they gave a different answer. The philosophers said the problem is that we know too little, and set out to find something that could not be doubted. The plays say the problem is that we refuse to accept what we already know. They show what that refusal does to the people around us. The two answers are not versions of each other. They point in opposite directions, and one of them has bodies at the end of it.

The philosopher's problem has to be felt before the plays can answer it. In 1641 Descartes, the French philosopher named earlier, published a small book of meditations. He begins by deciding, once in his life, to rid himself of every opinion he has ever held and start again from the foundations. His rule is severe. Anything not entirely certain and indubitable is to be treated exactly as if it were false.91 To make sure nothing slips through, he supposes that some malignant demon, exceedingly potent and deceitful, has employed all his artifice to deceive him. The sky, the earth, colours, sounds, his own hands, may all be the demon's illusions.92 He is looking for one thing the demon cannot touch. He compares himself to Archimedes, who said that with one fixed and immovable point he could move the whole earth. One certain thing, and everything else can be rebuilt on it.93 He finds it in the fact that he is thinking, and so must exist, at least while he thinks. Everything else, including other people, has to be argued back from there.

A second objection is that this is a modern reading, forced onto plays written for other reasons. The answer is that every step above was taken on the plays' own words. The steps. One, the modern philosopher asks for one thing that cannot be doubted and treats everything else as false. Two, applied to a person, that rule turns them into stone, and the plays show it with the word stone. Three, the men who fall in the plays do not lack knowledge, they refuse to let what they know count. Four, the cure shown is faith before sight, acceptance before proof. The plays' answer to the problem of other minds is that it is a problem of acceptance rather than knowledge. The demand for proof is the disease. Which raises a question the plays cannot avoid. Othello wanted his wife's worth to him made certain, fixed, a monument, and the plays say worth cannot be held still like that. But then where does worth live at all. Is anything worth something in itself, or is a thing worth only what someone happens to value it at?. One play puts that question into a soldier's mouth, and the answer he gets is the next part.

Part 6: What Is Aught But As 'Tis Valued

The plays run the oldest argument about worth. Is a thing valuable in itself, or only because someone values it?. They rule against the purest form of the second view. Value is kept up by valuing, the plays agree. But not by any valuing. One man says a thing is worth exactly what he prices it at. The same play shows him unable to keep his own price up when the thing changes. And the man who says the opposite is shown abandoning his own argument, for the sake of honour, and dying of it. The ruling is harder than either speaker wanted, which is how the method works.

The argument is staged as a council of war. Troy has been besieged for seven years over one woman, Helen. The Greeks have offered terms. Give her back and the war ends. Hector, the city's best soldier, argues for giving her back, and his reason is a calculation. Brother, she is not worth what she doth cost the holding. Every tenth soul lost in this war was worth as much as Helen, he says, and they have lost thousands of tenths.108 His younger brother Troilus answers with a question that has been argued about ever since. What is aught, but as 'tis valued?. What is anything worth, except what someone values it at?. Helen is worth a war because they have made her worth a war.109 Nothing is precious in itself. Things are precious because a person prizes them, and the prizing is the whole of the worth. Give that view its due, because it is the view most modern people hold without knowing it. Value is subjective, and a thing is worth what someone will pay.

He did not die of his philosophy. He died of abandoning it, and the play lets the difference show. The play's ruling is that the right view is the one you can keep holding while the world changes. Even the man who saw it could not keep holding it. That is a bleak ruling, and it is the ruling.

The same argument is run again in a different key in a comedy, where everything has a price. In The Merchant of Venice a moneylender agrees to lend three thousand ducats to a man who has insulted him for years. He names his terms as a joke. If the money is not repaid on the day, the forfeit will be an equal pound of your fair flesh. It is to be cut off and taken in what part of your body pleaseth me.116 A man's body, written into a contract as a quantity. The whole play lives in the space that sentence opens. The line is theirs, not his, and it is a sneer at a Jew by men who have never had to count anything.117 But the sneer names what the play is testing. In this city, everything is on one scale, love, flesh, ducats, and the play asks whether anything can be lifted off it.

Now the ruling. Value is neither in the thing alone nor in the will alone. It is a relation between them that has to be kept up. Troilus put all the worth in his wanting and could not survive her change. Hector put it in both and could have survived, and then sold it for honour. Troilus wanted love to be exactly what he priced it at. Both wanted a worth that would sit still, and a worth that sits still is a monument.

The plays hold that worth is made and kept by valuing, but only by a valuing that answers to something in the thing. The man who cannot keep his valuing up has discovered nothing about the thing. He has discovered that he was worshipping his own price. The most valued and most groundless thing in the plays has not yet been tested. Kings believe their crowns are worth something in themselves. The next question is what a crown is actually made of, and what happens to the man who insists on finding out.

Part 7: Idol Ceremony

Power in the plays is a performance that works only while nobody insists on seeing behind it. There is a second half to that. The plays show that it is a performance, and they show why that truth cannot be used. The man who tears the curtain away does not free anyone. He breaks the thing that was holding the weight, and the weight comes down on everybody, for generations.

Start with a king who believes the opposite. Richard the Second has just landed back in his kingdom. His cousin has raised an army and the country has gone over to him. Richard is not worried, and he explains why. Not all the water in the rough rude sea can wash the balm off from an anointed king. The breath of worldly men cannot depose the deputy elected by the Lord. For every soldier his cousin has raised, he says, God has a glorious angel in heavenly pay.123 He believes kingship is a fact about his body, put there by God at his coronation with holy oil, and that no army can touch it. Within the same scene his own army has melted away and he is sitting on the ground. He tells sad stories of the death of kings. For within the hollow crown that rounds the mortal temples of a king keeps Death his court. Death lets a king strut for a little scene, be feared, kill with looks. Then he comes at the last with a little pin and bores through his castle wall, and farewell, king.124 The man who said the oil could not be washed off has, in a few minutes, described the crown as a hollow ring. Death sits inside it. Both speeches are his. He has not learned anything between them. He has swung from one certainty to the other, and the play will now test both.

Part 8: Cheap as Beast's

The plays put the two modern views of nature into combat and rule that neither can be lived. One view says nature is a moral order that punishes the wicked and protects the old. The other says nature is appetite and force, and the strong take what they can. King Lear gives each view to a man, runs them both to the end, and kills the first on stage in its most famous scene. What is left is what human beings add to nature, and the play shows the adding in the one place nobody expects it.

The second view was heard when the method was introduced, in the mouth of the bastard Edmund. Thou, Nature, art my goddess. To thy law my services are bound. By it he takes his brother's inheritance, his father's title, and both of the king's elder daughters.138 For four acts it works. The play will come back to him at the moment he least expects.

Part 9: The Readiness Is All

The plays' answer to death is readiness, and readiness is a harder thing than consolation. It means giving up the demand to own the timing, and refusing to let the ending make the life worthless. It is proved by one man's arc across five acts, and tested against three other answers in a single play. Then it is set beside the classical alternative, which the plays try and reject.

Hamlet's first position on death is fear of the unknown, and he states it in the most famous speech in the language. He is asking why anyone bears a weary life rather than ending it. The answer is the dread of something after death, the undiscovered country from whose bourn no traveller returns. It puzzles the will, and makes us rather bear those ills we have than fly to others that we know not of.157 Thus conscience does make cowards of us all, the line Part 3 counted as his second excuse.158 This is the demand for certainty, turned on the last question. He will not move until he knows what comes after, and nobody can know that, so he does not move.

So the first position is fear of the unknown, and it paralyses, because it demands knowledge of the other side before acting. The classical cure, indifference, is tested in Brutus and fails in one battle. The fullest argument for accepting death is given by a man who knows the death will not happen. It falls off the condemned man the moment hope appears. The plays' own answer is readiness, which means giving up the demand to know or to own the timing, and acting in the hour you have. Readiness is acknowledgment turned toward your own death. Othello could not accept that a wife's love cannot be proved, and Hamlet in his first speech could not accept that death cannot be known. The cure was the same in both cases. Stop demanding the proof, and let what you already know count. What Hamlet already knew was that he was a man who would die, and once he let that count he became able to act.

So the plays meet death with readiness, not consolation, and readiness means acting without the certainty the first speech demanded. That leaves the hardest case of acceptance without proof. A death can be accepted because it comes to everyone. A wrong done to you comes from one person, who chose it. Whether that can be accepted too, and what it costs, is the last experiment.

Part 10: The Rarer Action

The late plays test forgiveness and find that it is the same act as acknowledgment. To forgive, in these plays, is to give up the demand to be proven right. In the hardest cases it is given up before the other person has repented, without proof that they ever will. That is the demand for ocular proof, reversed. The plays show what it costs, because it costs a great deal, and they count every item.

The fullest case is a magician on an island. Twelve years earlier, Prospero was Duke of Milan. His brother Antonio took the dukedom with the help of a neighbouring king, and put Prospero and his small daughter to sea in a rotten boat. Now the same men have been shipwrecked on his island by his magic, and he holds every one of them in his power. He can do anything he likes. Though with their high wrongs I am struck to the quick, yet with my nobler reason against my fury do I take part. The rarer action is in virtue than in vengeance.

Forgiveness in the late plays is acknowledgment, the same act that was named in the storm and at the statue. It means letting what you know about a person count, that he is your brother, that he is one of your kind. It is offered before repentance and without proof of it, and he is never asked to prove he deserves it. Prospero states a condition and then forgives a man who never meets it. The young woman kneels for the man she thinks killed her brother. It is not safe. Antonio may be plotting again tomorrow, and his last line suggests he has not changed. And it is paid for in full, in dead children and lonely women and a duke thinking about his grave. The plays do not pretend otherwise. They show the bill, and then they show the man paying it and forgiving anyway, and they call that the rarer action.

The hardest case of acceptance without proof is forgiving a wrong that one person chose. The late plays run it and pass it, at a cost they count out loud. The forgiven man does not have to speak. The gods do not have to appear. The act is human, and it is the same act that the tragedies showed men refusing to perform. Every experiment has now been run. What they add up to, and what a person who has heard them is supposed to do tomorrow, is all that is left.

Part 11: What He Knew

There is one philosophy in the plays, and it can now be said whole, because every part of it has been run. People are not ruined by what they do not know. They are ruined by refusing to accept what they already know, and the plays show that refusal taking the same shape whatever it is aimed at. A self, a wife, a daughter, a crown, the gods, death, a brother. In each case a man demands that the thing be proved before he will accept it, and the demand destroys the thing. In each case the cure shown is acceptance without proof. That is the claim the whole argument was built to earn, and here is its skeleton.

One. Shakespeare holds no doctrine, and the famous lines belong to characters the plays are pricing. The verdicts live in the plots, in who dies and what happens next, which no reader can bend. Two. There is no inner self beneath the roles. A person is what they do and how they are seen. The men who search their faces for something more find a mirror and a blank. Three. Another person's love is not a fact that evidence can settle. The men who demanded proof of it got a handkerchief, a spider in a cup, and a daughter's honest nothing, and killed what they wanted proved. Four. The modern demand for certainty, one immovable point and everything else treated as false, turns a person into stone when it is aimed at a person. The plays' alternative is acknowledgment, letting what you already know count. Five. Worth is kept up by valuing, but only by a valuing that answers to something in the thing. The man who prices by his own wanting cannot survive the thing changing. Six. Power is ceremony, place, degree and form, and the fiction carries real weight. The man who tears it down to see behind it breaks the floor. Seven. Nature gives no moral order and no justice. The play that tests this hardest puts the hanged daughter's father in the middle of the line promising justice. What makes a life more than cheap is added by people, and it is not rewarded. Eight. Death is met by readiness, which is giving up the demand to know or to own the timing. Readiness frees a man to act in the hour he has. Nine. Forgiveness is acknowledgment aimed at a wrong, offered before repentance and without proof of it, at a cost the plays count aloud.

Nine steps, and one shape under all of them. The demand for proof. Richard, Othello, Lear, Leontes, Hamlet in his first speech, Prospero with his condition. The same man, in six bodies, making the same demand. In the tragedies where it is made, it wins, and the people it was aimed at die. In the late plays a man gives it up and someone lives. Not every tragedy is built on it. Macbeth, and Romeo, and Antony die of other things, and some of them die of trusting. The claim is about what happens when the demand is made, and about that the plays never once vary.

Here is what those plays say to a person who has heard all this and has to go to work tomorrow. It should be said plainly, because the plays are plain about it. Most people have run Lear's love test. Not with a kingdom, but with a question that has the same shape. Prove that you love me. Show me in a way I cannot doubt. And when the honest answer comes back, which is always a bond, no more nor less, it is never enough.191 What was wanted was an unlimited measurement, and there is no such thing. The plays say the question is broken, not the answer. Most people have demanded ocular proof of someone. Not of a wife's fidelity, perhaps, but of a friend's loyalty, a colleague's honesty, a parent's regard. And having demanded it, they have found that the demand itself changed the thing. A loyalty asked to prove itself is already a different loyalty. The plays say that is the mechanism, and never bad luck. Most people have searched for their true self, the one beneath the job and the family and the face in the mirror. The plays say the search ends at a broken mirror, and that this is not a loss, and Part 3 said why. Nobody is being cheated by that. And most people are waiting for an apology before they forgive. The plays' hardest case is a duke who waited for one, said his mercy depended on it, did not get it, and forgave anyway. His brother's only remaining words were about selling a fish. The plays do not say the brother deserved it. They say the rarer action is in virtue than in vengeance. The duke went home to think about his grave one thought in three, and this was still the better ending.

A thoughtful person will raise one last objection at this point. Why go to the plays for any of this?. Philosophers have said it in order. There are books on the self, on other minds, on value, on the state, on death, on forgiveness. They argue their claims step by step instead of hiding them in plots. If the philosophy is real, read the philosophers. If it is only in the plays, it is not philosophy, it is a mood. The answer is that the plays do one thing a treatise cannot, and it is the thing that matters most for these particular claims. A treatise can tell you that the demand for certainty about another person is incoherent. It cannot show you what that costs when a man lives it, in the hour, with the person in front of him. Othello is a demonstration of what happens to a man who requires certainty about love anyway, and no argument that it is impossible. The demonstration includes the sentence where his reasoning turns. It includes the object he accepts instead, and the way he wants her to look when she is dead. No argument delivers that, and the claims in question are claims about what it is like to live a certain way. For claims of that kind, the case is the proof. That is why the method matters and not just the results. The plays do not illustrate a philosophy. They are the form the philosophy has to take. Its subject is what a demand does to a life, and only a life can show that.

Then the limit, conceded in full, because claiming more would be doing the thing the plays warn against. There is no politics of justice in the plays. They show that the fiction of the crown is load-bearing and that tearing it down costs a civil war. They do not say what a just order would look like or how to build one. In the tragedies no god acts, and in the late plays the gods are thanked afterwards for what people did. There is no system. Nothing here tells you what to value, whom to trust, how to rule, or how to die. The plays show the price, and they show one act that avoids paying it. They leave the choice where it was, with the person watching. That is smaller than a philosophy of life. It is also, for the specific claims it makes, harder to argue with than any philosophy of life. The evidence is a body on the floor, and it is there in every reading.

The plays leave three things standing at the end, and they should be left standing here. Cordelia is dead. Nothing about the honesty of her nothing saved her. The play knew it, and put her father over her body in the middle of the sentence that promised justice, so nobody could look away.192 Antonio is unrepentant.193 Forgiven without condition, he spent his last line pricing a slave, and the man who forgave him went home to think about his grave. And the demand is still available. Anyone can run the love test tomorrow. Anyone can ask for the ocular proof. Anyone can go looking in the mirror for the king. The plays cannot stop that. They can only show, four hundred years in advance, what the face in the glass will say when it is asked. And what will be carried in, and by whom, when the asking is done.
