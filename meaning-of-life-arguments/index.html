Cold open

Most people believe there are two answers to the question of what life is for. Either something above us gives a life its point, or we make the point up ourselves. Both fail as they are usually held, and each breaks at a place that can be named exactly. The first is wrong to say that a life can matter only if God gives it a point. The second is wrong to say that a life matters whenever it feels as if it does. What survives is smaller and sturdier than either: a life means something when the person living it loves something worth loving, and does something about it.

The question usually comes at night, as the thought that in a hundred years none of this will matter. That thought is real, but it is not a discovery about your life. It comes from the ability to see a life from far outside. That ability would produce the same thought if God existed and had a plan for everyone. A thought that would come whatever your life was like is no verdict on yours.

Every serious argument about the meaning of life belongs to one of a handful of families. Meaning comes from God or an immortal soul. Meaning is crushed by the size and age of the universe. Life is mostly suffering, so the sum comes out negative. Meaning is whatever fulfils you. Meaning is doing real good, whatever you feel. Life is absurd, and the only honest response is defiance. The hunger for meaning is itself the trouble, and should be let go. Each of these is held by serious people, and each has an argument behind it that deserves its full strength. Each of them gets something right. All but the last fail at a specific point, on a specific case, in a way that can be shown. The last turns out to be an ally.

The cases are not exotic. A woman caring for her dying father. A doctor who saves lives and feels nothing. A man drugged into loving a pointless task. A thought experiment in which the world ends thirty days after your death, and what that would do to the work you care about most. When the families are tested against cases like these, the two popular answers both fall. What is left is an account of meaning that needs neither God's plan nor permanence, and that a believer can hold in full. It also explains why the night thought keeps coming back.

Part 1: The Night Thought

The question that arrives at night is not whether you are happy. It is whether anything you do matters, and those are two different questions. The difference between them has been measured, and it runs in a direction most people would not guess. The night question is also a real question, not a muddle about words. Asked properly, it splits into two questions that need different answers.

The man who asked it harder than almost anyone is a good place to begin. Leo Tolstoy was approaching fifty, the author of War and Peace, and by any outside measure he had won. In his own account of these years, he lists what he had. "I was not yet fifty; I had a good wife who loved me and whom I loved". He goes on to good children and a large estate.1 He was famous, he was unusually strong for his age, and he was still working. And he could not stop asking one question. In his words, "What will come of what I am doing to-day or shall do to-morrow"? And then the wider version. "What will come of my whole life"?2

Most people meet a smaller version of the same thing. A life that is going fine, a job, people you love, things you enjoy. And then some night, with the light off, the thought arrives that in a hundred years none of this will matter. Everyone you know will be gone, and everyone who remembers them will be gone too. Then the morning comes, the day fills up, and the thought goes wherever it goes. What it leaves behind is a suspicion. Maybe the night is when you see clearly, and the day is how you avoid looking.

Before any answer can be tested, the question has to be pinned down, because it is easy to confuse it with a neighbour. The neighbour is happiness. It is natural to assume that a meaningful life and a happy life are the same thing described twice. Consider two lives that differ on that one point.

The first is a man who retires at sixty with enough money and moves somewhere warm. He swims in the morning, eats well, sleeps well, and is asked for nothing by anyone. He is, by every report he gives, content. The second is a woman of the same age who spends those years caring for her father. He is dying slowly of a disease that takes his memory first. She is tired, often frightened, and short of sleep and of money. Which of them is happier is an easy question. Which of them is living a life that matters is a harder question, and the honest answer leans toward her. That hesitation is the distinction. There is a thing her life has that his lacks, and it is not pleasure.

That thing is what the word meaning points at here. Not how a life feels from the inside, moment by moment, but whether it amounts to something, whether it has a point. The two often travel together. But the woman and the retired man show that they can come apart, and if they can come apart, they are two things.

A team of psychologists tested whether the two come apart in real lives, and not only in made-up ones. There is a natural guess about what they found. Ask a few hundred adults how happy their lives are, and how meaningful. Would the two answers track each other? Most people would guess that they track each other closely. And they did. In an online study of about four hundred adults, people who rated their lives as happy mostly rated them as meaningful too. The overlap was large.5

There is an objection that would stop everything before it starts, and it needs to be heard properly. Words have meanings. A sentence means something, a road sign means something, a raised hand at an auction means something. In each case, meaning is what a sign stands for. A life is not a sign. It does not stand for anything. So asking what your life means is like asking what a mountain means, or a Tuesday. On this view the question is not hard. It is broken. It uses a word outside the only place the word works, and the feeling of depth is just the feeling of grammar going wrong.

Some people say exactly this, usually with relief. The answer to it is in how the word is actually used. When someone says a visit from an old friend meant a great deal to them, nobody thinks the visit was a sign that stood for something. When someone calls their job meaningless, nobody hears that the job has no definition. In both cases "meaning" means point, worth, significance, mattering. It is the ordinary word for whether something is worth the doing. That is a perfectly sound question to ask about a job, a visit, or a life. So the objection is right about road signs and wrong about the question. The night thought uses meaning in the sense of mattering, and that sense has never been confused.

What the objection does get right is that the question needs splitting, because two different questions hide inside it. The first is about the whole show. Is there a purpose behind the universe, or behind the human story, something it is all for? Call it the question about the whole. The second is about one life inside the show. Did your grandmother's life amount to something? Is yours amounting to something now? Call it the question about one life.

The two questions can get different answers. The universe might have no purpose at all, and a particular life inside it might still be well worth having lived. Or the universe might have a grand purpose, and a particular life might contribute nothing to it. A small example makes the gap plain. A hospital has a purpose, which is to heal the sick. A porter in that hospital might still live a pointless, wasted life. And a beach has no purpose at all. Yet the person who pulls a drowning child out of the sea beside it has done something that matters by any measure.

Now look at what the night thought does with these two questions. It starts from something about the whole show: none of this will last, the universe does not care. And it ends with something about one life: so mine amounts to nothing. That is a step from the first question to the second, and it is a step that needs an argument. It might be a good step. But it is not obvious, and it is usually taken without anyone noticing it was taken.

Tolstoy's own question shows the step clearly. A little further on in his account, he puts it in the form he could not get past. "Is there any meaning in my life that the inevitable death awaiting me does not destroy"?10 That sentence connects the two questions with a theory. It says a life can only matter if something about it survives the end. If that theory is true, then the night thought wins whenever nothing survives. If it is false, the night thought has to find another argument.

Three things are now in place. The night question is about mattering, not about pleasure, and mattering and pleasure can be told apart, both in made-up cases and in real ones. The question is not a confusion of words. And it contains two questions, one about the whole and one about a single life, joined by a step that most people never examine. The oldest way of taking that step says a life can matter only if something above gives it a purpose. And something after death must keep it. That is exactly what Tolstoy feared he lacked. The next question is whether a life really needs God, or an immortal soul, in order to matter at all.

Part 2: Meaning From Above

God and an immortal soul could add to the meaning of a life. But a life does not need God's plan, or an afterlife, in order to matter. And neither of them, if they exist, would silence the night thought. One thing needs saying before the argument starts. This is an argument about what meaning requires, and it says nothing either way about whether God exists.

The view being tested is the oldest answer, and its best defenders state it with real force. One of the best-known modern versions comes from a Christian philosopher who argues that a godless world is absurd. His central question is short. "If each individual person passes out of existence when he dies". That is the condition. The question that follows is, "then what ultimate meaning can be given to his life"?11 He is careful with that word ultimate. He does not deny that people feel their lives matter. His claim is that without God, the feeling has nothing under it.

Its structure is simple. First, without God, nothing we do lasts, and every life ends in the same silence. Second, what does not last, and serves nothing that lasts, cannot finally matter. Third, only a purpose set by a creator can give a life a point beyond itself. So without a creator and without an afterlife, no life has an ultimate point. This is Tolstoy's question from his crisis, given a structure. He asked whether any meaning in his life would survive the death waiting for him. The argument answers that only God could make it survive.

This defender is careful about the second step. He argues that endless life without God would be just as empty, only longer. So lasting matters to him only as part of God's purpose, and the weight of his case rests there.12 That makes it the strongest form of the view, and the one worth testing.

The first trouble is with the idea of being made for a purpose. Consider two different ways a thing can have a purpose. A hammer has a purpose. Somebody made it to drive nails, and it is a good hammer when it does that well. A woman training for a marathon also has a purpose. She has chosen a goal, and her days are organised around it. These are opposite relations. The hammer's purpose belongs to someone else and is placed on it from outside. The runner's purpose is hers, because she is the one who holds it. When people ask whether their life has a purpose, they mean the runner's kind. The theory being tested offers them the hammer's kind.

There is a sharp case that shows why the hammer's kind is not enough. The American philosopher Thomas Nagel asks us to suppose something about our origins. Suppose we learned that human beings had been bred as food for some other creatures.13 Those creatures raised us for a reason, and one day they will collect us. Our lives would then have a purpose, set by beings far more powerful than we are. It is hard to see how this news would make anyone's life meaningful. It would make it horrifying. Being part of someone else's plan does not, by itself, give your life any worth at all.

The believer has a fair reply to that. God's purpose is not like the purpose of a farmer. God is good, and his plan for a person is that person's own good: to love, to be known, to flourish. That is not being used. That is being cared for. The reply is right, and it concedes more than it seems to. The purpose is now valuable because of what it is for, which is the person's good. The worth sits in the love, the flourishing and the being known. The fact that someone assigned the purpose has stopped doing the work. What does the work is that the purpose aims at something good.

The second trouble follows straight from that. Nagel puts it in one sentence. "A role in some larger enterprise cannot confer significance". The condition he attaches is the whole point: "unless that enterprise is itself significant".14 A part in a play matters only if the play does. A job in a company matters only if the company is doing something worth doing. So when a life borrows its point from God's purpose, the question is handed upward. Does God's purpose matter, and what makes it matter? If the answer is another, larger purpose, the same question goes up again. Borrowing never pays the debt. At some point, something has to matter in itself, not because it serves something further.

Here the believer has a much better move. God, on this view, is exactly that point. God is perfectly good, and perfect goodness is the one thing whose worth needs no further support. Asking what perfect goodness is for is like asking what the end of a road leads to. The chain of borrowing stops at God, and only at God.

That move is sound in its shape, and it is worth accepting the shape. Chains of borrowed worth have to stop somewhere, at something valuable in itself. Where they actually stop, in an ordinary day, is worth seeing. A father pulls his small daughter's hand away from a hot stove. Why? So she will not be burned. And why does that matter? The question has no grip. Her not being in pain is not good because it serves something larger. It is good, full stop, and the chain ends there, inside an ordinary kitchen.

Nagel makes the same observation in general terms. "Chains of justification come repeatedly to an end within life". And he adds that whether the whole process can be justified "has no bearing on the finality of these end-points".15 A child kept from harm, a friend kept from despair, a fact found out after years of work. These are places where the question of what something is for simply stops.

What it does not say
Nagel's own point here rejects the plain infinite-regress argument for absurdity; it is used in the video to support that chains of worth can end inside ordinary life, which is a different use of the same passage.

Set the two positions side by side. The believer showed that chains of worth can end in something valuable in itself. That is right. But it was never shown that they can end only in God. Once you grant that some things are good in themselves, the hand pulled from the stove qualifies. God may be the greatest of those things. He is not the only place where the chain can stop.

The believer has one more reply, and it is the deepest. The child's relief is good, they say, only because God makes goodness real. Without God, nothing is good in itself, and in the end it makes no difference whether a person lived as a saint or as a tyrant. That reply changes the subject. It no longer says that meaning needs God's plan. It says that goodness itself needs God, which is an older and larger argument about what goodness is. That argument is not settled here, and nothing here pretends otherwise. If goodness itself needs God, then so does every account of meaning, this one included. What is settled is narrower. A life does not need God's plan, or endless time, in order to matter. And believer and unbeliever agree about which things in a life matter, even where they disagree about why.

The third trouble is with living forever, and it is quick. Suppose some activity is pointless when you do it for an hour. Doing it for a year does not give it a point. Doing it for a billion years only gives you a billion years of pointlessness. Nagel asks the question this way. He takes a life that is absurd when it lasts seventy years. Would it not be "infinitely absurd if it lasted through eternity"?16 Length multiplies whatever a life already is. It cannot turn nothing into something. The believer's own best defender agrees, which is why his case rested on God and not on endless time.

Nagel's point is conditional (IF already absurd, length does not cure it), not a claim that eternal life is in fact worthless.

Laid end to end, the argument has four steps. One: being made for a purpose, by itself, gives a life no worth, as the case of humans bred for food shows. Two: a purpose matters only if what it serves matters, so borrowed worth has to stop somewhere. Three: it does stop, at things good in themselves, and those are found inside ordinary lives. Four: endless duration multiplies worth and pointlessness alike, and creates neither. So God and an afterlife could add to the meaning of a life, by adding more things worth caring about. But a life without them is not left with nothing. What a believer gives up here is one claim: that God's plan is the only possible source of a life's meaning.

That leaves the night thought itself. For someone certain that God exists and has a plan, it would still not be silenced, and the reason is already on the table. The same voice that asks what your life is for can ask what God's plan is for. The believer can answer that God's goodness needs no further reason. But anyone who can accept that about God can accept the same of a child spared a burn. And someone in the grip of the night thought tends to feel neither. The night thought is not stopped by adding a larger frame. Where it comes from, if not from a missing God, is a question that will need its own answer later.

So the first answer fails in its strong form. A life does not need God's plan or an immortal soul in order to matter. And if a life seems pointless, more time is not the cure. Whether shortness can take away a point a life already has is a separate question, and it returns when death is faced directly. But there is another version of the night thought that has nothing to do with God. It looks at the size of the universe and the length of its history, and it sets one human life beside them. The question is whether, against something that large and that old, a single life is simply too small and too brief to matter.

Part 3: Too Small, Too Brief

The argument from the size and age of the universe fails, because size and length were never what made anything matter. But it points at something real, and that should be granted in full. There is a point of view, one that takes nobody's side, from which no human life stands out at all. That is a genuine lack. But what that view lacks is a place to see worth from, not the worth itself.

This version of the night thought needs no God and no theology. It needs only what physics now says about where everything is going. Our own star is the nearest case. Over billions of years, the sun will grow brighter and then swell. Long before the end, it will make the Earth too hot for anything like the life on it now. Later still, the sun becomes a red giant.17 Two astronomers who modelled this in detail put the result in one flat sentence. "We find that planet Earth will not be able to escape engulfment".18 The planet itself will be swallowed.

Set one human life against that. Seventy or eighty years, on one planet, near one ordinary star. The night thought draws its conclusion from the comparison. We are too small and too brief, and whatever we do is lost in a place that size. Many people find this the most honest form of the doubt, because it asks you to believe nothing except the science.

The trouble shows up when the two features, size and length, are changed one at a time. Take size first. Suppose the universe were the size of a house, and you were one of its largest objects. The question is whether the things you do now would matter more. The work, the people, the small kindnesses. It is hard to see why they would. Nagel, the philosopher of the humans bred as food, puts the point exactly. "And if our lives are absurd given our present size, why would they be any less absurd if we filled the universe". He adds the two ways it could happen: because we were larger, or because the universe was smaller.20

What it does not say
Nagel raises size and brevity in order to reject them as the source of absurdity, not to endorse them.

Length works the same way, and the case of eternity already showed it. A life that lacked a point at seventy years would not gain one by lasting forever. Duration multiplies what is there and creates nothing. So the complaint that we are too brief assumes that more time would help, and the case of eternity showed that it would not.

That leaves very little of the argument. If changing the size changes nothing, size was never doing the work. If changing the length changes nothing, length was never doing the work either. The comparison with the cosmos felt like an argument. It was really a mood, the dizziness that comes from setting one small thing beside one very large thing.

A plain case makes the point concrete. A nurse on a night shift notices that an old man on her ward is frightened. She sits with him for twenty minutes, though she is behind on her rounds, and he falls asleep calmer. What made that matter? It mattered because of what it did for him. A frightened man was less afraid. Nothing in that depends on how many galaxies there are. If the universe were a thousand times larger, he would have been exactly as comforted, and she exactly as kind.

Now the same case, stripped down. Any act whose worth lies in what it does for someone keeps that worth, whatever the size of the space around it. The nurse and the old man can be swapped for a teacher and a struggling pupil, or a stranger and a lost child. Stated generally, worth does not depend on how large a thing is relative to everything else.

That is the weak form of the cosmic argument, and it fails. The strong form concedes everything just said about size. Its complaint is about point of view. The nurse's act mattered to the old man, to her, and perhaps to his family. Every one of those is somebody's point of view. The strong form asks how human lives look from a standpoint that takes nobody's side at all. And it answers that, from there, nothing human stands out.

That standpoint is not imaginary. People can actually take it, and the night thought is what taking it feels like. From there, a nurse's twenty minutes are a small event on a small planet, no more important than a stone rolling down a hill. There is nothing in physics that cares, and nothing in the far future that will remember. It is not a mistake to feel that as a loss.

But it is fair to ask what that view is able to see. Mattering, every time it shows up, involves someone. A thing matters to someone, or it is good for someone, or it is a truth someone found or a beautiful thing someone made. A standpoint that takes nobody's side still sees the nurse's act, but only as a very small event among countless others. It has set aside the someone for whom the act mattered, so it can see the size of the act and not its mattering. So when nothing stands out from there, that is not a finding that nothing matters. It is what any view looks like once the places where mattering happens have been taken out of it.

An analogy helps, with its limit marked. A man sends an anonymous letter to a friend in despair, and it carries the friend through a bad winter. Nobody else will ever know. Seen from a sorting office, it is one envelope among millions. Seen from the friend's life, it is the thing that kept him going. Both views are accurate, and only one of them is placed to see what the letter did. The limit is that a postal clerk could at least open the letter, while the view from nowhere has stepped back from every reader there is.

One more thing needs saying about Nagel. Nagel, who takes apart the argument from size and length, does not conclude that life is free of absurdity. He thinks there really is something absurd about human life. He just thinks it comes from somewhere else, from something we do, and not from the size of space. That other source is the real engine of the night thought, and it needs its own hearing. For now, the point is narrower. The size of the universe and the length of its future are not, by themselves, a reason to think a life is worthless.

Put together, the cosmic argument leaves this behind. The cosmos is enormous and will outlast every trace of us, and that is simply true. But making it smaller, or making us longer lived, would not give anything a point it lacked. Worth lives in what a thing does for someone, or in what someone finds or makes, and that is untouched by the scale of the room. What the cosmic picture really shows is how a life looks from a view that takes nobody's side. That view is real, and what it lacks is a place to see worth from, not the worth itself.

That moves the question inward. The next argument sets the universe aside and looks only at a life as it is lived, its pains and its pleasures, from the inside. Some of the sharpest thinkers who ever wrote about this added up that ledger honestly. Their verdict was that the total comes out negative, and that a life is mostly suffering broken up by boredom. Whether they were right, and whether it would settle the question of meaning if they were, comes next.

Part 4: The Pessimist's Ledger

The strongest pessimist argument is an argument about the balance of pain and pleasure in a life. It is stronger than most people admit, and the measured evidence makes it stronger still, not weaker. But it is an argument about happiness. And because meaning is not happiness, it cannot settle the question of meaning, even if every word of it is true.

There are two old pessimisms, and they should be kept apart. The first is very ancient. It is the voice of the book of Ecclesiastes, a teacher looking back over a lifetime of work. Run it on an ordinary case. A woman wants a promotion for two years. Those two years are tense, because she does not have it. She gets it, and for a week she is delighted. So the ledger, fairly kept, reads like this. Schopenhauer is right that much of life is spent wanting, that getting often disappoints, and that the pleasure of a gain fades. He may even be right that many lives contain more suffering than joy, once everything is counted. None of that needs to be denied. Not all of it, since finding a truth or making something is not plainly a relief. The real question is what follows from the ledger, and that is where the argument goes wrong.

What follows is a verdict on how a life feels. It is a verdict on happiness. And the opening distinction of this whole question was that meaning and happiness are two different things. The woman nursing her father through a long dementia had years that were, on any ledger of feelings, mostly painful. She was tired, frightened and grieving in advance. If the pendulum theory is right, her few good moments were only relief. And yet her life in those years is the clearest case of a life that mattered.

A follower of Schopenhauer has a reply ready, and it is a clever one. Suppose, years later, she looks back and feels that those years were the most meaningful of her life. That feeling, the follower would say, is just one more satisfaction. It is the want to have done right by her father, finally quiet. It is negative, like every other pleasure, and so it proves nothing about meaning either.

The reply is right about the feeling and misses the point. What made her years matter was not a pleasant feeling inside her, then or later. It was a fact about her father. He was washed, fed, talked to and not left alone. That happened to him, whatever was happening in her.

Her caring counts too, but caring here is not a pleasure on the ledger. It is her being turned toward him and acting on it, and what mattered was both her care and what it did for him. The follower could press further and call his comfort only relief. Grant it. Relief of a real pain, given by one person to another, is still a change worth making. No pessimist thinks it indifferent whether he is left in pain. Even if every feeling she ever had was only pain and relief, the fact stays where it is. The ledger counts her feelings. Meaning, in the sense the night thought is asking about, was never in the ledger at all.

The hardest test of that claim comes from the camps. Viktor Frankl, a psychiatrist who survived several of them, remembered men who walked through the huts comforting others and giving away their last piece of bread. From them he drew the claim that everything can be taken from a person except one thing, "the last of the human freedoms". Of the men who gave away their bread, he admitted that "they may have been few in number".30 And his testimony comes from survivors, which is not the same as everyone. So the pessimist wins a real point, and loses the argument that matters here. The point won is about happiness. Pleasure fades, wanting returns, and some losses never heal, and a sober person should expect all three. The argument lost is about meaning. A life can be mostly painful and still be full of meaning, because meaning is not a sum of feelings.

There is one limit on that. For Tolstoy, hiding his cord, the night question had become whether to go on living at all. Meaning can be a reason to go on even when the ledger of feelings runs negative. Whether it always outweighs that ledger is a further question, and nothing here settles it. What the ledger cannot decide is whether what a person does matters.

That leads straight to the answer most people now give. If meaning is not the sum of your feelings, perhaps it is one particular feeling, the sense of being fulfilled. On this view, your life has meaning when it feels meaningful to you, and nobody else gets a say. It is the most popular answer of the age, and it needs testing next.

Part 5: Make Your Own Meaning

The most popular answer of our time says that a life has meaning when it feels meaningful to the person living it. There is nothing above to consult, so the only judge is you. This answer fails, and it fails on cases its own defenders cannot accept. Feeling fulfilled is part of what meaning is, but it is not the whole of it.

The ruling on the popular view can now be given. It is right that feeling matters. A life that its own owner experiences as empty is missing something, and the next answer will show how much. That pushes the question outward, toward what a life is actually doing. If meaning needs something worth doing, perhaps that is all it needs. Part 6: Meaning Without Feeling

The rival answer says that a life has meaning to the degree that it does real good or reaches real value, whatever its owner feels. It gets right exactly what the feeling view got wrong. But it fails on a different kind of life, the life of someone doing valuable work with no heart in it. And it has to answer two fair charges. One is that it ranks lives like a prize committee, and the other is that nobody has the authority to say what is worth doing.

Put fairly, the view starts from things almost nobody doubts. Some activities are worth doing whatever anyone happens to feel about them. Relieving pain is one. Finding out what is true is another. Making something beautiful, and raising a child well, are others. One contemporary philosopher sorts the places where meaning is found into three old headings. He calls them the good, the true and the beautiful. By those he means kindness and morality, knowledge and wisdom, and art and creativity.41 A life spent on any of these is pointed at something real, something whose worth does not depend on anyone's mood.

Two doctors work in the same emergency department for thirty years. They save roughly the same number of lives, with the same skill, and their patients do equally well. The first cares about the work. She is moved by it, proud of it, and sometimes kept awake by it. The second burned out in his fifth year and never recovered. He still does the job well. If the objective view were the whole truth, those two lives would be equally meaningful, because they achieve the same value. That is hard to believe. What differs is his relation to it, and that relation is the thing the objective view leaves out.

And stated generally, value without the person's engagement is incomplete. It is the mirror image of what went wrong with the copyist, who had the engagement and nothing worth engaging with.

Before joining the two halves, the objective view has two charges to answer, and they are serious. The first is elitism. If meaning comes from doing good, finding truth or making beauty, it sounds as if only great scientists, artists and saints qualify. Everyone else is a supporting player in someone else's story.

That charge would sink the view if it were true, and it is not. The value it asks for is not rare and not large. Wolf is explicit that the range of worthwhile things is very wide. It takes in, in her words, "both positive relationships with family and friends and engagement with political and social causes". And she adds that "the range extends far beyond that".46 Keeping one friend through a bad decade is worth caring about. So is raising one child, nursing one parent, or making one small business honest. The test is the worth of the thing, not its size. A life does not need to change history to be pointed at something good.

What it does not say
The earlier wording "more confident about particular judgments than we are about any theory of value" is not in the book; this is the sentence that makes the point.

That is how most knowledge works. Nobody has a perfect theory of what makes an argument sound, and everyone can still tell that some arguments are better than others. Nobody can define health in a sentence, and everyone knows that a broken leg is not it. Likewise, nobody needs a theory of value to know that a friend is worth more of your life than a goldfish. The clear cases are clearer than any theory that could be built to explain them. A theory is tested against them, not the other way round. And where the cases are not clear, it is honest to say so, and to admit that any particular judgement could be wrong.

There is a middle view between one person's taste and a worth beyond all of us. It looks like the way out of the whole dispute. It says that worth is what people would agree on, if they thought about it carefully together. On this view, a friend is worth more than a goldfish because nearly anyone who thought it through would say so. And that agreement is all the worth there is.

The view has real strengths. It explains why the clear cases feel clear: they are the ones that careful people come to agree on. It explains how the goldfish lover can be wrong without any appeal to God: she is out of step with what careful people would judge. And it keeps worth human, which is where the night ward and the father's bedside said it lives.

Two cases test it. In the first, a whole society agrees, after long and careful argument, that a duel to the death is the honourable answer to an insult. Young men die every year defending their names, and everyone thinks it glorious. In the second, the same society, two generations later, agrees that this was a waste of young lives. On the plainest version of the middle view, both verdicts were right in their time. The duels really were glorious while everyone agreed, and stopped being glorious when the agreement changed. That is very hard to accept. The natural thing to say is that the first generation was wrong, and the second found it out.

That is the trouble in its general form. If agreement makes worth, a whole society can never be mistaken about what is worth caring about. Whatever it agrees on is worth caring about, by definition. But societies do seem to discover that they were wrong, about cruelty, about who counts, about what deserves a life. To discover you were wrong, there has to be something you were wrong about.

A careful defender has a reply. The duelling society was wrong, they would say, because it had not reflected well enough: it never counted the dead men's side. That is a better version of the view, and it raises the deciding question, which is what makes some reflection the right kind. The only good answer is that good reflection tracks something it can get wrong. That something is worth.

So the middle view gets one thing right and one thing wrong. What careful people agree on is the best evidence anyone has about what is worth caring about, and nothing better is on offer. But evidence is not the same as the thing it is evidence for. Agreement is how worth is found, not what makes it.

Two things are not claimed here. First, nothing here says what finally makes worth real, whether God, human nature, or something else again. Believers and their critics can go on arguing about that. The claim is only that in the clear cases, worth is not one person's taste. Second, the goldfish lover herself may disagree, and she is not obliged to accept a vote. What can be offered her is reasons, not a majority. A goldfish can be cared for, but there is little in it to understand, to learn from, or to grow by. A friend, a child or a piece of music has far more in it to meet. That difference is a fact about the things, not a vote.

So here is what the objective view has earned, and what it has not. It is right that meaning needs something worth caring about outside the feeling. It survives the charge of elitism, because worth is everywhere in ordinary lives. It survives the charge of arbitrariness, because the clear cases rest on reasons anyone can check. But on its own, it cannot explain the burned-out doctor. Value without engagement is missing something, exactly as engagement without value was.

So there are now two failed halves. Feeling alone gave a fulfilled Sisyphus a meaningful life, which was wrong. Value alone gave the burned-out doctor a meaningful life, which was also wrong. Each half is missing precisely what the other one has. The obvious next question is whether the two halves can be joined, and whether the joined account survives the cases that broke each of them.

Part 7: When Love Meets Worth

The account that survives both failures joins the two halves. A life has meaning when the person living it loves something that is actually worth loving, and is able to do something about it. Feeling alone was not enough, and value alone was not enough. Where the two meet in action, every hard case so far comes out the way careful judgement says it should.

The clearest statement of this account belongs to Susan Wolf. "On this conception, meaning in life arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness, and one is able to do something about it or with it".49 Subjective attraction means that you are drawn to the thing, that you care about it. Objective attractiveness means that the thing is worth being drawn to. She also gives it in a shorter form, which is easier to carry. "A life is meaningful insofar as it is actively and lovingly engaged in projects of worth".50 Projects here is a broad word. It covers a friendship, a child, a craft, a cause, a garden, anything a person can give themselves to.

The account has three parts, and each one is doing work. The first is love, in a wide sense: caring about something, being moved by it, wanting it to go well. The second is worth: the thing cared about deserves the care. The third is action: the person is not only admiring the thing from a distance, but doing something with it or for it.

The third part is easy to miss, so it needs its own pair of cases. One woman cares deeply about the old people in her town who live alone. She reads about them, worries about them, and talks about how sad it is. Another woman cares about them just as much, and spends one evening a week visiting two of them. The object is the same, and so is the love. Only one of them is engaged. It seems plain at once that the second woman's care has entered her life in a way the first woman's has not. Love that never touches the world is a feeling about a thing, not a life with it.

Now the account can be run against every case so far. The drugged Sisyphus has love, and he certainly has action. What he lacks is worth, since a stone pushed up a hill forever does nothing for anyone. So his life fails, just as the hesitation said it should. The copyist of War and Peace fails the same way. The burned-out doctor has worth and action, because the good he does is real. What he lacks is love, so his life is missing something, and the account says exactly what.

The emergency doctor who still cares passes on all three. So does the woman nursing her father through his long decline, from the very first case. She loves him, his care is worth giving, and she is the one doing it, night after night. That is why her life in those years seemed so clearly to matter, even when it was painful and even when it was not happy.

Wolf has one more case that shows the account from the zero end. She calls it the Blob. It is, in her words, "a life lived at a not unpleasant level of consciousness, but unconnected to anyone or anything, going nowhere, achieving nothing".51 Think of years spent half asleep in front of a screen, not suffering and not caring. The Blob has no love, no worthy object and no action. It fails all three tests at once, which is why it is the purest picture of a meaningless life that anyone has drawn. It is worse than Sisyphus, because even the drugged Sisyphus cared about something.

One last objection is about vagueness. It concerns the borderline cases: a life given to chess, or to collecting rare stamps, or to a sports team. The account does not hand down a sharp verdict on each of these, and it should not pretend to. Wolf's formula says a life is meaningful insofar as it is engaged in projects of worth, and insofar means that meaning comes in amounts. A chess player who teaches children the game is doing more than one who only plays. So the account does compare lives, as the chess case shows. The result can be put briefly. Meaning is loving engagement with something worth loving, and it needs all three parts: the love, the worth, and the doing. It survives the drugged Sisyphus, the burned-out doctor, and the strongest objection from the other side. But it leaves one case unexplained, and it is the case this whole question started with.

Tolstoy is the hard case. He had work that most people would call worth doing, and he was active in it, writing, running an estate and raising a family. He had loved his wife and children, and by his own account he still did. Yet his account reads as if the caring itself had gone flat, while nothing outside had changed. So the account explains part of his case: the love half was failing. What it does not explain is why it failed, in a life where nothing had gone wrong. A group of thinkers in the last century gave an answer. The demand for meaning, they argued, collides with a world that answers nothing, and that collision is the real name of the problem.

Part 8: The Absurd

Albert Camus was right that the human demand for reasons meets a world that gives none. He was right, too, to refuse both despair and the comfort of a leap into faith. His famous answer is that revolt gives life its value, and the argument here is that a stance cannot be the whole of it. And a sharper critic showed that the clash Camus described does not sit between us and the world at all. It sits inside us, which is why nothing in the world could ever end it.

Camus's essay on the absurd starts from the felt experience that Tolstoy described, the moment when the ordinary routine of life suddenly asks why. A person wants reasons. In Camus's words, "He feels within him his longing for happiness and for reason".56 And the world does not answer. Then comes the sentence the whole essay rests on. "The absurd is born of this confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world".57

That leaves the question of what exactly the step back is, and why no answer, however large, could ever make it stop.

Part 9: The Step Back

Every adult can do something a mouse cannot: step outside their own life and look at it as if it were someone else's. The night thought comes from that power. No answer could ever silence that thought, because the same power can step back from any answer, including God's. And a doubt that would arise whatever your life was like is no verdict on yours. So the night thought is real, and it is the price of a faculty, not a discovery about your life.

Tolstoy shows the power at work more clearly than anyone. In the years of his crisis, he could not think about any of his plans without the same question arriving. He would think about his estate, and about how much land and how many horses he would one day own. And then, in his words, came the reply, "and what then?72" The question had no particular target. It fell on the estate, on his children's education, on his fame as a writer, on everything. Whatever he set before it, it asked the same thing.

Two arguments from earlier used this same move, and how they used it matters. The first showed that a purpose borrowed from God only hands the question upward. Stripped to its steps, the argument is short. One: we can see our lives from inside, where things matter, and from outside, where they look accidental. Two: there is no frame so large that we cannot step outside it, God's included. Three: so no fact about the world could stop the outside view from being taken. The fourth step carries the most weight, and it needs a picture. Consider two smoke alarms. The first goes off when there is smoke in the kitchen. The second goes off at random, whether or not anything is burning, day and night. When the first one sounds, you should look for a fire. When the second one sounds, you have learned nothing at all about your kitchen. An alarm that would ring whatever the conditions carries no information about the conditions.

The night thought behaves like the second alarm. It would arrive whether your life were full of love and worth or empty of both. It arrived for Tolstoy before anything in his outward life had gone wrong. It would arrive for a saint, and the believer had no protection from it either. So its arrival is no sign that your particular life is pointless. It is a sign that you are the kind of creature who can take the step. A pessimist could reply that a thought which comes in every possible world might simply be true in every world. But then it is a claim about all lives at once, and it needs an argument of its own. Each argument of that kind has been tested here, and none of them showed that lives are worthless.

The analogy has an edge, and it should be marked. The random alarm is broken, and it shows nothing true. The step back is not broken. It shows something real: that your concerns are yours, that they could have been different, that the universe did not choose them. What it does not show is that they are worthless. The view from outside is accurate about how accidental your life is. It is silent about whether what you do matters. Mattering only shows up inside human lives, where someone is helped, or something is found or made.

Part 10: Let the Question Go

The oldest answer from the East does not try to win the argument with the night thought. It says the hunger behind the thought is the trouble: the need for your life to last, to count, to be secured. Let that hunger go, and the torment ends without the question ever being answered. This answer is right about the hunger. It goes wrong only if it is heard as a reason to stop caring, because the same tradition asks for care without limit.

The answer starts from a diagnosis. In his first sermon, as the early texts record it, the Buddha named the cause of suffering. And it is always "seeking satisfaction now here, now there". The same passage lists what the thirst is for. It is a craving for pleasures, a craving for life to go on, and a craving for success.79 And it names the way out, which is the ending of that thirst: letting it go, and harbouring it no longer.80

It is that engagement with the scorekeeping taken out. What remains is the thing the step back fixes on more often than anything else, which is death. The question is whether death destroys the meaning of a life, or whether, as some argue, it is what gives a life its meaning.

Part 11: Death

Death does not erase the meaning of a life, because what was done stays done. Death is not what gives a life its meaning either, though it can sharpen one. And the famous argument that living forever would be unbearable is weaker than its fame. What it does show is that an endless life would stay meaningful only for someone who never ran out of things worth loving.

Tolstoy's question returns here, because this is where it was always aimed. He asked whether any meaning in his life would survive "the inevitable death awaiting me". The question assumes that death reaches backward in time. When a life ends, whatever it amounted to is cancelled, as if the ending were a kind of erasing.

Two cases test that assumption. A string quartet plays a piece of music in a small hall, beautifully, to an audience of two hundred people. The piece ends, as every piece ends, and nobody leaves thinking the music was worthless because it stopped. Now change one thing, and suppose the hall burns down that night, with the only recording of the performance inside it. The music is gone in a stronger sense now. And yet it is still true that two hundred people heard it, and that for an hour it was played well.

Neither the ending nor the loss reaches back and makes the performance not have happened. That is the plain fact death cannot touch. The nurse sat with the frightened old man on the night ward, and the woman cared for her father through his decline. When each of them dies, it will remain true, forever, that those things were done. Death ends a future. It has no power over a past, and the past is where everything a life has done is kept.

Frankl, the psychiatrist from the camps, built part of his thinking on exactly this point. People look only at what he called "the stubble field of transitoriness", the bare field left after a harvest. They overlook "the full granaries of the past", where a life has stored its deeds, its joys and its sufferings once and for all.85 His conclusion was blunt. "I should say having been is the surest kind of being".86 That is one man's testimony, shaped by what he lived through, and it is not a proof. Frankl claims more than the quartet showed, that the past is stored and kept. The argument here needs only the smaller point, that what happened stays true.

That is the rule found when eternity was first tested. If a life is full of things worth loving, more of it is more of that. If it is empty, more of it is more emptiness. So neither death nor deathlessness decides whether a life means anything. The love and the worth inside it decide that, at whatever length it runs.

Death, then, leaves the account of meaning exactly where it was. It cannot reach back and cancel what a life did, and memory is not what makes a past real. A deadline can sharpen a life without being the source of its worth. And an endless life would be neither guaranteed meaning nor condemned to boredom. It would mean what its loves meant. If a life's meaning does not need its owner to survive death, there may still be a survival it does need. The surprising answer is that it needs other people to survive, and to keep going long after its owner is gone.

Part 12: The Afterlife You Actually Need

Much of what makes a life matter to the person living it depends on something other than that person surviving death. It depends on other people surviving theirs. In a precise and limited sense, the future of strangers matters more to us than our own survival. That is the true thing hidden inside the night thought. The thought worries about whether you will last, when what a life leans on is whether others will.

The argument comes from the American philosopher Samuel Scheffler, and it starts with a thought experiment he himself calls crude and morbid. Suppose you knew that you would live a normal life, to a normal age, and die in the ordinary way. But suppose you also knew one more thing. In his words, "the earth would be completely destroyed thirty days after your death in a collision with a giant asteroid".95

Before going on, it is worth making a guess. Nothing in your own life changes. Every year that would have been lived is still lived, by you and by everyone you love who would have died first anyway. The end comes after you are gone, and you will never see it. The question is how much this knowledge would change the way a person spends their remaining years. Most people's first answer is very little. It is sad, of course, but it happens after death, and nothing after death can touch the dead.

Scheffler's answer is that it would change a great deal, and he goes through what would change. Start with the work whose payoff lies in the future. "This is true, for example, of much research in science, technology, and medicine". His example is a researcher trying to find a cure for cancer.96 That work makes sense because of the people who would be cured in the decades after the researcher is gone. Remove those people, and the reason to spend thirty years in the laboratory drains away. The researcher has lost nothing personally. What has been lost is the future in which the work would land.

Scheffler's own list is research, activism, institution-building, new buildings, infrastructure and the environment; the effect he describes is weakened confidence/motivation, not proven worthlessness.

The same goes for much else on his list. Campaigning for a political cause, reforming a public institution, putting up a building meant to last a century, protecting a forest or a river. All of these point past their makers' lives. All of them, in the doomsday case, would come to seem far less worth the effort.

So the guess was wrong, and the place where it went wrong is instructive. The guess assumed that what we care about is our own experience, so that whatever happens after we die cannot matter to us. But a large share of what people actually do with their lives is aimed at a world they will never see. That aim only makes sense if there will be people in that world.

Scheffler also offers a second case that involves no violence at all. It comes from a novel about the near future. In his summary, "human beings have become infertile", with no recorded birth in over twenty-five years.97 Nobody is killed. Everyone alive lives out their natural span. But the human story is visibly ending, one generation from now. Scheffler argues that this world, too, would drain the point out of a great deal of ordinary activity. Schools would teach children who will have no successors. Libraries would store books for readers who will never be born.

Scheffler hedges this as a claim about what our attitudes reveal, not that future strangers are objectively more valuable than people we love.

That is why he calls it the afterlife, in a deliberately unusual sense. He does not mean heaven, and he says he believes death is final. He means the plain fact that others will go on living after each of us has died. That afterlife, the collective one, turns out to be the one our lives are actually built on.

The case has a limit, and Scheffler marks it himself. Not everything would lose its point. Relieving someone's pain right now would still matter. And in his words, "Nor, similarly, does it seem likely that friendship and other close personal relations would cease to matter to people".99 That fits everything established so far. The nurse's twenty minutes with the frightened old man would be exactly as worth giving in the doomsday world. Their worth was complete when they happened. What would collapse is the long-range part of a life: the work that only makes sense if the story goes on.

So meaning in a life is held in two places. Part of it is complete in the moment, in what was done for someone, and nothing can take it back. Part of it is held in trust by other people's future. The second part is larger than most people suspect, and it does not depend on any one of us surviving at all.

There is an obvious objection, and it is the cosmic doubt coming back. Humanity will end eventually anyway. The sun, as the astronomers say, will swallow the Earth in the end, and long before that something else may finish us. So the collective afterlife is only a delay, and the doomsday case is just the real future moved closer.

Scheffler grants the fact. He writes that human life "will, in any case, come to an end eventually".100 And he notes that he normally takes it for granted that life will go on long after he is gone. The reply that follows goes beyond what he says, and it should be marked as such. It starts from what the projects actually need. A cure for cancer needs people to cure, and it needs them for centuries, not for billions of years. A school needs pupils for generations. The work that leans on the future needs a long future, not an endless one.

That is why the doomsday case horrifies and the distant end of the sun does not. The asteroid takes away the stretch of future that our projects actually reach into. Every project has a horizon, the stretch of future it is aimed at. A cure is aimed at the patients of the next few centuries, a school at the next few generations. Nothing anyone works on is aimed at people a billion years away. So the far end of the sun cuts off nothing that the work was reaching for. The difference between the two is not squeamishness. It is a difference in what the work requires.

One concession should be made, once, and plainly. On the very longest scale, this support runs out too. Stepping back far enough, past the end of the sun, the collective afterlife ends like everything else. That is the step back again, and the same answer holds. The view from that distance is accurate about how temporary everything is, and silent about whether what was done mattered.

Now go back to the night thought in its exact words. In a hundred years, none of this will matter. Taken literally, that is close to the opposite of the truth. In a hundred years, what you did will be part of the world that other people are living in. The child you raised may have raised children of their own. The institution you fixed may still be running, or may have been rebuilt on what you learned. The patient you treated may have lived to see grandchildren. The night thought was right that you will be gone, and wrong that this makes your work disappear.

The collective afterlife comes to this. Much of what makes a life worth its effort depends on the survival of other people, not on the survival of the person living it. That support is real, it is limited, and it holds across the stretch of future that human projects actually reach. It gives the night thought's fear a different shape. The question was never whether you would last. It was whether what you love is worth loving, and whether it will be there for others when you are gone. What remains is to put every piece of the argument back together, and to ask what it asks of one ordinary life.

Part 13: What Survives

Every argument has now been tested, and what survives fits in a few sentences. A life means something when the person living it is lovingly engaged with things worth loving. It needs neither God's plan nor permanence, though a believer can add both. Much of it is held in trust by other people's future. And the night thought is the shadow of the faculty that lets a person judge anything at all. So it can be held with irony, or let go, instead of feared.

Before the pieces are put back together, there is one measured result that bears on the whole account, and it is worth a guess first. Researchers followed several thousand American adults for about ten years. They asked how close people felt to their family, to their friends, and to the wider community they lived in. Ten years later, they measured how much meaning people found in their lives.101 Which of those three ties would predict more meaning a decade on? Most people guess family, or close friends.

Now Tolstoy, whose question opened all of this, needs an honest ending. He did find a way out of his crisis. He found it by turning away from people like himself, wealthy and educated, toward the working people on the land around him. In his words, "And I learnt to love these people".104 He saw their lives as answers. And he was clear about what he thought the answer was. Their faith gave their lives meaning, and their work confirmed it. His own conclusion was put as a rule for living. "Live seeking God, and then you will not live without God".105

That is his reading, and nothing here argues against it. The core of his answer was faith. What can be added, as a reading he would not have chosen, is narrower. What he could see in the working people was lives spent in labour and care for others, lived wholeheartedly. By the account reached here, those are meaningful lives, whatever one believes about God. A believer can hold that account in full and add God to it. The argument against meaning from above only ever denied that God's plan is required.

Here is the whole argument, as plain steps. One: the night question asks whether what you do matters, which is different from whether you are happy. Two: a life does not need God's purpose or endless time to matter, because chains of worth end inside ordinary lives. Three: the size and age of the universe change nothing about what an act does for the person it is done for. Four: the pessimist's ledger of pain is about happiness, not meaning. Five: feeling fulfilled is not enough, as the drugged Sisyphus shows. Six: doing good without caring is not enough either, as the burned-out doctor shows. Seven: meaning is loving engagement with something worth loving, and it needs all three parts. Eight: the night thought comes from the step back, which no answer could stop and which proves nothing about your life. Nine: the hunger behind that thought can be let go, without letting go of care. Ten: death cannot cancel what was done, and endless life would mean only what its loves meant. Eleven: much of a life's meaning depends on other people's future, not on its owner's survival.

There is an old line that belongs at the end of a list like that. Aristotle, measuring a good life, said that "one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day". The same holds for one night. A night when the step back is taken and everything looks small is one night in a whole life. It is not a verdict on the life.

So here is what the argument does to the night thought when it next arrives. It will arrive. Nothing here prevents it, and nothing could, because it comes from a power you would not want to lose. But it arrives without its authority. You know where it comes from: the step back, the view from outside, the same view that would have come for a saint. You know why it would come anyway, like an alarm that rings whatever the conditions. You know that the hunger inside it can be set down. And you know what Nagel said about that distance. If nothing seems to matter from there, then that does not matter either.

What the night thought hides is a better question, and it is the one worth taking into the morning. It has three parts, because the account has three parts. The first asks what you actually love. The second asks whether it is worth loving. The third asks whether you are doing anything with it, or only admiring it from a distance.

Those questions have answers, and the answers can be wrong. A person can discover that most of their love has gone to things that do not deserve it. Another can discover that they love something worthy and have done nothing about it in years. A third can find that they are doing a great deal of good with no heart left in it. Each of those discoveries hurts. Each of them, unlike the night thought, tells you something true about your particular life, and points at something you could change.

That is the difference between the two kinds of question. The night thought asks about the universe, and it asks from a distance at which no life, however good, could pass. The three questions ask about this life, from inside it. That is where the frightened old man was comforted, the father was cared for, and the cure was looked for. From there, mattering is not a mystery. It is what happens when someone who cares does something for something worth caring about.
