Part 1: What the Books Do Not Say

Almost everything sold today under the name of Hermes comes from a book printed in 1908, not from the ancient Hermetic books. The ancient books and the modern one agree on a single starting point: mind comes first, and matter second. From that same starting point they walk in opposite directions. The modern book concludes that a trained mind can bend the world toward what it wants. The ancient books conclude that wanting is the chain, and that a mind is freed by being loosened from it.

The modern version deserves its strongest form first, because it is not stupid, and many careful people hold it. It runs like this. Everything you have ever met came to you through your mind. Every colour, every pain, every person you love has reached you as an experience, and experience is a mental thing. So mind is the one thing you can be sure of, and the world is known only through it. Now add a second observation, which anyone can check. People who expect to fail often do, and people who expect to succeed often find a way. A frightened mind sees threats everywhere, and a calm one sees openings.

Put those two together and you have a philosophy. If mind is first, and what a mind holds shapes what it meets, then changing your life means changing what your mind holds. Hold the thought of wealth and wealth draws nearer. Hold the thought of illness and illness finds you. Train the mind, and the world rearranges itself around the training. And this, the story goes, is not a modern invention. It is the oldest wisdom there is, kept by Egyptian priests and passed down in secret under the name of Hermes. This is the teaching of a whole modern movement, and one book of 1908 gave it Hermes as its patron.

That is the belief, in its best form. It has three pieces, and they need to be separated, because they stand or fall on different evidence. The first is that mind is first. The second is that a trained mind can steer material circumstances. The third is that both of these are ancient Egyptian teaching. The ancient dialogues mostly hold the first. Their philosophy rejects the second. And the third is simply false.

Take the source of the modern summary first. In 1908 a small publisher calling itself the Yogi Publication Society printed a book called The Kybalion.9 Its authors signed themselves Three Initiates and gave no names. The book sets out seven principles, and these seven are what most people now mean by Hermetic philosophy. The first reads: The All is Mind; The Universe is Mental.10 The second reads: As above, so below; as below, so above. Then come vibration, polarity, rhythm, cause and effect, and gender, each stated as a law of nature.11

So of the three pieces, the third has gone. The seven principles are not ancient Egyptian teaching. They are a modern summary, written in a modern American voice, with an ancient name attached. But the first piece, that mind is first, is genuinely in the old books, and it is worth seeing how plainly they say it.

The first of the Greek dialogues opens with a vision. A man is thinking about the things that exist, and his bodily senses go quiet, the way they do in deep sleep.20 A presence appears to him and tells him it is mind itself, the mind that rules all things.21 It shows him a boundless light, and then a dark and restless watery chaos below it. Then it explains what he has seen. In the old English translation of 1906, the words are: That Light, He said, am I, thy God, Mind, prior to Moist Nature.22 Mind is the light, and the dark water is matter, and the light came first.

Now the two paths can be laid side by side, and the split is exact. Both start with mind first. The modern book reasons that since mind is first, mind can get the world to give it what it wants. The ancient books reason that since mind is first, mind is degraded every time it makes itself the servant of what it wants. One treats wanting as the engine and mind as the fuel. The other treats mind as the self and wanting as the leash.

The difference is easiest to see in two people, so take two people who want the same thing. Both of them have been passed over for a promotion, and both of them believe that mind comes first. The first sits down each morning and holds the image of the new office, the new title, the new salary, as clearly as he can. He is using his mind to serve the want. The second sits down each morning and watches the want itself. She sees how much of her day it owns, how it colours every meeting, how it makes her small. She is using her mind to see the want, and as she sees it, it loosens.

Both are doing mental work. Both would say that their inner life comes first. But they are doing opposite things, and they will judge success by opposite measures. For the first person, success is the office. For the second, success is the morning she notices that the office has stopped mattering, and that she is still there, larger than she was. The modern teaching that thoughts attract their objects is a manual for the first person. The Hermetic dialogues are a manual for the second.

There is an objection here, and a fair one. It asks who cares where an idea came from, if it works. If the seven principles help someone get out of bed, it should not matter that they were written in 1908 and not under the pharaohs.

On the question of age alone, the objection is right. A new idea can be true, and an old idea can be false. But two things here survive the objection, and both matter to anyone who has been drawn to this material.

The first is that the modern book borrows an authority it does not have. Its whole appeal rests on age: older than Moses, kept by Egyptian priests, never written down. Offered as one American writer's theory, its principles would have to be argued for, and judged like any other self-help. Dressed in the name of Hermes, they arrive pre-approved, as if thousands of years of sages had already tested them. The name is doing work the argument never did.

The second is more important. The two teachings do not just differ in pedigree. They make opposite predictions about what a freed life feels like. On the modern view, a mind that works properly brings you more of what you want. So a life full of unmet wants is a sign that your mind is failing. On the ancient view, a mind that works properly wants less and sees more. A life full of unmet wants is the ordinary human condition, and the work is to stop being ruled by it.

Someone who reads the old books through the new one will measure themselves with the wrong ruler. They will try the ancient practice of watching their desires, and then judge it by whether the desires got fed. By that measure the ancient practice always fails, because feeding the desires was never its aim. That is the practical cost of the mix-up, paid by exactly the person who came looking for the old wisdom in good faith.

So this is what remains of the modern belief. Its first piece stays: the first Hermetic dialogue really does say that mind comes before matter, and it means it literally. Its second piece goes. The philosophical dialogues never teach that a trained mind should steer circumstances toward its wishes, and they say the wishing keeps a mind small. Its third piece goes too. The famous principles come from a book of 1908, and the famous slogan from an Arabic text of alchemy.

That leaves one claim of the legend still standing, and it is the one the legend leans on hardest. The modern principles are not ancient. But for a century and a half, some of the best minds in Europe believed the old Greek books themselves were older than Plato. They put their author within a few generations of Moses. The next question is whether they were right.

Part 2: Hermes First, Then Plato

The Hermetic books are not the wisdom of ancient Egypt handed down from the age of Moses. They were written in Greek, in Egypt under Roman rule, by several hands, sometime between the first and fourth centuries after Christ.31 For about a hundred and fifty years, the best-read people in Europe believed otherwise. The proof that they were wrong took away the books' authority. It took away none of their argument. The difference between those two losses is what makes the books worth reading now.

Here is what the lost date actually costs, and what it does not. Two opposite mistakes are easy to make, and both have been made about these books.

The first mistake is to think that old means true. This was Ficino's mistake, and it is the 1908 book's whole method. An idea does not become more likely to be correct because it was written down early. Early people were wrong about most things, including the shape of the heavens these books describe.

The second mistake is to think that late means false. It is the same error turned upside down. A claim written in the third century is not refuted by being written in the third century. Suppose the books argue that a mind is freed by letting go of what it wants. That argument is exactly as strong, or as weak, in Roman Egypt as under the pharaohs.

So it is fair to ask what was really lost in 1614. Something real was lost, and it should be named exactly. An ancient text that predicted Christian doctrine would have been evidence, testimony from outside that something true had been seen early. A third-century text that echoes Christian doctrine is not evidence of anything except its own century. The books stopped being witnesses. What they still are is arguments, and an argument does not need a date to work.

That changes how they have to be read, and the change is a gain. Read as revelation, the books only had to be believed. Read as arguments, they have to be tested. Part 3: The Man Who Fell in Love With His Image

The Hermetic creation story is an explanation of something nearly everyone has felt. There is a part of you that watches your life, and a part that lives it, and they are not the same. The story says the watcher came first, and that it is held by the life it watches. It was not dragged down by force. It fell in love. That last detail looks like decoration. It turns out to be the books' most important claim, because a bond made by love is undone differently from a bond made by force.

Start with the feeling, before the story. A man is at the dinner table with his family, tired, and his son spills a glass of water across the bread. He snaps. His voice is louder than he meant, and the boy goes quiet. And in the same second, something in him sees it happen. It sees the raised voice, the small face, the whole scene, as if from a little above the table. That watching part did not snap. It looks at the snapping with something like dismay, and it has no hands to stop it.

Most people know some version of that. The watcher who lies awake while the body is exhausted. The watcher who sees you reaching for the phone for the fortieth time and says nothing useful. It is a strange arrangement. The part that sees seems more like you than the part that acts, and yet the part that acts is the one running things. The first Hermetic dialogue is a story about how that arrangement came to be.

So the fall is not about vanity. It is about mistaken identity. The Human sees a form like himself and takes it for a place he can live. That is a different error from admiring yourself. It is the error of finding yourself in something that is not you, and then moving in.

A woman looks in the mirror before a wedding and thinks she looks wonderful. That is vanity, and it is mostly harmless. Another woman has spent twenty years as the competent one at her firm, the person everyone calls when something breaks. Part 4: Giving Back the Planets

The Hermetic way back is a climb through seven heavens. At each heaven the climber hands back one of the drives that made up their personality. The claim inside the picture is that most of what you call yourself was lent to you. Freedom, on this account, is not improving those drives. It is returning them, one by one, switched off.

The ancients saw seven moving lights in the sky: the Moon, the Sun, and the five planets visible to the naked eye. In the Hermetic picture each of them turns in its own sphere around the earth, and together they are called the harmony.76 Below them is the world of birth and death. Above them is what the text calls the eighth, and above that, God.

That is the difference between this teaching and nearly every modern programme of self-improvement, and two cases make it clear. Take a man whose ambition is eating his life. One kind of advice says to make the ambition healthier. Set better goals, balance them with family, channel the drive into something worthwhile. The ambition stays where it was, and the man spends his life managing it.

The Hermetic advice is of a different kind. It asks him first where the ambition came from. Perhaps it arrived at a datable moment: a father who praised only results, a school that ranked children by marks. It was lent to him, and at some point he started calling it his own. The work is to see that it is a loan, and to give it back. The first approach improves the drive. The second returns it. The ascent is made entirely of the second kind of work, repeated seven times.

The same test can be run on anything a person carries. There is the temper inherited from a parent, the one that comes out of your mouth in your mother's exact words. There is the anxiety of the age, picked up from the news and the phone without anyone choosing it. There is the appetite for things nobody wanted until an advertisement taught them to want it. For each one the question is the same. Is this mine, or is it lodged in me.

The story supplies a way to answer that question, though it never states it as a rule. A drive that can be watched is not the watcher. If you can see your temper rising, as the man at the dinner table saw his, something in you is what the temper happens to. The drive is a guest in that something. And a guest, however long it has stayed, can in principle be asked to leave.

The ascent, laid bare, comes to four steps. One: the drives that make up a personality were received, on the way into a life, from outside the self. Two: what was received can be returned. Three: returning a drive means switching it off, not improving it. Four, on the books' account: what remains when every borrowed drive is gone is what the person was before they borrowed anything.

There is a problem with reading the climb this way, and it is best faced at once. The text places the ascent at death. The body dissolves, and then the soul rises. A reader is entitled to ask whether a description of the afterlife can be turned into advice about an ordinary afternoon.

The text itself answers that, in two places. The whole dialogue is told by a man whose thought rose to a great height while he was alive, with his body's senses held back. The vision is already a small ascent, taken in life. And when the vision ends, he goes out and preaches to living people. Change your minds, he tells them, you who have walked with error and kept company with ignorance. The climb is completed at death, on the books' own belief, but it is begun now, and the beginning is a change of mind.

The first serious objection is about what is left.

Hand back every drive, the objection says, and nothing remains. A self is its drives. Take away a person's wanting, their ambition, their daring, their capacity to plan, and you have not freed them. You have produced an empty shell, a person who sits in a chair and does nothing, because they no longer have any reason to stand up. A famous eighteenth-century argument says that whoever looks inward finds only passing perceptions, and never a self.83 The ascent, on this view, ends in a corpse.

The text has an answer, and it is precise. What reaches the eighth sphere is the essential human, the being of mind that leaned down and fell in love. It was never one of the planets' gifts. It came from the father before it passed through any sphere. So the drives can all be returned, and something is still there to return them. That something was never built out of drives in the first place.

That answer has to be weighed. Nobody can show that the remainder exists apart from every drive, and the books do not try. But the test does not have to be run all at once. Each time a single drive is handed back, there was someone who did the handing. Anyone who has let an old anger go knows the question from the inside. It was not the anger that let itself go. Something else did, and that something did not shrink when the anger went. That is not proof of a deathless remainder. It is not even proof that the remainder is free of drives. The thing that let the anger go may have been another drive, a love or a tiredness. What it does show is that the self is not the same as any one of its drives. Each can be handed back in turn. That is the step the practical argument needs, and the larger claim, a deathless remainder, stays the books' own.

The second objection goes straight at the end of the climb. The text says the climber becomes God. That is not a softened phrase in the original. The Greek word means to be made a god, and the old translation renders it as being made one with God. This is the good end, it says, for those who have gained knowledge. To many ears this sounds like the most inflated spiritual claim possible. It sounds like exactly what a cult leader would say about himself on the way to taking his followers' money.

Look again at the list of things handed back, and the objection turns over. At the fourth zone the climber gives up domineering arrogance. At the second, scheming. At the seventh, the lie. Every trait a megalomaniac needs is confiscated on the way up. A person who arrives at the top claiming to be a great one has, by the text's own logic, not climbed past the fourth sphere.

And the climber does not arrive as anyone in particular. The Greek says he comes to the eighth stripped naked of the harmony's workings.84 There he joins those already present and sings to the father with them. Then, the text says, becoming powers, they are in God, no longer separate beings but forces within him.85 The individual with a name, a temper, a history and an ambition has been handed back piece by piece. In this dialogue's picture, what becomes God is what was left when the particular someone was gone.

Part 5: Make Yourself Like God

The most ambitious argument in the Hermetic books is about knowing. It says you can understand only what you are able to become. So a mind that wants to understand everything must first be made as large as everything. That argument is stronger than it sounds, and the best philosophy of the ancient world agreed with half of it. It is also weaker than it claims, and the place where it breaks can be pointed to exactly.

The argument comes from the eleventh dialogue, where Mind itself is the teacher and Hermes is the pupil. It begins with a rule. If, then, thou dost not make thyself like unto God, thou canst not know Him. Then the reason for the rule. For like is knowable to like alone.87

Most people, asked what it takes to know something, would give a different answer. Knowing is collecting information. To know about God, you read about God, you study the arguments, you listen to people who have thought about it. The knower stays the same size, and the stock of facts grows. The Hermetic claim reverses that. On its account, knowing is a change in the knower. A mind understands something only to the extent that it can take that thing's shape.

That sounds mystical, but in ordinary life it is often simply true.

The first case is chess. A beginner can know a great many facts about the game. The knight moves in an L, the bishop stays on its colour, a pawn that reaches the far side becomes a queen. None of that lets him see why a grandmaster's quiet little move wins the game twenty turns later. To see that, he would need to hold the whole board in his head at once, every piece and every threat in relation to every other. No new fact will do it. His mind has to become the kind of mind that can hold that much.

The second case is grief. A woman's mother has died, and her friend knows that fact. He knows the date, the illness, the funeral arrangements. But he understands what has happened to her only when something in him can stretch to the size of her loss. Perhaps he has lost a parent himself, or he can reach for the nearest thing he has felt. Until then, he has information about her grief, and no understanding of it.

Both cases show the same split. There is knowing about a thing, which needs only facts. And there is understanding a thing, which needs a mind of matching size and shape. The Hermetic name for the second is the principle that like is known by like. And the principle says that the bigger the thing, the bigger the mind must become to understand it.

This is not an eccentric idea, and it has two respected ancestors. Plato had already said that the way out of the world's troubles is to become as like God as a human can.92 Aristotle rejected the old slogan that like is known by like, but he held something close to it.93 The mind, he wrote, before it thinks, is not actually any real thing.94 It is capable of receiving the form of an object, its pattern without its stuff.95 When it knows, it takes on that form, and he concluded that actual knowledge is identical with its object.96 In one of his most famous lines, he wrote that the soul is in a way all existing things.97

So the Hermetic author was not making something up. He joined Plato's goal to an account of knowing in which the mind becomes what it knows, and pushed both to their limit. If knowing a thing means taking its form, then knowing everything means taking the form of everything. And if God is the one who holds everything, then to know God, the mind must grow to hold everything too.

The obvious objection is the one a child would make. I know what a stone is, and I have not turned into a stone. So the principle must be false. Like is not known only by like, because hard things are known by soft minds every day.

Aristotle had already answered this, and his answer is the right one. The mind does not take on the stuff of what it knows. It takes on the form, the shape and pattern and nature of the thing, without the matter. When you know a stone, the stone's form is in your mind, and the stone's weight stays on the ground. That is why he called mind the form of forms. It is the one thing that can take any shape without being any particular thing.98

With that answer in place, the principle comes out restricted and much stronger. For a stone, taking the form is easy, because the form is simple. Nothing in that is mystical, and almost everyone who has learned anything difficult has felt it.

Now the bridge back to the climb through the seven heavens comes into view. The locked-up soul, the one that says I fear the sea, is not just timid. It is small in exactly the sense the principle cares about. A frightened mind can take only one shape, the shape of what threatens it. A wanting mind can take only the shape of what it wants. Every drive that is still running in a person narrows the range of shapes their mind can take.

That is why the drives have to be handed back before God can be known. The point is not moral. A mind full of its own wanting is too small and too fixed in shape to hold anything as large as the whole. Returning the drives is how the mind gets back its range. The ascent and the principle are one argument seen from two sides.

There is one step in the middle, though, that can actually be tested. If the argument is right, one prediction follows: when a mind is made to hold something vast, the small grasping self should shrink. The pupil does not grow into a giant ego. He grows larger in view and smaller in self at the same time.

Before the evidence, consider what most people would expect. If you made someone feel as large as the sky, it seems natural that their ego would swell with it. Fear and wanting keep a mind small, which is why the drives must be returned before anything large can be understood. That leaves a harder question about the whole itself. If the pupil is told to grow until he holds the entire world, the world had better be worth holding. Some of the Hermetic books say it is God's own image and a second god. Others say it is full of evil from end to end. Both cannot be right, and the next question is which one is.

Part 6: Beautiful but Not Good

The Hermetic books contradict each other about the world. Some of them say the visible world is a second god, full of the creator's presence, and the best place to look for him. Others say it is the fullness of evil, and that no good can be got from anything in it. The contradiction is real, but it resolves into one precise position that a third author states in a single line. The world is beautiful, but not good. It is worth everything to look at, and it is worth nothing to depend on.

Three: so nothing in the world is good in that strict sense, though everything in it can be beautiful. Four: a person who looks at the world sees its beauty and is enlarged by it. Part 7: The Bowl of Mind

The Hermetic books divide human beings into two kinds. Every person, they say, has reason, the power to speak, plan and argue. Not every person has mind, the power to see what their life is for. That division is real, and it should not be explained away. What softens it is one dialogue's insistence that mind was offered to everyone. Whoever lacks it, on their account, has not yet chosen to take it.

Two people show the distinction before it has a name. The first is a brilliant swindler. He can read a room in seconds, plan ten moves ahead, argue any side of any question, and talk an old woman out of her savings. Nothing is wrong with his thinking. His thinking is excellent. But ask him what his life is for, and he has no answer except more of the same.

The second is a woman who never finished school. She could not win a debate against the swindler, and she would not try. But she knows what matters in her life and what does not, and she sees through him in the first five minutes. If the swindler is all calculation, she has something calculation does not give. She can see the whole of a situation and know where she stands in it.

The Hermetic books have a name for each of those powers. The swindler's power they call reason, in Greek logos, the faculty of speech and argument. The woman's power they call mind, nous, the faculty that sees the whole. It is the same mind that the earlier argument said must grow to take the shape of everything. And the fourth dialogue says that God gave the two out very differently.

Against all this stands a serious charge. This is spiritual elitism, and of a dangerous kind. It divides humanity into the knowers and the herd, people with mind and people who are, by the books' own words, like animals with speech. History is full of movements built on exactly that division. There are the elect and the damned, the awakened and the sleepers, the enlightened ones and the ordinary people who do not get it.

And the division does damage in a predictable way. If other people are like irrational creatures, their opinions do not count, their objections are noise, and their suffering matters a little less. Tell followers they have something the many lack, and that the many dislike them for it, and they have a reason to ignore every criticism. Any objection can be filed under the many, who do not understand. That is the structure of a closed group, and the line about the many can be read as building it into the doctrine.

There is a second problem, a logical one, hidden inside the first. Mind comes to the holy and the good, the first dialogue says. But on the books' own account, a person becomes good by having mind. So the circle seems closed. You need goodness to receive mind, and you need mind to become good. Whoever starts outside the circle seems to have no way in at all.

The first charge lands in part. The harsh words are in the text, and the line about irrational creatures sits inside the bowl story itself, aimed at those who missed the call. The authors did speak of them with contempt. Nothing in their argument requires that contempt, and a reader can leave it where it is.

But the second objection, the closed circle, is answered by the bowl story itself, and the answer changes what the division means. Look at who the herald speaks to. The proclamation goes to the hearts of men, all of them. And look at what the first step is. Those who understood the herald's tidings dipped themselves. Understanding a proclamation needs only reason, which everyone has. So reason is not useless. It cannot produce mind, but it can hear the call to go and get it.

Then the dialogue says the thing that turns the division from a caste into a choice. The man who hath the will to choose, it says, is left the choice of one or other. The choice is between the things that perish and the things that are divine. And the choosing of the better, it adds, proves a lot most fair, a happy fate, for him who makes the choice.137 Nobody in the bowl story is born with mind or without it.138 Everyone is born with reason, everyone hears the same herald, and the difference comes from what each one does next.

Read that way, the bowl story stops being a claim that some people are animals. The harsh sentence about irrational creatures then describes a condition, not a class. It is a condition everyone knows from the inside.

There is a test case that shows what is at stake in choosing this reading. Two brothers grow up in the same house, with the same parents and the same school. At forty, one of them stops and asks what his life is for, and starts to change it. The other never asks, and his life runs on exactly as it was set going. A modern explanation would point to temperament, to luck, to some event that struck one brother and missed the other. All of that may be true.

The bowl story gives a different explanation, and it is both more hopeful and more demanding. The herald called to both brothers. One of them heard and went down to the bowl. The contempt is not load-bearing. Take it out, and the argument loses nothing. If the bowl is offered to everyone, the person who has not yet dipped is someone who has not yet been reached. The right attitude toward them is the herald's, who calls, and not the snob's, who sneers.

What survives from the bowl is short. Mind is not the same as cleverness, and it is not handed out at birth. It was offered to everyone, and it is taken by choosing. Whoever has not chosen is not shut out. On the revised reading, the line between having mind and lacking it runs through every person, not between them.

Part 8: Fated, and Punished

The Hermetic answer to fate is that everyone is subject to it in what happens to them. Only those without mind are ruled by it in what they become. Events are fate's. What a person turns into because of those events is not, unless they let it be. That is a real position on human freedom, close to the best one the ancient world produced. It survives the loss of the astrology it was wrapped in. It leaves one old question unanswered, and the books' own attempt to answer it fails.

The problem comes straight out of the books' picture of the heavens. The first dialogue says the seven planets govern the world below them, and it names their government plainly. Men call their ruling fate.141 Everything that happens on earth, on this view, is set going from above. And that seems to leave the choice to dip in the bowl hanging in empty air. If the stars move everything, they move the hand that reaches for the bowl, and the hand that does not.

Two objections remain.

The first says that this is consolation dressed up as freedom. Being calm after you are fired is still being fired. Real freedom means being able to change what happens: to keep the job, to stop the disease, to prevent the war. An inner freedom that changes nothing outside is a way of making people accept what they should be fighting.

There is truth in that, and the answer is narrower than the books sometimes make it sound. Nothing in the Hermetic position forbids action. The woman from the office can look for a new job, sue her employer, organise her colleagues. What the position claims is that whatever she does, she does as herself, and not as a person the event has rebuilt. The bitter man also acts, but his actions are written by the injury. The freedom at stake is freedom from being authored, and that is real freedom, because a life authored by its injuries is not one's own.

The second objection is the one Tat raised, and it has not gone away. If the murderer had no mind, and was moved by fate like an animal, then punishing him is like punishing a dog for biting. It may be necessary for protection. It is not justice. And the books' answer, that punishment is fated too, does not touch this.

On this, the books' text does not solve the problem. The best they can do comes from the bowl story. The man without mind was offered the bowl like everyone else, and did not take it. So his condition is not pure fate. It is partly the result of a call he heard and did not answer. But the books never say whether he could have accepted, given everything the stars had done to him. That is the old question of free will, and the books leave it where they found it.

What the books do settle is the narrower question, and the narrower question is the one most people actually live inside. It is not whether the murderer deserves punishment. It is whether a person who is hit by events must be remade by them. To that, the answer is no, and the reason is given.

One more thing has to be said, because the whole position was built on astrology. The books believed that seven planets literally govern events on earth. Nobody now believes that, and the argument does not need anyone to. But the structure of the argument does not depend on what fate is made of. Replace the planets with genes, with upbringing, with the economy, with chance. The same two-layer picture remains. What happens to a person is caused from outside. What they become because of it is where mind does its work.

The position, stated as steps, is this. One: everything that happens to a person is caused by things outside their control, call it fate or anything else. Two: those events fall on everyone, the wise and the foolish alike. Three: a person without mind is also made by those events, in their character and what they do next. Four: a person with mind is touched by events, and not made by them. Five: that difference is what freedom is, and it is available whatever the stars, the genes or the economy do.

Fate, then, rules everything that happens, and it does not have to rule what anyone becomes. That picture can answer more than the books' text did. But it rests on an assumption nobody has yet defended, about which part of a person is the person.

Part 9: Which One Is You

The Hermetic picture of freedom assumes that the watcher is the real person, and that it ought to be the one running the life. The books had a reason for that. The watcher is the first Human, made of light by the father, and the body's life is something it borrowed later. That reason was set aside along with the deathless soul. Without it, the watcher needs another claim to the steering wheel. There is one, and it comes from outside the books. The watcher's right to run a life rests on how much it can see, not on where it sits.

Go back to the man at the dinner table. Something in him snapped at his son, and something in him saw it happen. The Hermetic story calls the seeing part the man and the snapping part a guest. But the temper could say the same thing the other way round. Why is the part that looks more him than the part that shouts? The feeling that the watcher is more you is real. It is also only a feeling. When an old anger is handed back, it shows that the self is not the same as that one drive. It does not show that the one doing the handing back has any right to.

Tat's question about the seducer is the same question asked from the other side. Suppose a man without mind is moved by his appetites the way an animal is. Is anything he does his own? Both questions ask which part of a person is the person. The books answered with a myth about light. A modern argument answers without one, and it arrives at a two-layer picture of the same shape by a completely different road.

Three people show the idea before it has a name. The first is addicted to a drug and hates it. He wants the drug, and he also wants not to want it, and every evening the first want wins. The second takes the same drug, with the same craving, and is glad of it. If the craving ever faded, he would do whatever he could to bring it back. The third has no view about his wants at all.153 He is moved by whichever want is strongest at the moment, and he never asks which one he would rather won.

The watcher's claim to run the life was never its height. It is its range.

That fills the hole, and it fills it in a way that has a cost. The watcher has authority because it sees more of what matters, and for no other reason. So its authority lasts exactly as long as it does. A watcher that narrows, into fear or contempt or blame, loses the right to rule in the same moment. And the test can be run on any voice in the head that claims to speak for the whole person.

Two things the answer does not give. It gives authority, not survival, and the deathless soul stays what the books believed and nothing more. It also does not show that a wider view is a warmer one. A person can stand very far back from their own life and feel nothing at all, and whether that is freedom is a separate question.

What it does give is a way to read everything that comes next. A person can be moved by events on the outside only, or all the way down. What decides it is how much of what matters the steering part can hold. So it matters exactly what narrows the view, what it is in a person that lets the events in. The books give a list of those things, twelve of them, and the name they give them changes what a vice is.

The word that matters is torment. The list looks like a list of sins, and almost every item on it would appear in any preacher's catalogue. But the dialogue never uses the language of sin, fault or crime for them. Its word is torment, and it says who they torment. The person who has them is the one who suffers.

That reverses a belief most people hold without noticing, and the belief deserves its best statement. Vice is pleasure. The greedy man enjoys his money, the angry man enjoys his rage, the lustful man enjoys his conquests. Virtue is the price you pay to be good, the pleasure you deny yourself. That is why virtue needs rewards, in heaven or in reputation, because on earth the sinner seems to have the better time. The saint goes without, and the sinner has the fun.

The rebirth dialogue says that belief is wrong, and wrong at its root. The sinner, on its account, is being punished, continuously, by the thing he thinks he is enjoying. And the one item on the list that shows this most plainly is envy, because envy has no pleasure in it at all.

Take a woman whose closest friend at work is promoted over her. She is happy for her friend, she says, and part of her is. But that night she lies awake going over it. She notices every small sign of the friend's new status, the new office, the slightly different tone in meetings. The friend's good news has become a weight she carries everywhere. Nobody is punishing her. Her friend has no idea. The envy is doing all of it on its own.

Envy gives her nothing. It does not get her the promotion, it does not hurt the friend, and it makes every success around her into a small injury. If anyone in that story is being tormented, it is the envious woman, and the torment is the envy itself. That is exactly what the dialogue means. The vice is the punishment. No judge is needed, because the sentence is carried out by the crime.

The usual reply is that envy is a special case. Some vices really do feel good. Anger has a hot, righteous pleasure in it, and lust has an obvious one. Greed gets you things. The dialogue seems to have chosen its example to suit itself.

The answer lies in the phrase the dialogue uses: the torments make the inner person suffer through the senses. The pleasure is real, and it is the bait. The torment is what the bait is attached to. A man who wins an argument in a fury has a few seconds of hot satisfaction. Then he replays the argument at three in the morning, rehearsing what he should have said, feeling the injury again and again. The pleasure lasted a minute, and the anger owned his night.

Lust works the same way. What the list names is the craving, the restless wanting that is never finished. It makes a person check and scroll and plan when they would rather be doing anything else. A pleasure that comes and goes and leaves a person free is not on the list. A compulsion that comes back whether the person wants it or not is. That is the line between a pleasure and a torment, and it is the line this reading of the dialogue draws.

Research on the brain has found the same line inside the head. The system that makes a person want a reward, the pull toward it and its signals, is separate from the one that makes them enjoy it. Part 11: Free, or Just Far Away

Everything argued so far trains one move, which is standing back from your own life. Medicine has a name for a person who watches their life from outside and feels almost nothing, and the name is a disorder. That is the strongest objection the Hermetic road faces. It lands on the darker books, and on any practice that stands back without one simple check. It misses the books' best account, whose end is joy and a mind that takes in everything. The check fits in a single question. Does standing back make you see more and care more, or see less and feel less?

Start with what the road has asked for. The watcher at the dinner table saw the scene as if from a little above it, and it had no hands to stop it. The climber handed back his drives one by one, switched off. Tat became a stranger to the world, at home everywhere and in nothing. Each step moves a person further back from the life they are actually living.

A case shows where the move can lead. A woman in her forties comes out of a hard year and takes up a practice of watching her own mind. She is serious about it, an hour each morning and longer at weekends. At first it works the way the books say. Her temper loosens, and the worries about money stop running her evenings. Then, over a few months, something else happens. Her daughter's birthday party feels like a film she is watching through glass. Her own hands look like someone else's. She wants nothing and enjoys nothing, and she tells herself this is the detachment she was promised.

Psychiatry describes a condition with the same posture. In its clinical form, depersonalisation means lasting or returning spells of unreality and detachment. The person feels like an outside observer of their own thoughts, feelings, sensations, body or actions.180181 Its two core parts are that detachment and a muting of emotion. The person can still show feelings, but they arrive strangely dampened.182 They know the face in the mirror is theirs, and it does not feel like theirs. One recent review argues that this state also puts a person at risk of depression.183

The diagnosis follows directly from the double vocation. If the human job is to look up and then to tend, then a humanity that stops looking up will also stop tending. A world that nobody regards with wonder is not a neutral world. It is a world that is used and not cared for.

A small case makes the point without any religion in it. A town has a river running through it. For one generation the river is where people swim, fish, and walk on summer evenings, and they notice it every day. For the next, it runs behind the car parks and nobody looks at it. When a factory starts to foul it, the first generation would have been in the streets. The second barely hears the news. Nothing about the river changed between the two. What changed was whether anyone was looking at it.

A sceptic will answer that every age complains the age after it has lost its reverence. The lament may just be the old nostalgia of a priest watching his religion fade. That is fair. The specific grief in the lament belongs to a particular people losing their particular gods. The past was not more reverent than the present, and the point does not need it to be.

What survives is narrower and more durable. Care follows attention. People look after what they look at, and they stop looking after what they stop seeing. The Latin dialogue had a cosmic version of that claim, with gods withdrawing from the land. The plain version needs no gods at all, and it is true.

The Latin dialogue, then, adds a second vocation to the Hermetic argument. The human being is made to look up and to tend, and the looking does best to come first. The first frees a person from leaning on the world, and the second puts their freedom to work in it. Making is part of tending, but a maker who has not first been freed will serve what they make. The magic built on the idea of a world bound together by hidden likenesses is false. And a world nobody wonders at is a world nobody cares for.

All of that is about the human life lived between heaven and earth. But every Hermetic book points past that life, to a place at the end of the climb where the knowing is complete. What the books say about it is mostly that it cannot be said. That claim needs the same testing as the rest.

Part 13: The Eighth and the Ninth

Every Hermetic path ends, the books say, in an experience that words cannot hold. That ending is not an evasion, because it fits their own account of what mind is. It is also the point where the argument stops being something a second person can check. Everything before the summit can be tested, and the summit cannot.

The Greek dialogues describe the climb, and say very little about the top. Then, in 1945, farmers digging near the town of Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt found a jar full of old books. They were written in Coptic, the last form of the Egyptian language, and most of them were Christian. But among them was a Hermetic dialogue that nobody had read for more than fifteen hundred years.216 It is called the Discourse on the Eighth and the Ninth, and it does what the Greek books only describe. It shows the climb happening.

The Hermetic route is real philosophy, and most of its steps hold up. The summit is a report, given by people who say they went there, in words they say do not work. The books did ask for belief. Part 14: How the Price Was Dropped

From the Renaissance to the twentieth century, the Hermetic claim kept its grandeur and lost its price. The grandeur is the claim that a human being is godlike. Set the three pictures of man side by side, and the pattern is exact. In the Latin dialogue, man is a great miracle because he can look up to God and tend the earth. He rises by handing back the self. In the modern Pico, man is a great miracle because he can make himself into anything, and he rises by shaping the self. In the 1908 book, the mind is the master of matter, and the self rises by getting what it wants.

At every step the grandeur stayed the same. The human is always godlike, always a miracle, always the master of something. What changed was the price. First the price was the self. In the modern reading of Pico, it shrank to the effort of shaping the self well. At the end there was no price at all, only a technique, and the old goal had turned into its opposite. The teaching meant to free a person from wanting had become a method for wanting more effectively.

There is a strong objection to telling it this way, and it comes from the modern reading of Pico. The self-shaping human is a better idea than the Hermetic one, the objection says. A human with no fixed nature, free to shape themselves, is the root of the modern idea of human dignity. It is why we think a peasant's son can become a scholar, why no one should be told their place at birth. The Hermetic books, by contrast, sneered at the many. So the move to the self-shaping human was progress, not loss.

Much of that is right. The dignity of the self-shaping human is real. It is a moral advance over the contempt the bowl dialogue shows for those who missed the call. A world that tells everyone they can become something is better than a world that tells most people they are animals with speech. If the question is who deserves respect, the modern reading wins.

But that is not the question the Hermetic books were asking. Their question was what makes a person free, and their answer was about the self that does the shaping. A self that shapes itself out of its wants is still run by its wants. It can build a magnificent life and still be the man rewritten by the layoff. Or it can be the woman at the firm who felt she had died when her job went. Freedom to become anything does not touch that problem, because the problem is in the one doing the becoming.

So the drift brought a gain and a loss. What was gained was dignity for everyone, and it was a real gain. What was lost, gradually, across all three readings, was the condition that the books attached to greatness. A person becomes great, on their account, by becoming less of a particular someone. The modern version promises greatness by becoming more of one, and that promise is precisely the trap the first dialogue described.

That leaves the original teaching without any of the things that once held it up. Its date is gone, and so is its pedigree. Its picture of the heavens is gone, and its magic, and its scorn for the many. The modern version that wears its name teaches the opposite of what it said. But the two versions still make opposite promises about a life that gets what it wants. A promise, unlike a pedigree, can be checked against real lives. The question is which promise holds up when it is measured.

Part 15: The Two Promises, Measured

Two promises have been made in the name of Hermes, and parts of both can now be checked against how real lives go. The modern promise says that a mind holding the picture of what it wants will get it, and be better off for getting it. The ancient promise says that getting it will not set you free, because the wanting is the chain. Psychology has measured pieces of each. The measurements side with the books against the habit nearest to the modern technique, and then they break the books as well. What comes out is a narrower claim than the books made: a certain kind of wanting binds, and not all wanting does.

The two predictions began with two people passed over for the same promotion. Recall what the first one did each morning. He sat down and held the image of the new office, the new title and the new salary, as clearly as he could. On the modern teaching, that image is working for him. It is also the first thing that has been measured.

The ordinary expectation is that the picture helps, or at least does no harm. A vivid image of the goal sounds like fuel. It keeps the prize in view, it feels good, and feeling good about the future seems like the opposite of giving up. Even someone who doubts the rest of the teaching would probably grant this much.

The result went the other way. Researchers asked people how much they enjoyed daydreams of a good outcome, and then followed what happened to them. One group was graduate students about to look for work, asked before any of them had an offer. Two years later, those who had dwelt most on pleasant pictures of starting work had sent fewer applications. They had also received fewer offers, and they were earning less. The same pattern turned up in students hoping to win over someone they had a crush on. It turned up in students facing an exam, where the grades came from the college records and not from the students.250 And it turned up in patients recovering from hip surgery, whose progress was rated by their physical therapists.

Friends die, bodies fail and neighbourhoods scatter, so every one of them is a want for something that can be lost. The study measured well-being rather than freedom. So the measurements cut both ways, and they need a ruling. They do not show that wanting is the chain. They show that some wanting costs people and some does not. One line is consistent with everything they found, though none of them tested it directly. On one side are wants that hang a person's worth on an outcome, or on how they compare with others. Money, fame and an admired image are, on this proposal, the wants most likely to become that kind. On the other side are wants a person can pursue without staking who they are on the result.

The books are not as far from that line as their hardest sentences sound. This line fits the measurements better than the books' own, and adopting it means revising the books openly. The claim survives, narrowed. Freedom is not the end of wanting. It is the end of the wanting a self lives inside, the kind that has made itself the person. Losing the thing then feels like losing yourself, as it did for the woman at the firm. The narrowing costs the books something real. When the first of them says that wanting holds a mind down, it overstates the case. The demand to hate everything that perishes overshoots, and so does the pious loathing of the senses. What the narrowed claim keeps is the diagnosis of a self that has moved into what it can lose. What it gives up is the suspicion of wanting as such, which was the books' own overreach and not a misreading of them.

These findings reach only the lower floors of the argument: the diagnosis of a self living inside its wants, and the picture of the prize. They test nothing about God, the deathless soul or the top of the climb.

Here is where the two promises stand. One: enjoying the picture of a goal goes with worse results, while confidence built on a record helps. Two: getting money, fame and an admired image did not raise well-being, which is what the books would expect. Three: getting closeness, growth and health did, so not every want costs the one who wants. Four: money can raise how a person judges their life, and the books have to concede it. The line proposed here is that what binds is the wanting a self lives inside. With the date, the heavens, the magic and now the blanket case against wanting all taken away, the question is what, if anything, is still standing.

Part 16: What Survives

Take away the planets, the false date, the magic and the case against all wanting, and the books still leave a coherent account of human freedom. A person is ruled by whatever they identify with. A mind grows free in the same measure that it grows large, and it grows large by handing back what it was lent. That account can be lived by someone who believes in no eighth sphere and no deathless soul. It is also harder and more honest than the version that has been sold under its name.

Begin with what fails. The antiquity fails. The books are not older than Moses, and the principles sold under their name were written in 1908.264 The heavens fail. Seven planets do not hand anyone their vices, and the stars do not govern fate. The magic fails with them, because herbs, stones and statues are not bound to the gods by hidden likenesses.

Some of the books' moral claims fail too. The contempt for those who missed the bowl's call is in the text and has no support in the argument. Listing every grief as a torment overreaches, because some sorrow is simply the right response to a loss. The defence of fated punishment, that the punishment is fated too, justifies nothing. And the suggestion that all wanting binds fails, because getting closeness and growth went with doing well.

And the summit cannot be checked. The leap from a mind that holds the whole in thought to a mind like God rests on an unargued premise. The silent knowledge at the top of the climb proves nothing to anyone who was not there. Whether a human being can become God gets no ruling, because no ruling is possible.

The belief many people bring to these books gets a ruling, though: that mind comes first, and matter second. The books hold it, and they do not establish it. Their boldest argument, about making yourself like God, takes for granted that God holds the world as a mind holds a thought. That is the same belief, assumed and never argued. Their argument from the made body was answered by biology. And nothing that survives needs the belief, because the watcher can be a working of the brain and still be ruled by what it identifies with. Keep the belief or drop it, and the account works either way.

That is a long list of losses. What survives is one linked argument about how a human life works. It has been argued for step by step, it can be checked against an ordinary life, and at several points it has been measured. The measurements cut it down as well as holding it up.

Stated as a sequence, it runs like this. One: a person is two things, a watcher and a life. Two: the watcher is ruled by whatever it identifies with, and it identifies with what can be lost. Three: the drives that hold it there were received, and can be handed back. Four: as they are handed back, the mind can take a larger shape, and understands more.

Five: a mind that large can look at the world without leaning on it. Six: events still fall on it, but they no longer remake it. Seven: the vices, which were the torments of identification, lose their ground. Eight: such a person looks up, and then turns back to tend the world. And nine: whether, at the end, they become God is something no one can check, and the first eight steps do not depend on it.

That last point matters more than any other in deciding what to do with these books. If the whole teaching depended on the summit, a reader who doubted the summit would have to throw it all away. It does not. Every step from one to eight can be checked against an ordinary life, and most of them hold. A person can take the route without believing the view from the top, even though the books themselves asked for belief.

Go back to the feeling that brings most people to these books. There is more to me, it says, than the life I am running. And the modern answer is that the more is a power, that the right thoughts bring the right results. The Hermetic books agree that there is more. They disagree, completely, about what it is.

On their account, the more is not a bigger version of the life, with a better job and a fuller bank account. It is the watcher, the part that sees the life and is not the same as anything it watches. It is already there. It does not need to be attracted from anywhere. It needs to stop being owned.

A single ordinary day shows the difference between the two promises. Two people have the same bad Tuesday. The car will not start, a message from the boss is cold and unfair, and at home someone they love forgets something that mattered. Nothing about the day is a tragedy, and both of them will remember it as a bad one.

The first person is run by the day. The cold message replays all afternoon, the anger rises and stays, the forgetting at home becomes an argument. By evening the day has written him. He is someone it made, and he goes to bed as that person.

The second person has the same day, and feels the same sting. But somewhere in the afternoon she notices the anger rising, and sees it as a guest and not as herself. On the way home she looks up, and the sky over the car park is enormous and indifferent and beautiful. She does not need it to fix anything. She arrives home still herself, and the forgetting stays a small thing.

Neither of them has more money at the end of the day. Neither of them got what they wanted from the boss. By the standard of the get-what-you-want teaching, they are equal, and both failed. By the standard of the ancient books, one of them was free and the other was not. That difference is the whole of what those books were about.

That is the test that separates the two teachings, and anyone can run it. One promises that you will get what you want, and it can be checked against a bank balance. The other promises that you will need less of it and be less owned by it, and it can be checked against a Tuesday. The second promise is smaller, and it depends less on luck.

The first Hermetic dialogue ends with a scene that holds all of this in one picture. The dreamer wakes from his vision and goes out among people. He finds them, in the old translation, given up to drunkenness and sleep and ignorance of God. And he calls out to them.

Ye earth-born folk, he says. Why have ye given yourselves up to death, while yet ye have the power of sharing deathlessness? And then: change your minds.265
