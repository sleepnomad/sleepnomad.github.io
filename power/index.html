That question has a serious answer, and the serious answer came from a man who was sick of lists exactly like yours. The way American towns were studied in the 1950s was to ask well-placed informants who really ran the place, and write the names down. Robert Dahl's objection was not that the names were wrong. It was that a reputation is not a finding, and a claim nobody could disprove is not knowledge.

His fix was a measurement rule, and it is still the cleanest one anybody has. Pick a town. Inside it, mark the areas where something is genuinely at stake, such as redevelopment, the schools, the party nominations. In each area, find concrete decisions where two sides wanted different things. Then see whose want became the outcome. If one set of people prevails across all the areas, you have found a ruling elite, and if nobody does, you have not. He ran it on New Haven, Connecticut, and published the result in 1961. Different winners in different areas. No single group across the board. Remirro pacified the Romagna by terror, and the method worked. The province was pacified. It also became a province that hated the government which had done this to it. The hatred did not stay with the officer who ordered the killings. It travelled up, to the man who had appointed him.

That envoy spent the rest of his life turning what he saw into a manual. The manual contains an argument that almost nobody bothers to state as an argument. Start where he starts. A ruler's aim is to keep his state, and keeping it requires that people reliably do what he says. Compliance runs on one of two things: love, or fear. Love is a bond of obligation, and men break that bond whenever breaking it suits them. Its continuance sits in their hands. Fear runs on your own capacity to punish, which never passes, so its continuance sits in yours.5 Any agent thinking clearly prefers the instrument whose supply he controls. That is the whole of it. Power is what does not depend on somebody else's continuing goodwill.

The difficulty there is not that the facts are thin. They are unusually thick, with a date, a committee and a deleted section. The difficulty is that the instrument you are holding was built to score something else. The first real test arrived in 1957. It says power is what shows up when two sides want different things and one side's want becomes the outcome. So the procedure is: find a decision, find a conflict, count who won. That rule has one enormous virtue, which is that it can come out wrong. A claim that some group runs the town becomes a claim you could check and lose. Before it, the going method was to ask around town who had the power and write the names down. That finds an elite in every town that has gossip.

Earlier you were handed a clock, 1953 or 1943, the mayor or the planning memo. Here the clock stops being a footnote about method. Setting the clock at the moment of decision is what makes the decision-maker the winner, every time, by construction. Each of the three tests in this part is a choice about where the story starts, and the flattering answers come from the late clocks.

The second test was built to catch what the first one drops. One 1960 formulation runs like this. All forms of political organisation favour some kinds of conflict and suppress others, because organisation is the mobilisation of bias. Some issues are organised into politics, and others are organised out. Take that plainly. Any committee has rules about what belongs on its agenda, and those rules are not neutral between the people who want different things from it. Winning by keeping a question off the table produces the same result as winning the vote, at lower cost and with no record. So the second measurement rule is this: find the issue that never became an issue, and find the arrangement that kept it out.15 The redacted report is that arrangement with minutes attached.

Ask why people obey and the answer is that they believe they ought to. Then ask what makes the order legitimate, and the answer is that they obey believing they ought to. The circle closes and nothing independent has been said. The critic's replacement is one sentence. A power relationship is legitimate because it can be justified in terms of the beliefs people already hold. That moves the test outside their heads. You are no longer surveying whether the governed accept the arrangement. You are asking whether it could be defended to them, which is something an outsider can check. And it explains what Weber's version cannot see, which is a regime steadily losing legitimacy while every single person still complies.

The second attack is empirical, and it is worse for the theory, because the theory half concedes it. The impersonal office badly understates how much of the outcome real officials generate. The rules underdetermine cases, and somebody has to close the gap. Weber himself says where the leverage comes from: technical expertise, plus official secrecy about what the file actually contains. Neither is conferred by any rule. Both belong to a person. The machine built to keep persons out has a door in the back, and the trained official is standing in it.

One piece of Weber's own scheme has been missing from this, and without it the end of his life makes no sense. He held that anyone claiming a right to be obeyed rests that claim on one of three grounds. The first is legality. You obey because a rule is on the books, and it was applied by the correct procedure. That is the office, and it is what this part has been describing. The second is tradition. You obey because it has always been so, and the man giving the order holds a status the past has made sacred. The third is the strange one. You obey because of something about the person, a quality you take to be exceptional. His word for it is charisma.

That word has drifted since, and his sense of it is narrower. It is not charm. It is a recognition, granted from below, by the people who follow. Nobody is appointed to it and nobody is issued with it. Which means it sits with the followers. You have handed it out yourself, to a manager or a candidate or a writer, without ever being asked to. It can be taken back. The prophet who stops delivering stops being a prophet, and no rule protects him, because rules are the thing he arrived without.

So that kind of authority carries a problem the other two do not. It cannot survive an ordinary week. A movement resting on one man's recognised exceptionality has no salary structure, and no answer to who comes next when he dies. So it builds them. The staff has to eat, so there are stipends, and stipends need accounts. The succession has to be settled, so the grace gets attached to a son, or to a position, or to an election. Weber's name for that drift is routinisation. What it does is manufacture an office out of a person. You have watched a small version of it. A founder's habits become the way things are done here, and then a written policy, and then a form somebody fills in. The exception becomes the rule, and the file appears.

The direction of that matters. It runs from the person to the office, and that is the only direction his own history ever shows it running.

Consider a man in Germany in 1919. The empire has collapsed, and there is a constitution to write. He is invited onto the draft board, and onto the German delegation to Versailles. He is famous among scholars, and almost entirely unsuccessful as a politician. His political efforts in those months bear next to nothing. One thing gets in. His advocacy of a strong plebiscitary presidency, a head of state elected directly by the whole nation, standing outside the parliament and above the ministries. He had a word for the arrangement, leader democracy. An election, on this picture, is a contest in which one man acquires a personal following. The whole nation picks him, not a party committee, and it picks him for the pull he has over people. And then the part the arrangement stands on. He stays answerable to the people who elected him. Which is Weber putting back the one thing the office had removed, somebody who can be asked.

One historian spent a career arguing that this followed from Weber's sociology rather than lapsing from it. And that it was wide open to an authoritarian reading. His critics answer that the elected leader was designed precisely to control the bureaucracy he had argued was far easier to capture than to destroy. That dispute is still running. But both sides agree on what happened, and what happened is the strange part. The theorist who proved that the office beats the person spent his last political energy trying to install a person strong enough to master the office. His own argument is the reason. If the apparatus outlives every change of master, nothing inside it can check it. The only counterweight left to a position is a human being.

So the machinery is real, and the file does not care whose hand is on it. There is nobody to argue with and nobody to petition. A machine that nobody can be asked about is still rule, running exactly as it was written to run.

The last check is empirical, and it is the hard one. Erica Chenoweth built the dataset that made all of this look like engineering. Nonviolent campaigns beat armed ones by roughly two to one. And no campaign in her record had ever reached three and a half percent of a population and then lost. Read the caveats she puts on that figure and it thins immediately. The three and a half percent is peak participation, not total sympathisers. It counts people actively and repeatedly in the streets. And it counts only maximalist campaigns, the ones trying to remove a head of state or expel an occupier, not the ones after a single concession. The record it describes runs to the mid-2000s.

And she is the one publishing the decline. From the 1960s, success rates for revolutionary nonviolent campaigns stayed above 40 percent, climbing as high as 65 percent in the 1990s. Since 2010, less than 34 percent of them have succeeded, and a mere eight percent of the violent ones.93 Average peak participation has fallen from about two percent of a population in the 1980s to one point three percent now.94 The colossus still falls when the people holding it up walk away. There are fewer of them walking, they are marching instead of striking, and the regimes have read the same literature you have.

One more limit, and it is the one nearest to your week. A peace researcher put it to Sharp in 1989, and he was arguing with the theory rather than against it. The model assumes a ruler on one side and subjects on the other, with a line between them. That is a fair map of a dictatorship. It is a poor map of an office, where almost everyone is somebody's superior and somebody's subordinate at the same hour. You sign off one person's leave and wait on another's signature for your own. There is no side of the line to defect from.

A union that came out of a strike becomes an institution that disciplines its own members.97 And the concession he made himself should be kept, because it is the honest shape of the thing. The theory is jargon-free, it is immediately actionable, and it works best exactly where the ruler and subject picture approximates reality.
