Cold open

Everything that exists is first of all one thing, and whatever makes it one cannot itself be a thing. That was the claim of Plotinus, a philosopher who taught in Rome in the middle of the third century.12 From that one claim he built an account of nearly everything. It covers where the world comes from, what a mind is and why time runs. It covers what is up to a person, why a face is beautiful and where evil comes from. And he drew a conclusion about the person hearing it. The highest part of you never left the world closest to that source.3 A good life is the slow work of turning your attention back toward it.4

Start with a body an hour after death. Every part it had an hour before is still there, the heart, the lungs, the bones, the face. Nothing has been cut away. And yet it begins to come apart, because whatever held all those parts together as one living thing has gone. Plotinus held that what holds a living body together is soul, and the corpse is the plainest case of what he meant.5 A house, an army, a choir and a person are each made of many parts, and each one is also one.6 The parts are counted easily. The oneness is the hard part, and he spent his life following it to where it comes from.

His system runs from the question of what makes a thing one, up to a source that has no parts at all. Then it comes down through mind, soul, time, nature and the body, and climbs back up through beauty and goodness. At each step there is a reason that can be tested, and some of the reasons fail.

Part 1: What Makes a Thing One

Nothing can exist without being one thing, and the oneness of a thing is never one of its parts.10 So a thing's oneness has to come to it from somewhere other than its parts. That is the first step of Plotinus's whole system, and everything else he built hangs from it. It sounds like a word game, and it is the hardest question in the system. The common answer to it runs into trouble with a case anyone can picture.

The sensible modern answer is that a thing is its parts, arranged. A house is bricks in a house-shaped order. A choir is singers in a choir-shaped order. Nothing more is needed, and anyone who asks for something more is asking for a ghost. That answer deserves to be held at full strength before it is judged. It is right that the parts are real. It is right that the arrangement matters, since the same bricks in a heap are no house. And it is right to distrust a mysterious extra ingredient that nobody can find.

The trouble shows up between two cases that differ in one way only. In the first, forty people stand in a square, each waiting for a different bus. In the second, the same forty people stand in the same square and sing one song together. The people are the same, and so are their bodies and their places in the square. The first is a crowd, and you would count forty. The second is a choir, and you would count one. Whatever makes the difference is not in any one of the forty, because each of them is present in both cases.

The arrangement answer says the difference is the arrangement, the singing together. But an arrangement is itself many things. The soprano's note lands on the same beat as the tenor's. The alto's line runs a third below the melody. The bass holds while the others move. Those relations are many, just as the singers were many. So the question comes straight back. Something makes the soprano's beat and the alto's third into one song, rather than two facts that happen to be true at once. Whatever is named as the glue turns out to be many things in its turn, and the question can be asked again about them.

Plotinus hears the obvious objection coming, and it is a good one. Perhaps oneness is not in things at all. Perhaps it is in us, in the way we count. We call the choir one because we choose to treat it as a unit, and we could as easily count forty. His answer has two steps, and both are strong. First, the thing is already one before anyone counts it. He says the unity in a choir or an army is something not of your placing.13 You did not make the singers sing together by counting them. Second, the counter has to be one as well. To count anything as one, there must be one counter who holds the whole in view at once.14 If unity lived only in counting, the counter's own unity would need another counter, and that one another. So the objection moves the question of oneness into the person counting, where it is still waiting.

That is where a careful defender of the arrangement view digs in. There are three ways to stop the question coming back. The first says any parts at all make a whole, and wholeness is nothing over and above the parts. The price is that your left shoe and the moon make a whole as good as any choir. The second says there are no wholes at all, only parts arranged choir-wise. The price is that there is no one thing that is you. The third says only living things are real wholes, and that life is what does the unifying. The price is that a choir, a house and a ship are not really one thing at all. The third is the closest to Plotinus, since the life that unifies a body is what he called soul. None of the three is refuted by his argument. What his argument does is make each of them pay, and show that oneness is something every theory has to account for.

Now the argument takes its second step, and this is where it becomes Plotinus's own. Soul gives the body its unity, but a soul is also many. It perceives, it remembers, it reasons, it wants, it is angry and it is calm. Plotinus says this directly: the soul gives unity and is itself a manifold.17 So soul cannot be the end of the line. It is one only because it too receives oneness from something else.

At this point the argument has a shape, and it can be said in four steps. One, to exist at all is to be one thing. Two, a thing's parts do not supply its oneness, because every part, and every relation between parts, needs a oneness of its own. Three, so every thing that has parts receives its oneness from something else. Four, the chain of givers cannot go on forever or end in many givers, so it ends in one with no parts at all.

Step four carries two claims, and each needs its own test. The first is that the chain cannot go on forever. Think of a line of people, each of whom has borrowed a coin from the next in line. If the line goes on without end, and nobody in it ever simply has a coin, there is no coin anywhere in the line. Every borrower is explained by another borrower, and the coin is never explained at all. Plotinus's principle works the same way with oneness. If every giver were one only by receiving, then oneness itself would never be accounted for, only passed along. The picture has a limit, since a coin is a thing that can be counted and oneness is not. What carries over is the shape, that something has to have oneness without borrowing it.

The second claim is that the chain ends in one giver rather than many. An old rival answer says it ends in many tiny things that are simply one, atoms with no parts. The reply that follows from Plotinus's principle goes to what an atom is supposed to be. Three things are true of any atom. It is round, or some other shape. It is this size. It is here and not over there. Those are three different facts about it, and a thing that three different facts are true of is already many in what it is. Its shape is one thing about it, and its place is another. So something that is one without borrowing its oneness cannot have a shape, a size or a place. It cannot have any feature at all, because one feature beside another is already two.

So the thing at the end of the chain is not a very small thing or a very large thing. It is not a thing at all, in the sense that a chair or an atom is a thing. Plotinus called it the One. The name points at it and describes nothing about it, which is the only thing a name for something featureless could do.18

Part 2: Why the First Thing Cannot Think

The source of all oneness cannot be a mind, because every act of thinking splits into two, the one who thinks and the thing thought. Anything that is two needs a one before it. So the first thing is beyond thinking, and beyond being any particular thing at all.19 This is the step where Plotinus parts company with the picture of God as a great mind. It is also the step where his system is most open to the charge of talking nonsense.

A follower of Aristotle has a strong reply. In a mind that thinks itself, the knower and the known are two sides of one act rather than two parts. A curve is hollow on one side and bulging on the other, and it is still one curve. Plotinus half agrees, since he says the first mind takes the double form while remaining essentially a unity. His point is that a unity with two sides is still a unity of two. It is less simply one than something with no sides at all. Whether two sides of one act count against perfect simplicity is exactly where the two schools part. The step is strong, and it is not compulsory, and someone who rejects it keeps a thinking God at the top.

So the conclusion follows directly. The source of oneness cannot be a mind, however great, because a mind is already a pair. It must come before thinking. This sounds like a demotion. A first principle that does not think seems lower than a mind, closer to a stone than to a god. That objection has real force, and the answer turns on why a thing might lack thought.

A stone lacks thought because it has less than a mind. It cannot hold anything in view. The One, on Plotinus's picture, lacks thought for the opposite reason. Thinking always has an object, even when the object is the thinker, and so thinking always brings a second thing along with it. A first thing that thought would already be two, and so it would not be first. A comparison helps here, as long as its limit is kept in sight. An eye needs light in order to see, and it is lower than the light it needs. The sun does not need light in order to shine, and nobody thinks the sun is lower than the eye for that. The comparison fails in one place, because the sun still has a size and a place, which the One does not.

The same reasoning takes the One past being as well. To be, for anything we know, is to be something. A chair is a chair and not a table. A number is seven and not eight. Every being is this rather than that, and the atom has already shown that this-rather-than-that is already two. So Plotinus says the One is none of the things of which it is the source.23 It is not a being among beings, not even the greatest one. It is what every being depends on in order to be one being at all.

Now comes the hardest objection to the whole system. Plotinus has just spent a great many words on something he says has no features. He has said that it exists, that it is one, that it is the source, that it is beyond thinking and beyond being. Each of those is a feature. If the One truly has no features, then every one of those sentences is false, including the sentence that says it has no features. The system seems to saw off the branch it sits on. A critic can fairly say that a philosophy built on something that cannot be described has described nothing.

Plotinus saw this coming, and his answer is careful. We can speak of our dealings with it, he says, and never of what it is. Every word we use about the One says something about us and our relation to it. Even calling it the cause, he says, describes something happening not to it but to us.24 Calling the sun the thing that warms me tells you about my skin in the sunlight. It tells you nothing of what the sun is like on the inside. The words are true, and they are true from my side. Plotinus has a phrase for what his teaching does. He says our teaching is of the road and the travelling.25 The words map the way toward the One, and they make no claim to describe what is at the end of the road.

The waiting for the day it all comes together is the seed's way of looking for the whole. But soul does more than reach. It also makes, since every plant, animal and body is shaped by it. And Plotinus made a claim about that making that sounds absurd when it is first heard. He said that nature makes things by looking at them, and that all human action is a weaker kind of looking.

Part 7: Nature Makes Things by Looking

All making is a weaker kind of looking. An acorn becomes an oak with no plan, no tools and no effort. Plotinus says nature makes everything that way, by holding a form in view. The living things follow from that holding the way heat follows from fire. Human action is much the same thing done the long way round. A great deal of what people do is done to arrive at something they could, in principle, have simply seen. Plotinus argued this in one of his most surprising treatises. It sounds like the opposite of everything a practical person believes, and it gives any practical person a test for their own week.

The practical view goes like this. Looking changes nothing. A person who sits and contemplates the sea leaves the sea as it was and leaves the world as it was. Things get made by work, by hands and tools and effort, and by plans carried out step by step. Nature, too, is a machine of forces, pushing and pulling matter into shape. On this view contemplation is a luxury. It is what people do on holiday, after the real business of living has been done by someone. That view is right that work makes things. Plotinus thinks it has the order of explanation upside down.

His way in is to put a question to nature itself. Suppose someone asked nature why it makes the things it makes. Plotinus gives its answer. Nature would say the questioner ought not to have asked, but to have learned in silence, as nature itself is silent.76 That reply sounds like a refusal to explain, and it is in fact the explanation. Nature does not talk itself through what it is doing, because it does not make by planning. There is no inner speech in a growing oak, no sketch, no list of steps.

Part 10: What Is Up to You

A person is free, for Plotinus, only when they act from their own understanding. Acting willingly is not enough, since a person in a rage acts willingly. And even a good deed is not where freedom lives, because circumstances call for the deed. Freedom lives in the inner act the deed comes from. That places it somewhere the chain of causes does not obviously reach, and it also means much of a scattered life is not free at all.

The modern objection comes first, because a person with a scattered life already half believes it. Everything a person does has causes. A decision is an event in a brain, and that event was caused by earlier events, back past birth to things no one chose. On this view, talk of turning attention upward is a story told about processes that would have run the same way without the story. Plotinus faced the ancient form of this view, in several versions, and he wrote a treatise against them.

Part 11: Why a Living Face Is Beautiful

Beauty is not symmetry. For Plotinus, beauty is form showing through a thing, the visible sign of the oneness that holds it together. Above all it is the presence of life. The soul is moved by beauty because, in a beautiful thing, it recognises something of its own kind. That account starts from an attack on the theory that almost everyone in his world held, and half of the attack still works. It ends with an instruction for a scattered life that sounds like a sculptor's advice.

The theory he attacks is the one most people would still give if asked. Beauty, he reports, was said by almost everyone to be the right proportion of parts to each other and to the whole, together with good colour.130 A beautiful face is a symmetrical face. A beautiful temple has the right ratios. A beautiful chord has its notes in the right relation. That theory explains a great deal, and it can be measured. Plotinus's claim is that proportion is not what beauty is.

Part 12: Desired Because It Is Good

A thing is not good because someone wants it. For Plotinus it runs the other way, and a thing is wanted because it is good. Wanting is how a living thing points at a good it did not invent. That is a direct answer to one of the most sensible beliefs of the modern world, that meaning is something people make rather than find. Plotinus also offers a test anyone can run on their own wants, and it turns on the difference between seeming and being.

The modern belief has a good case. Nothing in the world is good or bad in itself. Things matter because people care about them, and caring is what makes them matter. A stamp is worth a fortune to a collector and nothing to anyone else. A football club is the centre of one man's week and a mystery to his wife. On this view a meaningful life is one a person builds out of whatever they choose to care about. Nobody can tell them they chose wrong, because there is no right answer to get wrong. The view is modest, tolerant and hard to refute, and it is right about one thing. Caring does make things matter to the one who cares.

Plotinus begins with the obvious problem for any theory that lets wanting decide. People want very different things, and often the wrong things. In his words, the objects of pursuit are many and different, so that mere choice gives no assurance that the thing chosen is the best.148 If choosing made a thing good, nobody could look back on a choice and see it was bad, and everyone has. So he says desire cannot be the test, and he turns instead to judgement.149 His conclusion comes in one sentence. The good must be a thing pursued, he says, not good because it is pursued, but pursued because it is good.150

Part 17: The Door He Kept Shut

A philosophy that ranks the body below everything it loves seems to point at a door out of the body. Plotinus refused to open it. He taught that a person must not force the soul out of the body. The soul must wait for the body to let it go, with an exception only for the hardest necessity.251 His reason comes from the system itself, not from a rule borrowed from outside it. And his student reports one occasion when the teacher applied that reason to a living person, who happened to be the student.

The door, then, opens onto nothing the system wants. The way out is up, from inside, and the body lets the soul go in its own time. That leaves a question about the going. When the body does let go, something on Plotinus's account goes on. The question is what that is, and whether it remembers the life it leaves, the people in it and the name it had.

Even so, the objection the trial supports is heavy. The union Plotinus prized above everything can be produced by a chemical, in a room, on a schedule. That seems to settle what it is. It is a state of the brain, real as a feeling and evidence of nothing, just as the sensible view said at the start. If a pill can deliver the summit, the summit is in the pill.

The objection runs two things together, and they need separating. The first is the experience as proof. If Plotinus's case for the One rested on the felt union, the trial would damage it badly. A feeling that can be produced in a room by a chemical cannot, by itself, tell anyone whether they have reached anything beyond the room. On that point the objection wins. The second is the argument. Plotinus does point to the experience, as confirmation for those who have had it, but his case for the One never rested chiefly on it. It rested on the choir and the crowd, and on the chain of givers that has to end somewhere. It rested on the eye that is always two with what it sees. The union is where his road ends. It was never the evidence that the road leads anywhere. The trial, careful as it was, does not touch those arguments at all.

There is a further point from inside the system. On Plotinus's own terms, the question to ask of any such experience is what the person was like afterwards. A vision with no change of life was, for him, God on the lips. The road, with virtue at the start and a changed life at the end, is what marks his union off from a feeling. The trial asked a version of that question too. Each volunteer named three adults who saw them regularly, and about two months later those people rated how the volunteer had changed.307 After the psilocybin day they reported small but significant positive changes in behaviour and attitudes.308 Small is the researchers' own word, and the comparison was with another drug, not with years of virtue. On Plotinus's test the pill is not ruled out, and it is not confirmed either. The feeling alone settles nothing.

What is left is to say what of it stands and what falls. After that, what a person with a scattered life can actually do with what stands.

Part 20: Giving Back the Divine

What stands of Plotinus is larger than what falls. Nobody has refuted his argument that oneness is received rather than made, and every rival to it pays a price. His claim that the source is not a thing stands in the narrow form his own argument earns. There is a source of oneness, and nothing more can be said of it. His picture of a self that lives wherever its attention lives can be tested by anyone, today. What falls is his account of a good world, at the point where it meets the person who suffers through no fault of their own. And what stays costly, all the way through, is the body.

Porphyry records the last words Plotinus spoke. As he was dying, Plotinus said that he was striving to give back the divine in himself to the divine in the All.309 That sentence holds the whole system in a few words. The divine in himself is the top of the soul, the part that never came down. The All is the whole that every part of the world belongs to, held together by soul and flowing from the One. And giving back is the turning, the attention moving from the cellar to the upper floor, for the last time.

Begin with what stands, and with the strongest piece of it. Forty people waiting for forty buses are a crowd, and the same forty singing one song are a choir. Nothing in any one of them makes the difference. Every theory of what does make it has a cost. Some make a whole of your shoe and the moon, and some deny that there is one thing that is you. Plotinus's climbs to something with no parts at all. The question is open in exactly the place he left it, and anyone who thinks it is easy has not tried to answer it.

His account of how the world comes from its source is coherent too, as far as it goes. The spring that feeds the rivers without deciding to and without running low is a real alternative to a maker who chooses. Its price is that nothing at the top cares for anyone, and that price should be paid in full by anyone who accepts it. Several smaller arguments stand on their own, apart from the system. The photograph cannot tell you whether it is a good likeness. The one who feels a crushed finger is one, however many cells fire. And the seed that grows toward a whole it will never reach by growing describes any life lived one day at a time. Two more stand without the system's help. Freedom placed in the inner act gives a line visible in any evening. On one side is the rage or craving that uses a person's hands, and on the other the understanding that decides. And many people refuse a mere feeling of their own good, which shows that what they care about is not their caring.

Now what falls. His account of a good world breaks at the victim, and it breaks in a precise way. It can explain the beaten child, as the result of another soul turning down the staircase. It cannot justify that suffering to the child. The play leaves out the terror, and the past lives blame without evidence. The true self that is never harmed helps only those who live from it. So the question is what a person with a scattered life can actually do with what stands, and the answer is short enough to carry. The first thing is to start where Plotinus starts, with order. A life that is a heap of competing pulls is a troubled mirror, and nothing shows in it. The ordinary virtues, courage, justice, self-control and good sense, are the first stilling of the surface, and they are how anyone begins.317 The second thing is to do the looking directly, at least sometimes. Most of a week is circuit, action aimed at a looking that never comes because the next thing always arrives first. The woman by the sea worked a year for a fortnight of seeing, and there is no reason the seeing has to wait a year. The third thing is to take away rather than add. The sculptor finds the statue by cutting away what hides it, and Plotinus's instruction is never to stop cutting.318 A scattered life rarely needs one more thing. It usually needs fewer. The fourth thing is the line from the evenings. The pull toward the next thing will come, and nobody chooses it. What a person can do is notice it and withhold the agreement, which on Plotinus's account is where freedom is kept or lost.319

That leaves the child. Nothing Plotinus wrote justifies the child's suffering, and nothing that stands of his philosophy should be used to make that suffering look acceptable. What follows is a step taken here, and it is not one Plotinus took. He told his readers to watch the world's cruelties as scenes in a play.320 He said the wise man's state does not hang on the fortunes of others.321 His own lines point toward looking on.
