Cold open

A young monk sits in meditation day after day, because he wants to become a buddha. His teacher picks up a roof tile, sits down near him, and starts rubbing it against a stone. The monk asks what he is doing. The teacher says he is polishing the tile to make a mirror. The monk points out that no amount of polishing will turn a tile into a mirror. And the teacher answers that no amount of sitting will turn a man into a buddha.1

That exchange is the whole of Zen in one scene. Zen says the trouble with a suffering mind is not its thoughts. The trouble is the hand that reaches: for things, for a better version of yourself, and in the end for peace itself. So the person who sits down to empty their mind and arrive at a calm, special state is not curing the trouble. They are doing it, more quietly.

Most people who are drawn to Zen believe almost exactly the opposite. They have heard that Zen means emptying the mind, living in the moment and getting past the ego. They picture enlightenment as a state, clear and silent, that you reach by sitting long enough and hard enough. They hear the famous riddles, like the sound of one hand, as tricks for breaking the thinking mind. Once the thinking breaks, the state is supposed to shine through. It is a reasonable picture, and it is the one the old teachers spent their lives taking apart.

The reason they had to take it apart is a problem that sits at the centre of Zen and never leaves. If grasping is the disease, then grasping at the cure is more of the disease. A person cannot try to stop trying. Any attempt to get free of grasping that needs the result to fill a lack is itself a grasp. The harder the attempt, the tighter the grip. Zen did not discover this problem by accident. It built its whole teaching around it.

Each answer Zen gave after that was a response to that one problem. One said that awakening comes all at once, because it is recognised and never built. One said that the ordinary mind, eating and washing its bowl, is already the way. One said to stop seeking, and to kill the Buddha if you meet him on the road. One handed students a single word and told them to choke on it until the seeking wore itself out. And one said to sit, and to want nothing from the sitting at all. Each answer holds up to a point, and the point where each one gives way teaches something about how a mind works.

Then the whole teaching breaks in two places, and both are real. The first is that a calm mind which sticks to nothing turns out to have no values of its own. In the twentieth century that mind was handed to soldiers, and some highly respected Zen teachers in Japan blessed the handing.2 The second is quieter and cuts deeper. The special inner experience that most people think Zen is about may be partly a modern way of describing it.3 The old teachers would have distrusted that description.4

What is left standing after both breaks is smaller than the promise of a silent mind. It is also more useful, and more honest, and it begins with the tile.

Part 1: Polishing a Tile

Zen begins by refusing the most natural idea anyone has about meditation. The natural idea is that meditation is a machine for producing a state: you put in hours of sitting and you get out a quiet mind. Zen's founding stories say that this picture gets the problem backwards. The effort to manufacture a quiet mind is made of the same restlessness it is trying to cure. And there is now a little evidence from psychology for one step of that argument.

That picture is not foolish, and it should be heard at its best first. The mind is noisy. Thoughts arrive without being asked for, most of them about yourself, most of them worried or rehearsing or replaying something. Attention can be trained, the way a hand can be trained on a piano. So it seems to follow that if you sit every day and bring your attention back each time it wanders, the noise will thin. Sit long enough and it might stop altogether. Whatever is left when it stops, clear and silent, would be what the old texts call awakening. On this picture enlightenment is a skill with a summit, and the summit is a state.

Now the story in full, because it was told to attack exactly that picture. The young monk in it is called Mazu, and he grew up to be one of the great teachers of Zen. He is sitting in meditation all day. His teacher asks him what he is sitting for, and Mazu says he wants to become a buddha. So the teacher picks up a roof tile and begins to rub it. He is polishing it, he says, to make a mirror.5 When Mazu objects that this cannot work, the teacher turns the objection around and adds a second picture. When a cart drawn by an ox does not move, he asks, should you whip the ox, or the cart.6

That last line puts the whole claim as sharply as it can be put. A man who sits in order to become a buddha has made the buddha into a thing he lacks and is trying to get. He has set up a gap between where he is and where he wants to be, and he is working to close it. Wanting, lacking and working to close a gap is the whole shape of craving. It is the same shape as wanting money, or a better body, or the respect of the people at work. The object has changed to something holy, and the grip has not changed at all.

Where this story comes from matters, and the answer holds for nearly every famous Zen story. Mazu lived in China in the eighth century. The tile story first appears in writing about two hundred years later. It comes from a book of teachers' lives, compiled long after the people in it had died.9 Most of the famous Zen stories are like this. A historian who spent his career dating them put it in one sentence. It's not true, and therefore it's more important. He meant that these are not transcripts.10 They are arguments, written and rewritten by people who were trying to say what Zen is, and that is exactly how they deserve to be read.

So the argument, stated plainly, is that the effort to produce a calm state is a form of the grasping it wants to end. That is a strong claim, and it can be tested on something smaller than enlightenment. Take one thought, and try to keep it out of your head.

Here is what most people would expect. If you try hard not to think about something, you will mostly succeed, the way you can mostly keep your hand off a hot stove. A famous experiment from 1987 seemed to show the opposite. People were asked not to think about a white bear, and the bear kept coming back. It seemed to come back even more once they were allowed to think of it again.11 That result became one of the best known findings in psychology, and it would suit Zen perfectly. It is also, on the latest evidence, overstated.12

In 2020 three psychologists pooled the experiments that had tested it since. What they found sits between the common guess and the famous story. While people are actively pushing a thought away, they mostly manage it. The exception is when their mind is under strain, tired or busy or carrying another load, and then the pushing fails.13 The comeback afterwards, the bear rushing back in, turned out to be small. Small studies with striking results get published more often, and once the authors corrected for that, the comeback might not exist at all.14 In their own words, the insidious effects of thought suppression may be overstated.

Now apply that to the person on the cushion who is trying to have no thoughts at all. Their quiet mind has a guard posted at the door, scanning every arrival. The guard is a mind watching its own contents, which is the very self-watching they sat down to escape. Anyone who has used a meditation app and found themselves checking whether they are calm yet has met this guard. And on the days when they are tired or upset, which are the days they most want the calm, the guard is all that is left. The emptied mind, made by effort, is a full mind with one more job to do.

The bear tested keeping a thought out. A smaller pair of experiments tested the act itself, the one the person on the cushion is doing, which is trying to relax. Students were asked to relax while holding a long number in mind, or while answering a test they had been told measured intelligence. All the while, a sensor on the skin recorded how keyed-up the body was. Being told to relax ought to help, or at the very worst do nothing. Under that kind of pressure it did the reverse. The students who were trying to relax were more keyed-up than the students who had not been asked to.16

That is the tile. The monk is right to want to be free, and wrong about what kind of thing freedom is. He has treated it as a product, and a product needs a producer. The producer is the restless self, working harder.

There is an obvious objection, and it is a fair one. Zen monks sit. They sit more than almost anyone, for hours a day and for weeks on retreat, and the word Zen itself comes from a word for meditation.18 A school that mocks a man for meditating, and then makes its own monks meditate all day, seems not to believe its own story. The answer is that the story is about what sitting is for, and nothing in it says to stop sitting. What sitting is for, if it is not for producing anything, is a question Zen took centuries to answer properly.

What can be said now is which half of the common picture stays and which half goes. What stays is that the restless mind really is the problem, and that practice really does matter. What goes is the idea that the cure is a quieter mind manufactured by effort. On that idea calm is a state you lack today and will own one day, if you work hard enough. The teacher with the tile was not telling Mazu to give up. He was telling him that he had misplaced the problem, and that the misplacing was the problem.

So the first step is a negative one. Silencing the thoughts is not the cure, because the silencing is one more thing the restless mind does. That leaves the question the whole teaching has to answer next, which is what keeps a mind suffering, if its thoughts do not.

Part 2: Bring Me Your Mind

What keeps a mind suffering, on Zen's account, is something the mind does, and the name for it is grasping. Grasping is a particular kind of reach toward what is wanted, and push against what is not. Wanting and doing are not grasping as such: washing a cup is not grasping. Grasping is reaching with the feeling that you are incomplete until you get the result, and the restless mind is that reaching, going on. The further claim is the hard one. The self that wants peace, the one who sits down and tries to get calm, is made by the same reaching. It is a product of the activity it wants to stop.

The story Zen tells about this is one of its oldest, and it has the same kind of history as the tile. It concerns Bodhidharma, the Indian monk whom later Zen remembered as its founder in China, and a student who came to him in distress. The student said that his mind was not at peace, and asked the teacher to pacify it. Bodhidharma answered with an offer. Bring me your mind, and I will.19 The student went away and looked, and came back, and in one telling his report is careful. No matter how I might look, the mind is not a thing I can find. And Bodhidharma said, there, I have pacified your mind for you.2021

On a first hearing that sounds like a trick with words, and the objection deserves to be put at full strength. Of course nobody can hand over their mind like a parcel. A mind is not a stone in a pocket, and failing to produce one proves nothing about whether you are anxious. The student's distress was real. He would have felt the same tightness in his chest after the conversation as before it. If Zen begins with a pun, it is a clever pun about a serious pain, and it cures nothing.

The answer is in what the student actually had to do. Bodhidharma did not argue with him about whether minds exist. He sent him to look for the thing that was suffering, and the looking is where the argument lives. It can be repeated by anyone, on any bad evening.

Try to find the mind that is not at peace. What turns up, when you actually look, is a series of particular things. There is a tight feeling under the ribs. There is a picture of tomorrow's meeting going badly, and a sentence about how you always do this. There is a pull to check your phone. Each of these arrives, is there for a moment, and gives way to the next. None of them is the unpeaceful mind. The tightness is a sensation, the picture is a picture, and the sentence is a sentence. The unrest is not sitting inside any one item on the list. It is in what happens between them, in the leaning toward the next one and away from the last.

That is the shape of the claim, and it needs two cases side by side to come clear. In the first, you have a headache, you notice it, and you go on reading. It hurts, and it is one more thing in the room. In the second, you have exactly the same headache. This time you think that it always comes at the worst moment, that it will ruin the evening, and that it might be something serious. Then you get angry with yourself for worrying about a headache. The pain in the two cases is identical. What differs is a second layer, made by the mind's reaction to the first, and the second layer is most of what makes the evening bad.

Buddhism had a picture for that second layer long before Zen existed, and Zen inherited it whole. In one of the early discourses the Buddha says that a painful feeling is like an arrow. The untaught person, struck by the first arrow, is then struck by a second.22 The discourse says what the two arrows are. The untaught person feels two pains, one in the body and one in the mind. The person who has learned how this works feels only the pain in the body.23 Here the first arrow will mean any real blow, even one felt in the mind, which is wider than the old discourse.

The same discourse names one more move, and it is the one that matters most for anyone who has tried to calm themselves down. The untaught person, hit by pain, goes looking for pleasure, because he knows no other way out of the pain. The reach for relief belongs to the second arrow too. The push against the headache and the pull toward the distraction are one movement, seen from two ends. That one movement is what Buddhism calls craving and Zen calls grasping.

Now the student's discovery makes sense, and it is no pun. He was asked to bring the thing that was suffering, and he found sensations and pictures and sentences, each one passing. He did not find a fixed object called his mind that the suffering belonged to. What he had been treating as a broken thing to be repaired turned out to be an activity. An activity cannot be handed over, because it is not a thing. What Bodhidharma pacified was the search for a mind to fix. In that moment of looking and not finding, the student was not firing the second arrow.

This leads to the hard part of the claim, the part about the self. Watch what happens at the moment of reaching. There is a sense of someone who needs the pain to stop, someone who must get through the meeting, someone who is failing to be calm. That sense of someone is strongest exactly when the reaching is strongest. On a quiet afternoon with nothing at stake it is faint. In a panic it fills the whole room. Zen reads that pattern as a clue to what the self is. The felt self is less like a driver sitting behind the wheel and more like the speed of the car. It is real while the car is moving, and there is nowhere to find it when the car stops. It is the reaching, felt from inside.

That is the reason the restless mind cannot simply decide to stop. The one who would decide is a product of the very restlessness it wants to end. Laid out as plain steps, the argument runs like this. First, something uncomfortable happens: a pain, a worry, a bad memory. Second, the mind reaches, to push it away, to fix it, or to grab something that will cover it. Third, that reaching is felt from inside as a self, someone with a problem who needs it solved. Fourth, that self, now feeling incomplete, reaches again, and the loop feeds itself. The person who sits down to calm themselves is reaching, with calm as the object. And so they are feeding the very sense of a someone who needs rescue.

A natural reply follows. If reaching is the problem, then the cure must be to stop reacting. Feel the headache, refuse the second arrow, and hold still. But that instruction is the guard at the door all over again. Refusing the second arrow by force means watching for it and pushing it away, which is a third arrow aimed at the second. Anyone who has told themselves to stop worrying has fired that one.

What the student did was different, and the difference is small enough to miss. He did not push his unrest away. He went looking for it, honestly, as an object, and the looking showed that there was no object there. The unrest did not end because he fought it. It loosened because the thing it depended on, the belief that there was a broken mind to repair, did not survive being examined. That is the first sign of how Zen would go about its work. Its method would be seeing rather than fixing, and every school that followed would argue about what that seeing is and how it happens.

A claim this strong invites being taken too far, so its limit needs saying. None of this says that anxiety is imaginary or that a serious anxiety disorder dissolves when you look at it. The first arrow is real, and sometimes it is very large. The claim is narrower and still strong. A great deal of ordinary suffering is the second arrow. The second arrow depends on reaching, and the one who reaches is not the solid thing it feels like.

So the diagnosis is in place. What suffers is an activity of grasping, and the self that wants rescue is that activity felt from inside. That is why it cannot rescue itself by trying harder. The student looked for a mind and found no fixed thing. Every later Zen teacher had to face what follows from that. If the mind is no fixed thing, something has to be said about what is there instead.

Part 3: Form Is Emptiness

When the student looked for his mind and found no fixed thing, he found something Buddhism says is true of everything. The word for it is emptiness, and it is the most misunderstood word in the whole tradition. Emptiness does not mean that nothing exists, or that the world is a void. It means that nothing stands on its own. Everything is made of its conditions, and has no core apart from them. And the tradition's own sharpest warning is that grasping emptiness itself, as an idea or a feeling, is the worst grasping of all.

Start with a fist. Close your hand, and there is a fist. It is real in every way that matters: it can knock on a door, and it can hurt someone. Now open your hand. Nobody asks where the fist went, because it is obvious that it never was a separate thing. It was fingers held in a certain way, and when the holding stopped, so did the fist. Now take a stone. A stone seems to be the opposite of a fist. It has been a stone for longer than anyone has been alive. Nobody holds it together, and it seems to be what it is all by itself.

Now look at the stone the way you looked at the fist. It is grains pressed together by weight and heat, over a long time, in a riverbed that is no longer there. It is a stone because rain has not yet worn it into sand. It is even a stone, rather than a seat or a weapon, partly because of what you want from it. Every one of those conditions is doing the work the fingers did in the fist. The only difference between the two cases is speed. The fist comes apart in a second and the stone in ten thousand years. Neither one has a core that is there apart from its conditions.

That is all emptiness means, and the best known line in Buddhist scripture says it in a few words. It comes from the short scripture called the Heart Sutra. Form itself is emptiness, emptiness itself is form.24 Form is anything that appears: the stone, the fist, the tight feeling under your ribs. To say that form is emptiness is to say that each of these is like the fist, made of conditions with no core. The second half matters just as much. Emptiness itself is form, which means that emptiness is not a second place behind the world, some great blank you could go to. It is just the way the stone and the fist are. There is no emptiness anywhere except in forms.

An objection as old as the idea comes straight back, and it is serious. If everything is empty, then nothing matters. Your child is empty, your promises are empty, and the suffering of other people is empty too. There seems no reason to keep a promise that has no core, or to help a person who is only conditions. On this objection emptiness is just nihilism with good manners, a teaching that dissolves the world and leaves nobody responsible for anything.

That last line is the hinge of everything that follows, so it is worth seeing what it rules out. It rules out the person who learns that everything is empty and then holds on to that idea. It rules out the one who uses it to stop caring, or to feel above the people who still care. And it rules out the meditator who reaches a quiet, blank state and decides that the blank is emptiness. In each case the person has made emptiness into one more possession. Emptiness was supposed to loosen the grip on everything, and they have closed their hand around emptiness instead.

Emptiness works a little like soap. It takes the grease off everything it touches, and then it has to be rinsed off too, or you are left covered in soap. The likeness holds for the order, first cleaning and then going. It fails in one place, because soap is a substance you add, and emptiness adds nothing to the world. It is only the noticing that nothing in the world was ever standing alone.

So two things are now in hand. Emptiness means dependence, the fist and not the stone, and it leaves the ordinary world exactly as real as it was. And emptiness held as a view, or sought as a state, is the snake by the tail. The second point closes a door that the meditator in the first story was trying to open. If even emptiness is not a thing to be grasped, then awakening is not a thing to be built or obtained either.

That leaves Zen with a question it would fight over for centuries. The awakened mind is not a product, made by sitting or by effort or by understanding emptiness. So it might already be here, and the work might be only to stop covering it. The question is whether that is true, and if it is, why anyone would still need to practise.

Part 4: The Mirror and the Dust

If the awakened mind is already present in everyone, then practice cannot be a way of cleaning it, because there is nothing on it to clean. Awakening, on that view, has to be sudden. It is recognising something that was always there, and recognition does not happen a little at a time. That claim split Zen in two. The honest ruling on it is that it is right, and that it costs more than its champions liked to admit.

Zen tells this argument as a contest. An old master asks his monks to write a verse showing what they understand, so that he can choose an heir. Everyone expects the senior monk to win, and he writes his verse on a wall in the night. The body is the tree of awakening, his verse says, and the mind is like a bright mirror on its stand. Carefully we wipe them hour by hour, and let no dust alight.30 That monk was a real and respected teacher named Shenxiu. The story was written by the rivals of his school, so it is a story with a side.

Part 5: No-Thought

Zen's most famous instruction about the mind is "no-thought", and it means almost the opposite of what it sounds like. It does not mean having no thoughts. It means that thoughts arise and pass without the mind settling on any of them, the way a hand can touch things without closing on them. The sermon attributed to Huineng says this in so many words. Then it attacks the blank mind by name, as a mistake and even as a danger.

The sermon builds its teaching on a few key words, and two of them matter here. We take no-thought as the doctrine, it says, and non-abiding as the basis. Then it defines them. No-thought is described as being in thought, without thought.46 An older English translation puts it more plainly. No-thought means not to be carried away by any particular idea in the exercise of the mental faculty.47 The thinking goes on. What stops is being carried away.

A concrete case shows what that means in an ordinary day. At ten in the morning a colleague makes a cutting remark in a meeting. It stings, and the sting is real, the first arrow. In one version of the day, you feel it, you answer or you let it go, and by lunch you are eating lunch. In the other version, the remark links to a thought about what they meant by it. That links to a memory of the last time, and that to a reply you should have made. The reply links to a speech you rehearse in the car, and at midnight you are still in the meeting. The remark was the same in both versions, and so was the sting. What differed was whether the thoughts were allowed to link.

Take the particulars away and the case still works. The colleague could be a bill, a symptom, a message left unanswered, or a face in a crowd. Any event at all gives rise to thoughts, and that part cannot be helped and does not need to be. Suffering is in the chain the mind builds out of them, and in settling into that chain as if it were a place to live. Said generally, thoughts are not the problem; abiding in them is.

An everyday object makes the difference easy to keep. This is not the dusty mirror of the senior monk's verse, the one that needed wiping; it is a mirror doing what mirrors do. A mirror and a phone's camera both receive whatever passes in front of them. The mirror shows everything, completely and at once, and the moment the thing moves away the mirror holds nothing of it. The camera keeps every image, adds it to a roll, and you can scroll back through the roll for years. A mind that abides is a camera roll. A mind that does not abide is a mirror. Both minds see. Only one of them keeps scrolling.

The sermon has a line for the mirror mind that is better than any summary of it. When in use it pervades everywhere, and yet it sticks nowhere.49 It reaches everything it meets, and holds on to none of it.

There is a natural worry here. A mind that sticks to nothing sounds like a mind that cares about nothing. It sounds passive, spaced out, the person who says whatever, as their life goes wrong. If thoughts pass through without stopping, then plans, promises, grief and love pass through too, and nothing is held long enough to matter.

The sermon's own words meet it in two ways. First, the sermon already rejected that state. Its phrase for the blank sitter's condition means a blank, neutral emptiness, the mind gone flat. No-thought is the opposite of that flatness. Second, look again at the meeting. The person whose thoughts linked into a chain was not more engaged with life. At midnight they were less engaged with everything around them, because they were somewhere else entirely, in a room that had emptied twelve hours earlier. The person who let the remark pass was present for lunch, and for the afternoon. If an answer was needed, they could give it the next day with a clear head.

The mirror image breaks down at exactly the point the worry presses. A mirror receives without keeping, but it is passive, and it does not answer the face in front of it. The non-abiding mind does answer, and Huineng insists on it: circumstances react on his mind. It is a mirror that can act, and it acts better than the camera roll because it is acting on what is here.

Gathered up, the argument is short. Events give rise to thoughts, and that cannot and need not be stopped. Suffering grows when thoughts link into chains and the mind settles into them. Trying to stop thoughts altogether is one more chain, and at its worst, the sermon says, it breaks the person trying. What Zen calls no-thought is thought met fully and not dwelt in.

That is a sharper claim than the one most people start with. The aim was never a quiet head. The aim is a head in which thoughts come and go and the mind stays where life is happening. And if that is right, something follows that sounds too simple to be a teaching. The awake mind is not an exotic state far from ordinary life. It is ordinary life, met without sticking.

No-thought means not abiding, and the blank mind is a failure Zen named itself. Some later teachers drew the bolder conclusion outright. If the awake mind is just the ordinary mind without the sticking, then eating, working and washing up are already the way. They meant it literally.

Part 6: Ordinary Mind

The boldest claim in Zen is that awakening is right here, in the ordinary mind. It is the mind eating, working and washing up, doing what it already does, with the grasping gone out of it. That claim holds up, and it holds up better than its critics allowed, because its makers defined "ordinary" with great care. But it carries a weakness in its own definition, and the weakness is ethical.

The claim has its classic form in a short exchange. Zhaozhou, then a young monk, asks his teacher what the Way is. The teacher answers in four words. Ordinary mind is the Way.55 Zhaozhou asks the obvious next question, which is whether he should try to direct himself toward it. And the teacher answers that the moment you try to direct yourself toward it, you turn away from it.56

That exchange contains Zen's whole paradox in a few lines. If the Way is the ordinary mind, then aiming at it is a reach, and the reach takes you out of the ordinary mind. The person who sets out to be ordinary on purpose has become extraordinary in the worst sense, someone performing ordinariness. So the claim describes something that is lost the moment it is pursued, and no one can follow it as an instruction.

Half of this objection is answered by Mazu's own definition. Ordinary mind, for him, is defined by the absence of grasping and rejecting. The other half is not answered. Look again at Mazu's list. Alongside no grasping and no rejecting, it says no right and wrong. That is meant as freedom from the judging, comparing mind, and in a meditation hall it may be exactly that. So Zongmi is half right. Mazu's definition answers the loosest form of his charge, because ordinary mind was never "whatever you do"; it was always "whatever you do without grasping". His ethical worry stays open, because the definition leaves right and wrong unaddressed and puts nothing in their place. The sermon alone cannot settle that half, because it never meets a case where the stakes are real. History supplied one, a thousand years on and on a terrible scale.

The awake mind, then, is the ordinary mind without fabricating and without grasping, and it is lost the moment it is aimed at. Its teachers refused to let even their best formulas be held. The test of a student was whether what they had seen survived a change of words. But if aiming at the Way turns you away from it, then seeking itself looks like the obstacle. That is a large claim, and Buddhism had already heard the plainest objection to it, which is that nobody gets anywhere without wanting to.

Part 7: Using Desire to End Desire

Any thoughtful friend raises the first objection to all this, and it is a good one. Nobody gets better without wanting to get better. If reaching is the disease, then the wish to be cured is a symptom, and every path is closed before it starts. Early Buddhism heard this objection and answered it, and the answer is right about most wanting. Zen's paradox survives for one kind only: the reach whose target is a state of your own mind, checked against a felt gap. So the paradox is real, and it is narrower than it first sounded.

The objection appears in an early discourse. A brahmin, a priest of the older religion, puts it to the Buddha's attendant. The attendant has just described a path whose first step is desire, the plain wish to act. The brahmin sees the trap at once. For it's not possible to give up desire by means of desire. And if that is so, he says, the path is endless, not finite, because every step feeds the thing it was meant to end.67

This is the strongest objection the paradox faces, and it comes from inside Buddhism itself. It also matches ordinary life. A person who wants to learn to swim stops wanting it on the day they can swim. If the park is right, then reaching for the cure is no part of the disease. Wanting to be free is a wish like wanting the park, and arriving would end it. The conclusion that seemed to follow from the ordinary mind, that seeking should simply stop, would then be a mistake. The seeker would only need to keep walking.

Two cases show where the park stops working. In the first, a woman wants to reach the park at the end of her street. She reads the signs, walks, and arrives. Her checking looked at the world, and the world can tell her she is there. In the second, the same woman sits on a cushion wanting to be calm. Now she checks her own mind, to see whether the calm has come. The check is a small reach away from what is happening, toward a better state, and it finds the gap it was looking for. She is further from calm for having looked. It is the guard at the door from the white bear experiment, now watching for calm instead of for a bear.

What separates the two cases is where the target sits. The park is somewhere else, so walking closes the distance and the wish runs out. Zen places its park where you already stand. The awake mind, on its account, is already present, and the moment you try to direct yourself toward it, you turn away from it. That reply is Zen's own, built from its own premises, and the old discourse never makes it. A wish checked against the world can be satisfied. A wish checked against the state of your own mind keeps finding a gap, because the checking makes one. Money wanted for a purchase is like the park. Craving for money or status is not. What it checks is a feeling of being enough, and that is a state of your own mind too. The cushion is the sharpest case, and not the only one.

So the ruling runs both ways. The attendant is right that wanting as such is not the disease. That fits what grasping meant from the start: washing a cup is not grasping, and neither is walking to a park. Zen is right about one kind of wanting, and the argument for it has four steps. First, a wish ends when it reaches what it wants. Second, the wish for calm aims at a state of your own mind, and measures its progress by checking for that state. Third, if what is sought is already here, every check is a step away from it. Fourth, this wish therefore cannot be satisfied by checking for it, and each check renews it. The paradox is real. It is narrower than the first sketch of grasping made it sound, when every reach, for things and for peace alike, looked like the same hand.

That ruling sorts what to keep from what to drop. Wanting to sit can stay, and so can wanting to help a friend, or wanting to get to the park. Each points at something to do or somewhere to go, and doing it ends it. What goes is wanting the calm, and checking whether it has come.

Desire can end desire, then, as long as what it wants lies somewhere else. The one wish that cannot end that way is the wish for a state of mind that, on Zen's account, you already have. Zen's teachers aimed their hardest words at that wish, and at every holy image it could fasten on. What they told it to do was stop, and the question is whether that order can be obeyed at all.

Part 8: Kill the Buddha

The seeking that blocks the way is the reach for a state of one's own mind. If so, every picture of that state becomes part of the obstacle, and that includes the Buddha. The Buddha in question is the awakened state given a face. Every ideal, every teacher and every picture of awakening is a place where that reach can settle. The more sacred the thing, the more comfortably it settles there. This is why Zen attacks its own holy things. But this answer runs into the paradox at its purest, because "stop seeking" is itself an order, and nobody can obey it by trying.

The line that made this famous belongs to Linji, a Chinese teacher of the ninth century. If you meet the Buddha, kill the Buddha. If you meet the patriarch, kill the patriarch. The list goes on, through saints and teachers and even parents and relatives. Only then, Linji says, will you be free, and not bound by things.70

The diagnosis comes out of this sharpened. Linji was right that the image of awakening is the last and strongest object of grasping. He was right that what is sought is already present in the seeing and hearing of this moment. And he was right that seeking is the only thing in the way. Zen did not leave it there, because it could not. Monasteries full of people needed something to do on Monday morning. Two answers came out of the next centuries, and they divided the tradition. One used the koan, a short, baffling exchange set to a student as a problem. Its aim was to make the seeking run until it exhausts itself. The other said to sit and want nothing from the sitting. A koan is made of words, and the sitting is meant to need none. So both rest on a view of what words can and cannot do.

Part 9: A Finger Pointing at the Moon

Zen's distrust of words is the part of it most often mistaken for nonsense, and it is one of the most exact things in it. Every word about awakening is a pointer. A pointer works when it is followed and fails the moment it is held. Zen's strange sayings are pointers built so that they cannot be held. That is a different thing from throwing words away, and Zen's own texts attack the people who threw them away.

The belief this replaces has two forms, and both are common. One says the riddles mean nothing, and that Zen hides an empty centre behind clever talk. The other likes Zen for exactly that reason. Thinking is the problem, it says, so drop the words, stop analysing, and just feel. Both forms agree that Zen is against meaning. Both are wrong about the same thing.

Zen states its own position in four lines, which later tradition put into Bodhidharma's mouth. A special transmission outside the scriptures. Not depending on words and letters.80 Pointing directly at the human mind. Seeing one's nature and becoming a buddha. A Chinese reference book written many centuries after Bodhidharma records the four lines, and at once warns against the obvious misreading. Many people, it says, throw out words and take silent sitting for Chan, and it calls them the dumb sheep of the school. Its own reading of not depending on words is precise: right in the words, the words cannot be grasped.81

The serious charge against all this is easy to state. A teaching that says the truth cannot be put into words has protected itself from every criticism. Whatever you object, the answer is that you have not seen it. That move can hide anything, including nothing at all, behind a riddle and a confident smile. A sceptic would say Zen has built itself a wall that no argument can reach.

Part of that has to be granted. The move can be abused, and a teacher who answers every question with a shout may have nothing to say. But the objection mixes up two things that are worth keeping apart. One is the argument about the mind, and that can be stated plainly. The second arrow, the fist and the stone, the mirror and the camera roll: each of these is a claim. Each can be followed, tested and disagreed with. The other is the seeing, and that is what cannot be handed over, like the laugh.

Zen's distrust of words, then, is justified for the seeing and not for the argument. Where a Zen text refuses to explain the seeing, it is being honest about what words can do. Where a teacher refuses to explain the argument, the sceptic is right to be suspicious. The sermons and letters mostly pass this test, arguing plainly and falling silent only where the listener has to look. The short stories of teachers and students often do not, and their argument has to be supplied by the listener.

Zen's words are fingers, meant to be followed and then let go. Its own tradition attacked both the people who held on to them and the people who threw them away. But a finger that can be held will be held, by a seeker determined to get something. Zen's answer was a pointer that the seeking mind cannot hold at all, and that pointer is the koan.

Part 10: Mu

The koan was the first method built to answer the paradox, and it is cleverer than it looks. A koan is not a riddle with a hidden answer. It is a device for letting the seeking mind run at full strength into something it cannot get past, until the seeking wears itself out. Linji's words, taken as an order to stop seeking, could not be obeyed. The koan never asks the seeker to stop. It gives the seeking everything it wants, except an answer.

Set the koan beside the thing it is usually mistaken for. A riddle asks what has keys but cannot open a single lock. You turn it over, you try a few answers, and then you hit on it: a piano. The answer pays you, and the seeking mind gets exactly what it came for. The koan looks the same from outside: a question and a strange answer. But the comment forbids every answer in advance. Not nothingness, which is the clever, mystical answer. Not yes or no, which is the doctrinal answer. Whatever the seeking mind produces, it has been told beforehand that this is not it.

So a riddle feeds the seeker, and a koan starves it while keeping it fully awake. That is the whole design. It asks the seeker to seek harder than they have ever sought anything, and gives them a target with no answer they can reach by seeking. The bet is that a grip squeezed to its limit lets go. All the reaching that was spread across a life is gathered into one point and driven into one wall.

What happens at the end of this, on the method's own account, is that the doubt breaks. The seeker who has been driven into the wall finds that the one who was driving has run out. The texts themselves supply the case against this. Look at the language. A hot iron ball in the throat. A mass of doubt in the whole body. Day and night, with no relief. Part 12: What the Laboratory Can Test

Science now offers a kind of outside test, and it can reach some of Zen's claims and not others. The diagnosis, that suffering grows in a loop the mind builds on top of events, can be measured, and it partly holds. The benefits of meditation programmes can be measured too, and they are real and modest. The claim Zen cares about most is out of a trial's reach, and the reason is built into what a test is.

Start with the loop. The colleague's remark at ten in the morning, still running at midnight, was a claim about ordinary days, and ordinary days can be sampled.113 A team of psychologists did exactly that, and they opened their paper by quoting the old idea back to itself. These traditions suggest that a wandering mind is an unhappy mind, they wrote, and then they asked whether the traditions were right. Volunteers were sent a message on their phones at random moments of the waking day. Each time, they said what they were doing, whether their mind was on it, and how happy they felt.

The real limit is philosophical. A trial needs a measure taken before and after: a mood score, a count of relapses. To run at all, it has to treat sitting as scales, a means judged by what it produces. Zen's diagnosis is a claim of exactly that kind: the loop causes suffering, and seeing it loosens it. So a trial can test the diagnosis and the melting, and it cannot test Dogen, or awakening, without first turning them into scales.

The strongest objection says that a claim no test can reach is a claim that says nothing. Every way it could fail has been ruled out in advance, which is the mark of an empty claim. The answer has two parts. Out of reach of a trial is not out of reach of everything, and Dogen's tradition checked the claim through the teacher and the life. That check has never been tested either, and a teacher can fail it. And the claim's other half, the warning about sitting for a result, does reach the lab. Sitting done for an outcome turns into watching for the outcome, which is the guard at the door. The small relaxation studies found hints of that guard, and no more. What survives is a clean division: the lab partly supports the moving parts of the diagnosis, and it neither confirms nor refutes the core.

For the person with the app, this sorts two projects that are easy to blur. Using a short daily exercise to lower stress is a legitimate project with modest support. Zen is a different project, and mixing the two up is what produces the checking. It is sitting with Dogen's words in mind while scoring the session, asking every few minutes whether calm has arrived yet. That checking is the scales, played in the middle of the music.

So the laboratory can test the loop and the slow melting, and finds them partly sound, and it finds the benefits real and modest. It cannot reach the claim that sitting is awakening. And the trials mostly tested short, gentle courses taken by beginners. Dogen's sitting was gentle too. Others in the same tradition made practice fierce, and drove the doubt until something broke, and what broke was sometimes the person. The trials asked whether sitting helps, and the fierce methods force the opposite question, whether a method strong enough to loosen a self can hurt.

Part 13: Zen Sickness

The methods that loosen the grip of the self are strong. Anything strong enough to loosen a self is strong enough to hurt the person who has one. Zen knew this from the inside. Its own records name a sickness that comes from practising too hard.120 Its own sermon warned that the wrong kind of sitting drives people out of their minds.121 Its protection was never a technique. It was a person who knew the ground. Modern research shows that even that protection can fail, and the phone app removes it altogether.

That forces a more careful version of the claim than the obvious one. People fell ill inside the tradition too, Hakuin among them, and people with teachers struggle today. What survives is narrower and still important. The strongest methods were built to be used under someone who knew the ground. That person could see when practice had gone past measure, and change it.

Now apply that to the person with the meditation app, who has no idea what Zen sickness is. For most people, sitting quietly for ten minutes a day is unlikely to do anything of the kind, and nothing here says otherwise. But some guided practice can slide toward what the sermon warned against: watch your mind, look for stillness, check whether you are calm. And if something strange starts to happen, a fear that will not lift or a sense that the self is coming apart, there is nobody there. The hermit in the cave is not in the app.

A fair-minded listener will push back here. This is fearmongering, they will say. Meditation helps most people who try it, the dangers are rare, and stories of Zen sickness will only scare people away from something good. There is truth in that. Nothing in the evidence shows that meditation harms most people, and the survey's quarter is a quarter of people who stayed with it. But the claim was never that sitting is dangerous. It was that the methods built to dismantle the grasping self have force, and that force needs measure, and measure needs someone who can see.

So the doubt that the koan drives is real medicine and real danger, and the tradition knew both. Dogen's gentle sitting and Hakuin's fierce doubt are tools of very different strength. Each depends on something outside the method to be used well. Behind that lies the harder problem the teaching has carried since ordinary mind left right and wrong unaddressed. Something has to decide whether a method strong enough to take a self apart is used well or badly. The insight itself is the obvious candidate, and history put it to the test.

Part 14: The Sword That Gives Life

The mind that sticks nowhere is a skill of attention, and a skill of attention has no values of its own. It makes a person freer and quicker in whatever they are already doing. It does not say what to do. When Zen spoke of a mind that had stopped sorting right from wrong, it left that question open. In the twentieth century the answer was supplied by a war. Zen's defenders have a reply, and part of it is true, but the record shows that the values always had to come from somewhere else.

Part 15: A Reason to Care

The sword taught that the insight has no values of its own. The strongest reply comes from Indian Buddhism, and it says the opposite. Seeing through the self, it argues, is already a reason to care about everyone's pain. That reply is half right. Seeing through the self erases the line between my pain and yours. The reason to remove pain at all has to be brought in beside it. And what has been measured in people who meditate fits that half, and no more.

The question was left open long before the war. Emptiness showed why anything can be harmed or helped, and it said nothing about whether to help. War is where that question stops being idle, and it has arrived. Two ordinary cases show what is at stake. Your hand touches a hot pan and pulls away before you have thought anything. A stranger across the room touches the same pan, and you wince, but your hand stays where it is. The pain is the same kind of pain. The only difference is whose it is. Common sense says that difference settles everything: my pain is mine to deal with, in a way that yours is not.

In the eighth century, an Indian Buddhist monk wrote a long teaching poem on the Buddhist path. In it he takes that common sense apart. You already protect someone who is not you, he points out, because you work to spare your future self pain that is not yours now.160 Then look for the one who owns the pain, and you find nobody there.161 That is the same search the student made when he went looking for the mind that was suffering, and it ends the same way. What is left is just pain. In one English translation, his conclusion reads: "All sufferings are without an owner". And so, "they should be warded off simply because they are suffering". Then he asks why any line should be drawn at all, and he means it as a challenge.162

Part 16: Is Awakening an Experience?

Most people who come to Zen believe it is about one thing above all: a special inner experience, sudden and total, that changes everything. That belief is partly modern, and the older tradition was stranger and harder. It did prize the moment of breakthrough, and its teachers described theirs vividly. But it never trusted a report of one. It tested awakening by how a person responded and lived, and it treated even the most real experience as something to throw away. And the record shows that even this test had a limit it could not see past.

Suzuki gave the belief its best statement. He wrote that satori, the moment of awakening, is the reason Zen exists, and that without it there is no Zen.174 On this picture everything else, the sitting, the koans, the bowing and the robes, is scaffolding around a single inner event. Get the event and you have Zen. Miss it and you have only a costume.

Part 17: Firewood and Ash

Zen's teaching offers no information about what happens after death, and no comfort of the usual kind. It does not promise that you survive, and it does not tell you that ending is nothing to fear. What it offers is the same thing it offers everywhere else. It removes the grasping that turns the fact of ending into a second arrow. And it refuses to let either "alive" or "dead" become a thing to hold.

Most people expect a spiritual teaching to say something reassuring about death. The reassurance comes in two kinds. One says that something of you continues, a soul, a self, a spark. The other says that death is natural, that you will be at peace, and that acceptance takes the sting out. Both are offered as information or as a mood to adopt, and both give the mind something to hold on to at the end.

Zen turns death into a test question instead. In the most famous koan collection there is a teacher who sets his students three barriers. The second is blunt. When your eyesight fails at the end, how will you be free of birth and death.190 There is no promise in that question about where anyone goes. It asks where you will be standing when everything you hold is taken away.

Part 18: Is Love a Kind of Grasping?

When someone you love dies, missing them is, on the reading defended here, part of the first arrow: the loss itself, as it is felt. What Zen can loosen is something that grows beside the grief, the circling question of why it happened and how it could have gone otherwise. The tradition itself is stricter. The sermon on no-thought treats the tears as a mistake. The old discourse of the two arrows lets the learned person feel only the pain in the body. On this point this reading parts from both, and the teaching's own tools give the reasons.

The objection comes first, and it is the strongest one anyone brings against Zen. Grief is what love does when the person is gone. A mother whose son has died keeps his room as it was, talks to him at the grave, and cries at his photograph. Ask her to let the thought of him pass, like weather, and you have asked her to stop loving him. She has not escaped a second arrow. She has lost her son a second time, by her own hand. On this view, holding on is simply what love is. And if Zen files that under grasping, Zen is wrong about the thing people care about most.

Zen's own text makes the objection stronger before anything can answer it. The sermon on no-thought ends with Huineng's death, and in its later edition he tells his students he will die the following month. The answer comes from tools the teaching already supplied, and each one cuts against the deathbed scene. The first arrow is real, and sometimes it is very large. Few first arrows are larger than a death in the family. No-thought is not no-feeling. The same sermon called the blank, flat mind a failure, and the deathbed praise of the unmoved face sits uneasily beside that. And missing a person is not a belief about where they went, so no correct belief about the dead removes it. The same goes for the two-arrows discourse. Its second arrow is the mind's reaction to pain, and missing someone is the loss itself, felt, before any reaction. Where the discourse counts it a second arrow anyway, this reading departs from it. So the live question is which part of grief is the first arrow, and which part is the second.

Two widows, bereaved in the same month, pull the parts apart. The first cries most days for a long while. She keeps his coat, sets out his cup by mistake, and tells him the news at the grave. Slowly, without deciding to, she starts eating properly again and seeing her friends. The second cries less. But every night she is back in the last week of his life. She asks why nobody caught it sooner, and what would have happened if she had made him see the doctor in March. She stops going to the places they went together, and stops opening the drawer with his photographs. Both women loved their husbands. The second is also carrying something the first is not, and it is the thing Zen has a name for.

So the ruling divides. Zen's claim survives for the loop and fails as a rule against grief. The praise of the unmoved monk is the tradition grasping at no-grief, the snake held by the tail once again. Feeling nothing became the proof of awakening, and a proof is one more thing to hold. Missing the person is the first arrow, and the circling why is the second. The second is a part measurably tied to grief that does not heal, and it is the part Zen's method works on. Applied to the widow, letting the thought pass means letting go of the doctor in March, and keeping the coat.

The mother at the grave keeps her son's room, and her tears, and this reading asks her to give up neither. What it offers her is a way out of the loop, the question at three in the morning that never closes. That leaves one thing to settle: what a person like that looks like at the end of the road, standing in ordinary life among other people.

Part 19: Back to the Marketplace

Zen's complete philosophy does not end in a state. It ends in a way of standing in ordinary life, with the reaching seen through, among other people. And it carries values that the insight did not supply and must not be allowed to dissolve. Zen's own best known teaching pictures end the same way. Their last picture is not emptiness. It is a man walking into town.

The pictures are a set of ten, with verses by a Chinese teacher of the twelfth century.209 They tell the path as the story of a herdsman and an ox. The ox is the true nature, the thing sought. In the early pictures the herdsman searches for it, finds its tracks, glimpses it, catches it, struggles with it, tames it and rides it home. That much is the picture most people already have of spiritual practice: a long search, a hard struggle, a prize won.

From the tile to the market, the whole argument fits in a few plain steps. First, suffering is kept alive less by what happens than by the mind's grasping, the second arrow that pushes at pain and grabs at relief. Second, the self that wants rescue is that reaching felt from inside, the fist and not the stone. Third, everything is like the fist, made of its conditions, and emptiness itself must not be grasped as a view or a state. Fourth, so awakening cannot be produced; it can only be recognised, suddenly, and then lived out slowly, like ice that melts. Fifth, what it recognises is a mind that meets thoughts without dwelling in them, the mirror and not the camera roll. Sixth, that mind is the ordinary mind, and it is lost the moment it is aimed at. Wanting a park or a clean cup is no trouble. The wish for a state you already have is never satisfied by checking for it. That is why the order to stop seeking cannot be obeyed. Seventh, Zen's methods are ways around that paradox: the koan lets seeking burn out, and silent sitting gives it no fuel. Eighth, and most cleanly, Dogen's sitting has no in order to at all, so there is no gap left to reach across.

Then the danger, and the two places where the teaching breaks, each one real. The methods that loosen the self are strong, and pushed past measure they make people ill, so they need someone who can see. The mind that sticks nowhere has no values of its own, and in a war it was handed to whoever held the sword. Loosening the self frees kindness from the preference for one's own side, but it gives kindness no direction. And awakening is not a special state to collect; it shows in how a person acts. Even that, the most the tradition could check, never guaranteed that the person was good.

The picture most people start with can be withdrawn completely. It said that Zen means emptying the mind, by effort, until a calm, special state arrives. Every part of that is gone. The emptying was the guard at the door, and the effort was the reaching. The special state, held as the goal, was the snake by the tail. What is kept is the part that was right from the start: the restless mind really is the trouble, and practice really does matter. Hakuin put the verdict on the old picture in a single sentence, using birth-and-death as his name for the restless round of grasping. All attempts to empty the mind are in themselves a sure sign that birth-and-death is in progress.217

The story began with a young monk sitting all day to become a buddha, and a teacher polishing a tile beside him. That young monk, Mazu, grew up to be the teacher who said that ordinary mind is the Way. Walking, standing, sitting, lying down, responding to things as they come, all of it. The man trying to polish his mind into a mirror became the man who stopped polishing. Nothing was added to him. What fell away was the reaching, and what was left had been there the whole time, eating rice when it comes.
