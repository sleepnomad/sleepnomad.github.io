Cold open

You had an argument this week, and tonight you are running it again. You say your lines better this time. You find the sentence that would have ended it. And under the whole replay sits one quiet certainty: you were right, and if the other person could only see it properly, they would agree.

A writer in ancient China built a whole philosophy on the thing that certainty leaves out. Every judgment of right and wrong, of big and small, of useful and useless, is made from somewhere. The other person is also standing somewhere, and from where they stand, they are right too. And on the plainest reading of his book, there is no third place to stand, above the two of you, that is not also somewhere. The claim is as simple as that. What he builds on it is a way of working, of dealing with dangerous people, of caring for someone, and of facing your own death. All of it grows from the plain fact that you are always standing somewhere.

His name is Zhuangzi, and the book that carries his name is a strange one. It is made of stories and jokes, a talking tree, a giant bird, a cook and his knife. And in among them sit a handful of arguments as tight as anything the Greeks wrote. Its first seven chapters have long been held to come from his own pen. Who wrote the rest is still argued over. So the name means the man and the book at once. When a story about the man himself comes from those later chapters, it is worth remembering that it may be someone else telling it.

Most serious people run their lives on a different picture, and it is a good picture. There is a right answer to the questions that matter, and a good life is spent finding it and holding to it. Arguments are how you find it, because the better argument wins. A person is worth roughly what they are good for. Skill means learning the rules and applying them more carefully than the next person. And death is the worst thing that happens to anyone, so grief is the true measure of love. None of that is naive. It is how careful adults organise a week.

Zhuangzi goes through those beliefs one at a time and finds, under each, a standpoint that cannot see itself. The size of your own flight decides what looks sensible to you. The judge who could settle your argument cannot be found, because every judge is already standing on one side or the other. The self doing the judging keeps changing under you. The best worker in the book follows no rulebook at all, and after nineteen years his knife is as sharp as new. And preferring life to death, he suggests, is preferring the place you happen to be standing right now.

If he is right, the good life is not a search for the one correct standpoint followed by a lifetime of defending it. It is moving well between standpoints, answering each situation through the openings it already has, the way a cook's blade finds the gaps in a joint. And it means loosening your grip on the one standpoint that never feels like one, which is your own self and its fear of ending. There is a price, too. Zhuangzi gives you a way to stand in the world. How to stop the powerful when they force their way on everyone else, or when to resist them, he never says. What follows is what has to be true if he is right, and the exact places where it breaks.

Part 1: The Giant Bird and the Little Dove

What looks big, sensible or useful to you is measured against the size of your own flight. And usefulness is not something a thing has, the way it has a weight. It is a fit between a thing and whoever is using it. That sounds like a small point about tools. By the end of the book it has become a point about your job, your worth, and your death. So it is worth getting exactly right at the start, where the book itself starts, with a bird.

The bird is called Peng, and it is enormous. When it rises, it climbs ninety thousand li, and the number is there to be absurd. Nothing real flies that high. But the book adds a practical detail that is easy to miss. The bird has to go that high, because a wing that size needs a vast thick mass of wind underneath it. If the wind is thin, it has no strength to carry the great wings. So the bird climbs until all the wind is below it, and only then can it ride that wind south.

Then the book adds a line that is easy to read past. The sky looks deep blue, it says. It asks whether that is its real colour, or only what endless distance looks like. Looking down from that height, the text adds, the view would be just the same.

That line matters for everything that follows. It suggests that height is not a view from nowhere. From ninety thousand li up, the ground is as far away as the sky is from the ground, and the same haze hangs in between. The biggest flight in the book still looks from somewhere, and what it sees is shaped by where it is.

Down on the ground, two small creatures watch this and laugh. A cicada and a little dove. We just make a dash for the nearest elm tree, they say, and sometimes we don't even make it and drop back to the ground. What would anyone need to fly ninety thousand li south for, they ask.

Before going further, it is worth checking which side you took. Almost everyone, hearing that, sides with the big bird. The little creatures are mocking what they cannot understand, and we have all met people like that. The book seems to agree. Right after the laughter it draws the lesson in plain words. Small understanding does not reach great understanding, just as a short life does not reach a long one. A mushroom that lives for one morning knows nothing of a month. An insect born in summer knows nothing of a year. That reading is the obvious one, and the rest of the book's opening chapter takes it apart.

The taking apart happens through a friend. Hui Shi is Zhuangzi's friend and his favourite opponent in argument. He comes back later in the book, and each time he stands for the sensible adult view. Here he arrives with a complaint about a vegetable.

The king gave him seeds for a giant gourd, he says. He planted them, and the gourds grew huge. Filled with water, one was too heavy to lift. Split in half to make a ladle, it was too wide and flat to hold anything. So, because they were useless, he smashed them. Zhuangzi's reply is a little impatient. You had a thing that size, he says, and it never crossed your mind to lash it on as a float and drift across the lakes.

Then he tells a story, and this story is the engine of the whole chapter. A family in the state of Song had a salve that kept hands from cracking in the cold. For generations they used it for one thing: washing silk in freezing water, which paid very little. A stranger heard about it and bought the recipe. He took it to the king of Wu, a state at war with its neighbour. That winter the two armies fought on the water, and the soldiers of Wu did not get cracked hands. Wu won, and the stranger was given land of his own. Zhuangzi's comment is the whole point. Keeping hands from cracking was the same in both cases. One man got an estate and the other never got past washing silk, and the only difference was how the salve was used.

Side by side, the two cases differ on one thing only. The salve is identical. The chemistry is identical. The cold water is identical. What changed is the person holding the jar and what that person had in mind. The family asked what the salve was for, and they already knew the answer, because their grandparents had known it. The stranger asked what the salve could do, and the answer depended on where he stood: near a king who needed to fight in winter.

The bird passage already gave this a name, and it fits the family's way of seeing: small understanding. Small understanding is not stupidity, since the silk family were skilled at their work and did it well for a century. It is a use fixed in advance, and then taken for a fact about the thing. Great understanding is the stranger's move: seeing that the use belongs to a standpoint, and that other standpoints make other uses.

The same case runs on something barer. Any object will do: a brick, a rope, a sentence you once said. The brick holds up a wall, or it props open a door, or it breaks a window. None of those uses is in the brick. Each is a meeting between the brick and someone with a purpose, standing somewhere. The salve and the brick are the same case with the details changed. And the general rule is the one from the start: usefulness is a fit between a thing and a user.

That rule turns back on Hui Shi, and the turn is sharp. He has the giant gourd. He has the big thing, the Peng among vegetables. And he still has small understanding. He walked up to it with one use in his head, holding water, and when it failed that use he broke it. So the chapter has already shifted the question. Hui Shi's problem is not the size of the gourd. It is the size of the frame he brought to it.

He tries once more, near the end of the chapter, with a tree. He has a huge tree whose trunk is too knotted to take a carpenter's line and whose branches are too twisted to measure. Carpenters walk straight past it. Your words are like that tree, he tells Zhuangzi, big and useless, and everyone ignores them. Zhuangzi answers with a picture. Then plant it in the land of nothing at all, and wander beside it, and lie down under it. No axe will ever cut it short, and nothing will harm it, because it has no use.

That tree will grow into one of the book's central ideas, and it returns later with a much harder argument behind it. For now, the thing to see is what Zhuangzi did. He did not argue that the tree was secretly useful for timber. He found a standpoint from which the tree's shape was the good thing about it. A man lies in its shade, and no carpenter will ever cut it down.

The text itself hands over the obvious objection. The chapter says outright that small understanding does not reach great understanding. That is a ranking. The mushroom is worse off than the tree, the dove is worse off than Peng, and the silk family is worse off than the stranger. So Zhuangzi is not saying every view is as good as every other. He is saying some views are bigger and better, and most people are sitting on the dove's branch. On that reading the lesson is simple: climb higher, and you will see what is really true.

Some of that objection stands. The book does rank. It does think the family missed something, and it does think Hui Shi, with his broken gourd, missed something. The first chapter makes it very hard to say that Zhuangzi thought all views were equal. The question is what the ranking is a ranking of.

The bird gives the first clue. The book says, in the same breath as the ninety thousand li, that Peng can only fly on a thick pile of wind beneath it. Without that wind, the great wings have no strength. So the big bird is also dependent on its conditions, the way the dove is on its elm tree. What differs is the size of what each one leans on.

Then the chapter keeps climbing. It describes a man who could ride the wind itself, who went off on it and came back after fifteen days. That sounds like the top of the ladder. The text says no. Even he still had something he had to wait on. Above him the text sets someone who rides the regular course of heaven and earth and wanders without limit. On what, it asks, would such a person depend? Then it names the ones at the real top, in three short lines. The utmost person has no self. The spirit person has no achievement. The sage has no name.

The ladder changes direction halfway up. Dove, then Peng, then a man riding the wind: each is bigger than the last. But the top rung is not a bigger bird. It is a person with no self to fly from, no achievement to measure by, and no name to defend. A mystical reader takes "depending on nothing" literally, as standing outside every condition there is. The plainer reading is that this person is not taken in by their own footing. The dove depends on its elm and thinks the elm is the world. The person at the top still stands somewhere, and knows it.

A later chapter tells what it is like to make that climb, and it gives great understanding a fuller picture. In autumn the rains come, and every stream pours into the Yellow River. The river swells until you cannot tell an ox from a horse on the far bank. The spirit of the river is thrilled. He thinks all the beauty in the world has ended up in him. He flows east to the sea, looks out, and cannot see where the water ends. He turns to the spirit of the sea and sighs. There is a saying, he says, about a man who has heard a little and thinks nobody is his equal. That man is me.

The sea's answer names three kinds of limit. You cannot talk about the ocean with a frog at the bottom of a well, because it is shut in by its space. You cannot talk about ice with an insect that lives one summer, because it is stuck in its season. And you cannot talk about the Way with a narrow scholar, because he is tied up by what he was taught. Now that you have come out of your banks and seen the sea, it says, you know how small you are. So now we can talk.

Place, time and training: those are the three walls of any standpoint, and every listener has all three. Where you grew up, when you were born, and what you were taught all decide what looks obvious. And the conversation only starts once the river knows it is a river. The sea does not tell him to become the sea. He stays a river, and he can talk now, because he knows what he is.

So the ranking is real, and it runs on one axis only. Great understanding is not a higher place to stand. It is knowing, wherever you stand, that you are standing there. A view like that sees that the use it finds depends on where it stands. So it can find the float in the gourd and the shade under the tree. A small view takes its own use for the thing's nature, and breaks whatever fails that use.

That also answers the question of which side you took at the start. If you sided with Peng because big is better, you were doing what the dove did, only from a higher branch. You measured by your own flight and laughed at the smaller one.

It also settles part of a sensible belief, that a person is worth roughly what they are good for. Things do get used, people do have skills, and a salve that cures cracked hands is a real good. All of that stays. What goes is the idea that the use lives inside the thing, or inside the person, as a fixed fact. It lives in the meeting between a thing and a purpose. Whoever told you what you are good for was telling you where they stand.

Size, sense and usefulness, then, are all measured from somewhere. The dove measures by its elm, the silk family by its grandparents' trade, and Hui Shi by the one use he walked in with. Seeing further means seeing the measuring, and not finding a measure that belongs to nobody. A careful person will object at once that this is about tools. Usefulness may depend on who is using a thing, but surely right and wrong do not. When two people disagree about what is right, one of them, surely, is simply correct.

Part 2: This and That

Right and wrong work the way "here" and "there" work. Every "this" that someone affirms makes a "that" on the other side of it. And no position anyone holds is a "this" from nowhere. That is the book's central argument, and it is easiest to feel in a quarrel between two groups of very intelligent people.

In Zhuangzi's time, two schools were fighting over how to live. The followers of Confucius held that a good life is built from ritual and family duty. Proper mourning for a parent, with its long rites, was at the heart of that, and so was music. The followers of Mozi were against music, and wanted the dead buried in a plain thin coffin with no long mourning. Mozi himself listed heavy burials, long mourning, singing and dancing among the Confucian habits that would ruin the world. Both schools were serious. Both had arguments, teachers, and followers willing to suffer for them. And each called right exactly what the other called wrong.

The same quarrel runs through ordinary houses. One partner thinks a big wedding honours both families and that saving the money would be mean. The other thinks spending a year's savings on one day is foolish. Each can explain why, and each explanation is good. Anyone who has been in that argument knows that the better speaker does not end it. It ends when someone gives up, and the one who gives up usually still thinks they were right.

Before the book's answer, two simpler cases. The first is the word "here". I stand by the window and say, the chair is over here. You stand by the door and say, the chair is over there. We are both right, and we are pointing at the same chair. Nobody would call a judge to settle whether the chair is really here or really there, because the word only works from somewhere.

The second is the word "this". I hold up a cup and say, this one. You, across the table, call the same cup "that one". Again, both right, and again, the word carries the speaker's position inside it. The chair and the cup show the same structure twice: some words point from a place, and they cannot be used without one.

Zhuangzi's move starts from a fact about his own language. The ordinary word for "this" was also a word for "right". To say "this is it" and to say "this is right" were close to the same act. Whether or not he meant it as a pun, he built on it. Everything is a "that" to someone, he says, and a "this" to someone else. From the other side, you cannot see what I see. I know it only from where I am. So "that" comes out of "this", and "this" leans on "that", and the two are born together.

Put without the chair and the cup, the claim is this. Whenever someone affirms something as right, they affirm it from a place. And in that same act, the opposite position appears, as the place they are not standing. On this view, the two schools are two people standing in two places, each saying "here", and each hearing the other say "there".

The comparison with "here" has an edge. What carries over is the structure: a word that points from a place, used by two people in two places. What does not carry over is the heat. Nobody loses sleep over whether the chair is here or there, because either person can walk to the other's spot and look. Moral words do not feel like that. "Wrong" feels as if it were written on the act itself, the way weight is in a stone. The shared word for "this" and "right" is suggestive, and it proves nothing on its own. The chair shows only that a word can be perfectly correct and still be said from somewhere. Whether moral words are like that still has to be shown.

The book's picture for what to do about all this needs one word first. The Way is the book's word for how things go, the whole run of the world as it happens. In the picture, every position in a quarrel is a point on a ring, each one facing across the ring at its opposite. At the centre of the ring sits a pivot, the kind of post a door turns on. The pivot is not on the ring at all, so it can turn to face any point. The book calls it the hinge of the Way: the place where "this" and "that" stop being set against each other.

The text is careful to say that the hinge does not abolish right and wrong. Its next line says the pivot can answer "right" without end, and answer "wrong" without end. The hinge does not stop answering. It answers both, from the centre, instead of being stuck at one point on the ring, facing one way.

What that looks like in practice, the book shows with a comic story. A man keeps monkeys, and he feeds them nuts. One day he tells them, three in the morning and four in the evening. The monkeys are furious. All right, he says, four in the morning and three in the evening. The monkeys are delighted.

Nothing real changed. Seven nuts a day before, seven nuts a day after. But the monkeys' rage and joy were real, and the keeper, the text says, went along with the way they saw it. Then comes the lesson. The wise person settles right and wrong in balance, and lets both sides go forward. A common way of translating it is that he walks two roads at once.

The book introduces the story as a criticism of people who argue. The monkeys stand for those who wear themselves out forcing everything into one answer. They never notice that the answers they fight over come to the same thing. The keeper is the one at the hinge. He is not fooled by the morning and evening argument, and he does not sneer at it either. He gives them the order that makes them happy, and he still feeds them. He knows exactly how many nuts there are, and what he gives up is only the need to have his first offer win.

A later chapter states the idea behind the keeper as plainly as the book ever does. Look from inside any one thing, it says, and it rates itself high and everything else low. Look through custom, and your rank is not even yours, because other people hand it to you. And look from the Way, and nothing is higher or lower than anything else.

The first two views are the monkeys' and the office's. Every creature ranks from inside itself, and most working lives are ranked by labels other people pin on them, which can be taken off again. The third view is offered as the one to have, and here the book leans hardest toward a view from nowhere. The mystical reading is right to lean on it. What keeps it from becoming a height above everything is the sea that teaches it. Set beside heaven and earth, the sea says of itself, it is like a pebble on a great mountain. Even the teacher of the view from the Way speaks from somewhere, and knows it.

The book has a musician to show what taking a side costs. Once "right" and "wrong" stood out sharply, it says, the Way was damaged, and likes and dislikes grew in its place. When a great musician plucks his zither, he makes one tune and loses every other tune he could have played. When he does not play, nothing is made and nothing is lost. The text sets not playing as the undamaged state, and then asks whether there is really any making or losing at all. It does not tell the musician to stop. What it takes away is the idea that a tune can be chosen for free. Every "this" you affirm is a completion and a loss at once, and the two schools had forgotten the loss.

There is a fair complaint about the keeper. The keeper does see more than the monkeys. He knows a fact that holds on every side, that the total is seven. And the book never says how you can tell that your own quarrel is only morning against evening. A year's savings spent on one wedding may not be the same seven nuts. What the story teaches is a way of standing in a quarrel, not a test for which quarrels are empty.

The whole book can be read in more than one way. The book is often taken to say that nobody can know anything, or to point past every view to one great hidden reality. The reading taken throughout is plainer. Every judgment is made from a standpoint, and a person can move between standpoints, as the keeper does while still feeding the monkeys. Two things in the text pull the other way, and both are real. Each time the book settles a quarrel, it reaches for the word "heaven": the wise person, it says, rests in heaven's balance. A mystical reader hears in that a height above both sides. And in one famous passage the book calls the Way a real thing, older than heaven and earth, which gave birth to heaven and earth. Those passages are there. But the stories that do the book's practical work, the keeper, the cook and the mirror, all make sense without them.

The objection comes fast, and it is a strong one. If right is only right from somewhere, then anything goes. The cruel man is right from where he stands, and nobody can say otherwise. That fear sits behind every attack on relativism, and it deserves a straight answer.

The answer starts with "here". "Here" depends on who is speaking. But it is not arbitrary. Once you know where I am standing, it has a perfectly definite answer, and I can be wrong about it. If I stand by the window and say the door is here, I am mistaken. Right-from-somewhere works the same way. Inside a standpoint there are better and worse answers, and people get them wrong all the time. What goes is one claim only: that my "here" is everyone's "here". Whether that leaves enough to condemn a cruel man is a harder question, and the book's answer to it comes much later, with seven holes.

This also sorts the sensible belief that arguments find the right answer, because the better argument wins. A great deal of it survives. Inside a shared standpoint, arguments work. The keeper and the monkeys can agree that there are seven nuts, and if one of them miscounts, a count settles it. What fails is the hope that argument can decide which standpoint to stand in. Morning or evening was never a question that counting could answer.

Laid out as steps, the argument runs like this. One: words like "here" and "this" point from a place, and cannot be used without one. Two: in the book's language, "this" and "right" shared a word, which suggests that "right" points from a place too. Three: every affirmation from one place makes its opposite visible from the other place. Four: so in a quarrel between two standpoints, neither side can simply show the other a mistake. Five: the useful response is the keeper's, who sees both, still counts the nuts, and gives up only the need to win.

Right and wrong, then, are said from somewhere, like here and there, and seeing this does not dissolve them. It moves you to the hinge, where you can answer both. But the ordinary way out has not been tried yet. Two people who disagree could argue it out properly, with evidence, before someone fair. Whether any such judge exists is the question the book takes up next.

Part 3: Who Will Judge?

No argument can settle a dispute between two standpoints, because every possible judge is already standing somewhere. That sounds like the end of knowledge. It is not, and the book is careful to show why. What goes is one kind of knowing, the kind that claims to see from nowhere. Everything else survives, and it survives from where it stands.

The ordinary way out of a quarrel is a fair judge. Courts use one, sports use one, and families use one when a grandparent is asked to decide. Most people, asked how disagreements should end, would say exactly that: a neutral judge, the best evidence, and the better argument wins.

The book gives the argument to one of its teachers, answering a questioner, and it runs like this. Suppose you and I argue, and you beat me. Does that make you right and me wrong? Suppose I beat you. Does that make me right? Or is one of us partly right, or are we both right, or both wrong. We cannot settle it between ourselves, so we look for someone to judge.

Then the teacher goes through every possible judge. If the judge agrees with you, he already stands where you stand, so how can he judge fairly. If he agrees with me, the same. If he disagrees with both of us, he stands in a third place, and his verdict is just a third opinion. If he agrees with both of us, he has not decided anything. So you, I and the judge cannot settle it, the teacher ends, and the text asks whether we must wait for yet another person. Every candidate turns out to be standing somewhere, and the next one will be standing somewhere too.

Set against that, the ordinary answer turns out to be half right. The fair judge works, and works well, in one situation: when both sides already accept the same rules. A football referee can settle whether the ball crossed the line, because both teams agree what a goal is. That is the seven nuts again, a question inside a shared standpoint. But sometimes the dispute is about the rules themselves, about what counts as a goal, or a good life, or a proper funeral. Then the referee is just another player. The guess was right about most arguments and wrong about the ones that keep you up at night.

The argument is also narrower than it first sounds. A judge who agrees with you because the evidence points your way is not biased, and the teacher does not deny that evidence exists. What his list of judges shows is this. When the dispute is over what counts as evidence, neither side can win without assuming its own answer, and neither can any judge.

Nor does the teacher end on a shrug. The voices in an argument wait on each other for a judge, he says, and that waiting is as good as not waiting. So let heaven's own edges sort them out, let them run on, forget duty, and make your home in the boundless. The words are compressed, and they can be read more radically than this. One reading, the one followed here, is simple. Stop needing the verdict. Let the disagreement sit inside the larger run of things, instead of forcing it to end in your favour. The question of who wins stops running your nights.

There is a measured reason the fair judge is so hard to find, and it is that everyone believes they are one. Researchers handed people a list of well-known ways the mind bends a judgment, such as taking credit for success and blaming failure on bad luck. They asked how much each person did these things, and how much the average American did. On nearly every item, people put themselves below the average. In later studies, people read an explanation of a bias right after they had shown it. Knowing about it did not make them suspect themselves any more. The finding has one limit. People rated themselves less biased than others, not free of bias altogether. But that is enough for the book's point. Each person in a quarrel experiences their own standpoint as the plain view, and the other person's as a mere standpoint. So when you look around for a neutral judge, the candidate who feels most neutral is almost always you.

The book drew the same picture from the inside, and it reads like a description of the night the argument replays. In sleep our spirits tangle, it says. Awake, everything we touch turns into a knot, and every day the mind goes to war. It shoots out words like a crossbow bolt, grabbing hold of "right" and "wrong". Then it digs in as if it had sworn an oath, guarding the win.

Two moves are in that portrait, and both are familiar. The first is the attack: the sentence fired off before the other person has finished, aimed at their "wrong". The second is the defence: holding a position as if an oath had been sworn on it, long after the reasons have stopped mattering. Neither move needs a judge, because each already assumes the verdict. And the argument does not stop at night. The spirits, in the book's words, go on tangling in sleep.

The book then tests the idea on cases, and they come in another teacher's mouth, in one of its funniest speeches. A student asks him whether he knows what all things agree is so. How would I know, he says. Do you know what you do not know? How would I know? Then does nobody know anything. How would I know? And then, instead of falling silent, he says: still, let me try to say something.

What he says is a list. If a person sleeps in the damp, his back aches and he ends up half paralysed, but an eel is fine. If a person lives up a tree, he shakes with fear, but a monkey is fine. So which of the three knows the right place to live. People eat grain-fed meat, deer eat grass, centipedes love snakes, and owls and crows love mice. Which of them knows the right taste?

Then the sharpest case. There were two women whom everyone in the kingdom called beautiful. When fish saw them, the teacher says, they dived deep. When birds saw them, they flew high. When deer saw them, they ran. So which of these knows what beauty really is? The same faces that stop people in the street send every other creature running.

The list is not about animals, and the teacher says so at once. He finishes: as I see it, the starting points of kindness and duty, and the paths of right and wrong, are hopelessly tangled. The eel, the monkey and the deer were a way of reaching ethics without anyone getting defensive. Nobody feels insulted when told a deer does not like the same face they do. But the structure is the same one as the two schools. Each creature is right from where it lives, and there is nowhere to stand that is not some creature's place.

This whole family of ideas has one very old enemy. "Every view is from a standpoint" is itself a view. Either it is true from nowhere, in which case at least one view is from nowhere and the claim is false. Or it is only true from somewhere, in which case nobody else has to accept it. Either way, it seems to saw off the branch it is sitting on.

The book saw this coming, and its answer is in the way the teacher speaks. He does not announce, as a fact about everything, that nobody knows anything. Asked three times, he answers three times, how would I know. When he does go on, he goes on tentatively: let me try to say something. He even turns the doubt on himself. How do I know, he asks, that what I call knowing is really ignorance, or that what I call ignorance is really knowing. And he ends with the words "as I see it", marking his own place.

So the claim is offered from somewhere too, and it does not need to be anything else. It is not a report from a height above all views. It works more like a challenge. Produce the judge who stands nowhere, and the argument falls. Anyone at all may try, and each attempt so far has been a person standing somewhere. The objection needed the claim to be a view from nowhere, and in the argument about judges it never was.

The book's best picture of this comes from its later chapters, and it is a story about the man himself. Zhuangzi and Hui Shi, his arguing friend, are strolling on a bridge over the river Hao. Look at the little fish darting about at their ease, Zhuangzi says. That is fish being happy. You are not a fish, says Hui Shi. How do you know they are happy? You are not me, says Zhuangzi. How do you know I do not know? Hui Shi presses. I am not you, so I do not know you, and you are not a fish, so you do not know the fish.

Then Zhuangzi goes back to the start. You asked me how I know. The word Hui Shi used can mean "how" and can also mean "from where", and Zhuangzi takes the second meaning. You asked from where I know, he says, so you already granted that I knew. I know it from here, above the river.

Many readers take the ending as a pun that wins by a trick, and in a way it is. A defender of Hui Shi has a fair answer. Hui Shi was only applying a rule, to himself as much as to Zhuangzi: nobody can know what goes on inside another creature. So the ending does not catch Hui Shi out. It refuses his rule. The rule demands that knowledge come from inside the fish, or not at all. Zhuangzi answers with the place he actually has: here, on this bridge, watching them move.

Knowledge from somewhere, then, is still knowledge. Zhuangzi on the bridge knows something about the fish, the way you know your friend is upset from across a room. He could be wrong, and a closer look could correct him. The one thing lost is the claim to know from nowhere, with no bridge under your feet.

In the argument you replayed last night, the judge you would bring in is almost always someone you already expect to agree with you.

Every judge stands somewhere, the book's own argument included, and knowledge survives by knowing where it stands. The eel, the monkey and the man on the bridge all know something from their own place. One standpoint has not been examined, and it is the closest one. Every judgment so far was made by someone, a self, looking out from its place. The book's next move is to show that this self does not hold still long enough to be a place at all.

Part 4: The Butterfly

The self that does all the judging is not a fixed point. It is a run of moods and roles that rise in you the way sound rises from a hollow when the wind blows. You can see it working, but you cannot find it as a thing sitting behind the moods. The book's name for the fact that you keep becoming other things is the transformation of things. That is the claim, and the scene that opens the argument is a man who has just lost himself.

A teacher sits leaning on his armrest. He looks up at the sky, breathes out slowly, and seems to have gone somewhere. His student, standing by, is uneasy. You do not look like the man who was sitting here before, he says. The teacher answers: good question, because just now I lost myself. The grammar of that line matters. There is an "I" who does the losing and a "me" that gets lost. Somebody is still there to report it. What has gone is the self he usually carries, the one that has opinions and holds them.

To explain, the teacher talks about wind. The great earth breathes out, he says, and we call it wind. When it blows, every hollow sounds. The knotholes in the trees, the cracks in the rocks, the cave mouths, each makes a different noise: a whistle, a roar, a sob. One wind, and ten thousand notes. And each hollow sounds the way its own shape makes it sound. He ends with a question: who is it, then, that stirs them up. The text has already answered it. Each hollow sounds by its own shape, so there is no separate player standing behind the sounds.

Now the same picture, turned inward. Joy and anger, grief and delight, worry and regret, restlessness and stubbornness. They rise in us, the book says, like music from an empty pipe, or mushrooms out of damp ground. Day and night they take turns in front of us, and nobody knows where they sprout from.

Those two cases, the hollows and the moods, are the same case. In both, something passes through a shape and comes out as a sound. The wind is the world as it reaches you: a remark, a bill, a bad night's sleep. The shape is you, with your history and your habits. And the note is the mood, which feels, from inside, like the truth about the situation.

That changes the argument from the opening. At two in the morning you are certain you were right and the other person was unfair. At breakfast, after sleep and coffee, it seems smaller, and you are not sure. Which of those two is the real judge. The usual guess is that there is a real self underneath, and it is the calm one, the breakfast one. The book's answer is harder. Both are notes. The breakfast self is a different wind passing through the same shape. It may be a better-rested note, but it is still a note, not a view from nowhere.

The book does not leave that as a picture. It goes looking for the one in charge. It seems there is a true ruler, the text says, and you can see it working, but you never see its shape. So it searches the body. A hundred bones, nine openings and organs inside, all present and working. Which of them do I love most, it asks. Are they all servants. Do they take turns being master.

This is where the book pulls hardest the other way. It is easy to take the true ruler as a hidden true self, a soul behind the moods. The text will not say it is absent. Whether you find it or not, it says, adds nothing to its reality and takes nothing away. What the text never does is find it. The working is real, and the thing behind the working, as something you could point to, is never found.

Near the end of the chapter, a small joke makes the same point from the outside. The faint edge of a shadow complains to the shadow. A moment ago you were walking, and now you have stopped, it says. You were sitting, and now you are up. Why can you not make up your mind? The shadow answers by wondering aloud. Perhaps I move because of something else, it says. And perhaps that something moves because of something else again. I may be like a snake's scales, or a cicada's wings, which move only because the body moves. How would I know why I do what I do?

The shadow is every one of us, asked to explain ourselves. We give an answer, and it sounds like a pilot speaking, a little someone inside who steers. But follow the chain back through the body, the mood and the wind. The shadow can name no link where a pilot sits. The shadow is honest enough to say it does not know.

An experiment in a shop caught people doing what the shadow refused to do. Researchers set up a table with four pairs of stockings and asked passers-by which pair was the best quality. The four pairs were identical. People picked the pair on the far right almost four times as often as the pair on the far left. Asked why, nobody mentioned where the pair was lying. Asked outright whether position could have mattered, almost everyone said no.

The shoppers had a reason for their choice, and they reported a different one. They were probably not lying. They did what anyone does when asked to explain a choice: they produced a plausible pilot after the fact. The limits are real. It was a shop and one small choice, and the researchers themselves allow that some shoppers may have denied the position effect to avoid looking silly. What survives is narrow and still unsettling. The reason given for a judgment and the thing that actually moved it can come apart, and from the inside they feel exactly the same.

Then comes the most famous story in the book. Once Zhuangzi dreamed he was a butterfly. He fluttered about, happy with himself, doing as he pleased, and had no idea he was Zhuangzi. Suddenly he woke, and there he was, solidly Zhuangzi. He did not know whether he was Zhuangzi who had dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming it was Zhuangzi.

That is usually where the story is stopped, and it is then told as a puzzle. Maybe you are dreaming right now. Maybe none of this is real. But the story has one more sentence, and it points the other way. Between Zhuangzi and a butterfly, it says, there must be some difference. And this is called the transformation of things.

So the story does not end in doubt. It ends by insisting the two are different. What it does with the difference is stranger. While he was the butterfly, he was wholly a butterfly, happy with itself, with no idea of Zhuangzi. Then he was wholly Zhuangzi. The difference between them is real, and what is not fixed is which one you are. The transformation of things means being fully each thing in turn, and not being a hidden someone who merely visits them.

The same holds in an ordinary day. A woman is a manager at nine, a daughter on the phone at lunch, a mother at six, and asleep at midnight. At each point she is wholly that. The manager does not know, in the moment, what the mother feels. Nobody would say the manager is a dream, and nobody would say she is the real one and the mother is a costume. The roles differ, and none of them is the one underneath.

A second passage takes the dream one step further, and it comes from the same teacher who went through every possible judge. When we dream, he says, we do not know we are dreaming. Inside the dream we may even sit down and interpret the dream we are having. Only on waking do we know it was a dream. And there may be a great waking, after which we would know that this whole life was one great dream.

Then he turns it on himself, which is the point. You and your teacher are both dreaming, he says, and when I say you are dreaming, I am dreaming too. The text calls this a strange saying. It is the argument about judges again, now aimed at the self. The one who would judge whether you are awake is inside the same night. No one now speaking stands outside the dream, the same way no judge stood outside the standpoints.

The obvious worry is about blame. If there is no fixed self, who is responsible for anything. Who made the promise, who owes the debt, who did the harm. A self that is only a run of moods seems like a perfect excuse. It was not me, it was the anger passing through.

The answer starts from what the text never says. It never says there is nobody. The teacher who lost himself is still sitting there, and he is the one talking. The ruler's working is plain, even if its shape is never found. What the book never finds is a pilot, a little someone behind the moods who watches them. It does not deny the person, who is the whole pattern of notes, over years, from this particular shape. A song is a fair comparison, with one limit. No single note is the song, and there is no song hidden behind the notes, yet you can say what the song is like. The limit is that a person, unlike a song, seems to choose some of its next notes, and the book never says who does the choosing. Holding the pattern responsible is an answer built on the book, not one found in it. It is also how people ordinarily judge each other: by what someone keeps doing.

What survives the worry is modest. There is someone, and that someone is the moving, not a watcher of it. The anger that passed through is yours because it passed through your shape, and that is exactly why the shape can be trained.

In order, the steps are these. One: moods rise in you the way notes rise from hollows when the wind blows, from the meeting of the world and your shape. Two: looking for the one in charge, you find the working but never the shape of a ruler. Even the reasons you give for a choice can be made up after it. Three: so the self is not a fixed judge standing behind the moods, but the run of them. Four: you become other things in turn, fully each one, and that is the transformation of things. Five: so every judgment in the earlier arguments was made by a changing self. The calm self is still a standpoint, not a view from nowhere.

The place every judgment comes from, then, is itself moving. The night self and the breakfast self are two notes of one instrument, and the butterfly was fully a butterfly. That seems to leave nothing to stand on. The question is what acting well looks like, for a self like that, in a world like this. The book answers with a man holding a knife.

Part 5: The Cook's Knife

If there is no fixed judge inside and no fixed standard outside, the book's replacement for finding the right answer is a skill. It is the skill of responding: acting through the openings a situation already has. And the best practitioners get there by attending to the thing in front of them, not to their plan for it. The book puts this in the hands of a cook, and his story is told almost as a demonstration.

A cook named Ding is cutting up an ox for his lord. The lord watches, amazed at how easily it goes, and asks how skill can reach such a level. The cook puts down his knife. What I care about, he says, is the Way, and it goes further than skill. When I first began cutting up oxen, all I could see was the whole ox in front of me. After three years, I no longer saw the whole ox.

Then he explains what he does see. I go by the natural lines of the animal, he says. I slip the blade into the big gaps, guide it through the hollows, and follow the way the ox is already built. The joints have spaces in them, and the edge of the blade has almost no thickness. Put something with no thickness into a space, and there is all the room you could want.

And then the knife. A good cook needs a new knife every year, because he cuts. An ordinary cook needs one every month, because he hacks through bone. This knife of mine has lasted nineteen years. It has taken apart thousands of oxen, and its edge is as sharp as if it had just come off the grindstone.

There are three cooks in that speech, and the difference between them is the whole lesson. The ordinary cook hacks. He meets the ox as a solid block and forces his way through it, and the bone wins, every month. The good cook cuts. He has technique, he knows where to aim, and he does well, but his blade still meets resistance and wears out in a year. Ding does neither. His blade goes where the ox already has room.

Before the book's explanation, the ordinary guess about what separates the good cook from the great one is worth stating. The common answer is more rules, learned better, applied more carefully. The great cook knows more about anatomy, keeps a sharper checklist, and makes fewer mistakes in following it. Ding says something else. He says the change came when he stopped seeing a whole ox. What changed was not a larger rulebook but where his attention went: onto the lines of this particular animal, as it is built.

The phrase "natural lines" needs one guard. It means the ox's own grain, the places where its body already comes apart. It does not mean a plan written into the universe. The book's phrase for Ding's method is going by how the thing already is. The opening is in the ox. The cook's work is to find it and not to make one.

Then comes the detail that most retellings drop, and it changes the lesson. Whenever I come to a tangled spot, Ding says, I see that it will be hard. I become careful, almost afraid. My eyes stay fixed on it. I slow right down, and move the blade the smallest amount, and the piece falls away like a clod of earth. So the master does not stop paying attention. At the hardest place he pays the most attention of anyone in the story. What he attends to is the knot, not his own wrist.

The lord's reply is the story's own moral. Wonderful, he says: I have listened to my cook and learned how to take care of a life. The book draws the lesson for keeping a life whole. Carrying it further, into how to act at work and at home, is a step beyond the text, taken in its direction. A knife suits the lesson, because a knife shows the cost of force as a notch in the blade. A forced life wears down the person living it in the same way, only less visibly.

The later chapters give more craftsmen, and each adds one piece. The first is a wheelwright. A duke is reading in his hall, and the old wheelwright working below puts down his tools and asks what he is reading. The words of the sages, says the duke. Are the sages still alive? No, they are dead. Then, says the wheelwright, what you are reading is the dregs the old men left behind.

The duke, angry, tells him to explain himself or die, and the wheelwright explains with his own trade. When I shape a wheel, if I go too slow the spoke slips, and too fast it jams. Not too slow and not too fast, I get it in my hands and my heart answers. I cannot put it into words. I cannot teach it to my own son, and he cannot learn it from me. That is why I am seventy and still making wheels. The story does not say that books are worthless or that the knack cannot be learned. It says the knack cannot be handed over as words. What the dead sages knew died in their hands, and what they left is the part that could be written down.

The second craftsman is a swimmer. At a waterfall so high and violent that no fish or turtle could live in it, someone sees a man in the rapids. The watchers think he wants to die and run down to pull him out. He climbs out further down, singing. Asked how he does it, he says: I have no method. I go down with the whirl and come up with the eddy. I follow the water's way and put nothing of my own against it. He also says he started with what he was born into and grew into it over a lifetime, until it was as sure as fate. The swimmer, too, had decades of practice behind his lack of method.

The same chapter has an old hunchback who catches cicadas with a long pole, as easily as if he were picking them up by hand. Asked whether he has a method, he describes his training, and it is measured. He spent a summer balancing balls on the tip of his pole. When two stayed on without falling, he rarely missed a cicada. When three stayed on, he missed one in ten. When five stayed on, catching them was like picking them up by hand. Now he holds his body like a tree stump and his arm like a dead branch. However big the world is, and however many things are in it, he knows only cicada wings. A will that is not split, the story concludes, gathers into something like spirit.

The hunchback adds two things the cook left out. The first is that the ease was built in steps, and he can tell you the steps. The second is where the attention finally goes. It narrows down to one thing, the wing of an insect, and everything else drops out of it. He holds himself as still as a stump and a branch, so that the cicada is what fills his attention.

Four craftsmen have now shown the same thing, and it needs a name. The plainest name is responsiveness. Responsiveness is acting through the openings a situation already has, found by attention to the situation itself. Its opposite is forcing: acting through a plan you brought with you, and pushing when the world does not fit it. The ordinary cook forces. Ding responds. The swimmer who fought the whirl would drown, and the one who goes down with it comes up singing.

Here the book's claim meets a finding from the laboratory. Researchers asked experienced golfers to putt in two conditions. In one, they listened to a stream of tones and called out a particular tone while putting, so their attention was elsewhere. In the other, they had to track their own stroke and say "stop" at the exact moment the club head stopped. Watching their own stroke, they left the ball about forty percent further from the target.

The same team tested footballers dribbling a ball around cones. Experienced players using their strong foot were slower when they had to track which part of the foot touched the ball. With their weaker foot, the result reversed, and tracking the foot helped. Beginners, fluent with neither foot, also tended to do better when they attended to it.

What maps onto the book is clear. A trained skill runs worse when attention is turned onto its mechanics, and runs as well as usual when attention is somewhere else. And where the skill is not yet trained, careful attention helps. Such attention is what the beginner needs, and it is what Ding does at the knot. What does not map is just as clear. The groups were small. And the study says nothing about oxen, ethics, or how to live. It supports one step of the argument, that attention to your own procedure disrupts a trained response, and no more.

The book also knows what drags the attention back onto the self, and it explains it with gambling. Bet with clay tiles, the book's Confucius says, and you play well. Bet your belt buckle, and you get nervous. Bet gold, and you lose your head. The skill is the same all three times. What changes is that you now care about something outside the game, and whoever gives weight to what is outside goes clumsy inside.

A laboratory study put a small version of the gold on the table. Researchers taught students with little or no golf experience to putt on an indoor green, about two hundred and seventy putts each, until they stopped improving. Then they raised the stakes. Now improving would win them a little money, and a partner's money depended on them too. Students who had simply practised got worse under that pressure. Students who had practised the whole time in front of a camera, told that coaches would study the tape, held steady or got a little better.

The researchers' reading matches the gambler's. Pressure makes people start watching and steering a movement that runs better on its own. The ones who had practised while being watched had already learned not to. The limits: these were trained beginners, the stake was a few dollars, and the camera group's gain was clear in only one of two experiments. What the study adds is a point about training. Practising under observation protected people against pressure, and caring less was never tested.

The first objection is that lives are not oxen. An ox has fixed joints, placed where every ox has them, and a cook can learn them. A quarrel, a job, a grieving friend, has no anatomy chart. So the cook's lesson seems to work only where the world is already carved at stable places.

The answer is that the claim was never that every situation has one fixed anatomy. It is that every situation already has some openings, and some bone. A teenager who will not talk has, somewhere, a thing he is actually worried about. A meeting that is stuck has one point where it is actually stuck. The forcing response is the lecture, the prepared speech, the plan pushed through regardless. The responsive one looks for the gap that is already there, and it does so this time, with this person, because the gap moves. That is harder than an ox, and the book never says it is easy. Ding himself meets knots.

The second objection is older and sharper, and it is a paradox. If the skill is to stop forcing, then trying to stop forcing is itself a kind of forcing. You cannot aim at spontaneity without destroying it, the way you cannot try to fall asleep. So the advice seems to defeat itself the moment anyone takes it.

The book's craftsmen answer it by what they did. Ding spent three years before he stopped seeing whole oxen. The hunchback spent a summer on balls balanced on a pole. The wheelwright is seventy. The swimmer grew into the water over a lifetime. None of them stopped trying. Each of them moved the effort off his own performance and onto the thing: the ox, the wheel, the current. The paradox only bites if the goal is to be spontaneous. Ding's goal was the Way, and he found it in the ox, and the ease came as a side effect of attending to it for years.

A third objection comes from the operating theatre, and it is the strongest evidence against the cook. Eight hospitals, from wealthy cities to a district hospital in rural Tanzania, brought in a one-page checklist. Before the anaesthetic, before the first cut, and before the patient leaves, the team says aloud the most basic things. Who is this patient, which side are we operating on, have the antibiotics gone in, is every sponge accounted for. After the checklist came in, deaths after surgery fell by nearly half, and complications by about a third. That looks like rules beating attention, in the one field where mistakes kill. It was a before-and-after comparison, not a controlled trial, and part of the drop may come from people knowing they were watched. Even so, the drop is large.

What was on the list decides it. Almost none of it touched the cutting itself. The checklist handled the steps a skilled team forgets under pressure: the wrong patient, the wrong side, the missing sponge. The cutting, and the moment when the tissue does something unexpected, stayed with the surgeon's attention. So the checklist and the cook divide the work. Rules carry the routine that attention drops, and attention carries the part no rule can reach. Ding's lesson was only ever about the second part.

So what survives the objections is a specific claim. The effort does not go away. It goes to the thing, and not to your plan for the thing.

The argument you replayed at night is, in these terms, a prepared speech. You were rehearsing your lines, your plan for the conversation, instead of the one thing the other person actually said. That is the ordinary cook's move, and it notches the blade.

For a changing self in a world without a fixed standard, acting well turns out to be a trained skill of response. Its training moves attention onto the thing. Ding's nineteen-year blade is the picture of it. One practical question remains. The craftsmen say how long their training took, and not what it took away. It has a second story, with a student and a dangerous prince, that says exactly what the training takes away.

Part 6: Fasting of the Heart

The training behind the cook's eye is subtraction. It does not leave you with no views. It stops the view you arrived with from deciding what you are able to see. The book shows this in a long conversation between a teacher and a student who wants to do good. The student's problem is that he is too sure of how.

Here the teacher is Confucius, the founder of the rival school. In this book he is a character, and he speaks the way Zhuangzi would. His student is Yan Hui, and Yan Hui comes to ask permission to leave. There is a young ruler in a neighbouring state, he says, who does whatever he likes. He treats his country as if it were nothing, cannot see his own mistakes, and his people are dying. Yan Hui wants to go and put him right. The teacher's first answer is blunt. You will probably go there and get yourself executed.

Yan Hui is not a fool, and he has plans. The teacher takes them apart one at a time, and Yan Hui keeps trying. Then I will be straight on the inside and bend on the outside, he says: keep my convictions to myself, and seem to go along. I will quote the ancients, so the criticism is not in my own words.

The teacher's reply is the key line of the passage. No, he says. Far too many policies, and you have not even looked at the man. You might stay out of trouble that way, but that is all. How would any of it change him? And then the diagnosis. You are still taking your own heart as your teacher.

In this book the heart is the part that thinks and plans, as well as the part that feels. So the charge is this. Every plan Yan Hui brought was made in his own head before he had seen the man. He is the ordinary cook, arriving with a speech, ready to hack. His goodness is real, and it is also a plan, and the plan will meet the bone.

Yan Hui asks what he should do instead, and the teacher tells him to fast. He means a fasting of the heart. Gather your attention into one, he says. Do not listen with your ears, listen with your heart. Then do not listen with your heart, listen with your breath. The ears stop at hearing. The heart stops at matching what comes in to what it already has. The breath is empty, and waits for whatever comes. That emptiness is what I call fasting of the heart.

The three kinds of listening are easy to tell apart in one small scene. A friend says, I'm fine. The ears hear the word "fine", and stop there. The heart hears it and matches it to a file: she always says that, she is annoyed with me, or she wants to be asked twice. Something real is being heard, but it is heard through what the listener already believed. The third kind of listening takes in the tired voice, the pause before the word, and waits, without filing it yet.

With that scene in hand, the name fits. Fasting of the heart is not emptying the mind of all content. The listener who fasts still has a history, still knows the friend, and still cares. What goes without food is the matching, the habit of turning each new thing into an example of something already decided. That habit is exactly where Yan Hui's plans came from.

Yan Hui goes away and comes back, and what he says is one of the strangest lines in the book. Before I had this, he says, I was so sure I was Hui. Now that I have it, there has never been a Hui at all. Is that what you mean by empty? That is it exactly, says the teacher. It is the same move as the teacher on his armrest, who had lost himself. The Hui who was sure, the Hui with the plans and the good intentions, was the thing standing between Yan Hui and the ruler. And the teacher immediately goes on to give him practical advice for dealing with the ruler. Emptying himself is how Yan Hui gets ready to act.

A second story in the same chapter makes the politics concrete. A man has been appointed tutor to another violent heir, and he asks a wise official what to do. The official's first advice is about method. Be careful, he says, and set yourself straight first. If the prince wants to act like a child, be a child with him. If he wants to throw out all the lines, throw them out with him. If he wants to go past every limit, go there with him. Move with him until you can lead him through, and do it without a mark on you.

He also names the danger in his own advice, and it is exactly the danger a critic would name. On the outside, nothing beats going along with him, he says, and on the inside, nothing beats staying at ease. But going along must not turn into being pulled in. If you get pulled in, you go down with him and are ruined. And your inner ease must not leak out for show, because to a man like that, a reputation for calm is a threat.

Anyone who has calmed a furious toddler will recognise the method. You do not start from where you want the person to be. You start from where they are, go there with them, and steer from inside. And the official is honest that the method has a failure built into it: you can go along so well that you stop steering. Then the rest of the advice comes as three animals.

The first is a mantis. A mantis will rear up and wave its arms at an oncoming chariot wheel, the official says. It has no idea the job is too big for it, because it is so proud of its own talent. Show off your fine qualities to a man like this, and you are that mantis. The target here is confidence in your own goodness, the thing that made Yan Hui's plans, and courage is left alone.

The second is a tiger keeper. He never gives the tiger a live animal, because the killing would stir up its rage. He never gives it a whole carcass, because the tearing would stir up its rage. He knows when it is hungry and when it is full, and works with its temper. A tiger is a different kind of creature from us, yet it is gentle with the man who feeds it, because he goes with it. The ones it kills are the ones who went against it.

The third is a horse lover. He loves his horse so much that he catches its dung in a basket and its urine in a fine shell. One day a fly lands on the horse, and he slaps it without warning. The horse bolts, breaks its bit, and smashes the gear on its head and chest. The official draws the lesson: his care went all the way, and his love was what got lost.

Side by side, the three show one contrast. The mantis acts from its own picture of itself. The horse lover acts from his own love, at a moment chosen by his own feeling, and the horse was never consulted. The keeper acts from the tiger: its hunger, its temper, the way it is already built. He is Ding with a tiger in place of an ox.

The book's last word on this is a picture. The utmost person uses the heart like a mirror. It does not chase things as they leave, and does not go out to meet them as they come. It answers whatever is in front of it and keeps nothing. That is why it can meet everything and not be hurt. A mirror is not blank, because it shows exactly what is there. What it lacks is a store: yesterday's face is not in it when today's face arrives.

The objection is obvious, and it is a serious one. An emptied person sounds like a pushover. Worse, the tiger keeper sounds like a courtier, the man who survives a tyrant by flattering him. Going with the tiger's temper is exactly what every collaborator says he was doing. If the fasting of the heart ends in a well-fed tiger, it has trained a servant, not a sage.

Part of the answer is in the keeper's own story. He still keeps the tiger. He decides what it eats and when, and he never hands it a live animal to kill. Going with its temper is how he stays in charge of a creature that could kill him. Responsive is not the same as compliant. The mantis complies with its own vanity, and the horse lover with his own impulse. The keeper complies with neither. From outside, the keeper and a courtier can look the same, and the difference is only what each is aiming at. The courtier aims at his own safety, and the keeper at the tiger's behaviour. The official's own warning marks the line: going along must never turn into being pulled in. And the teacher's complaint against Yan Hui's safe plan was that it would not change the ruler. The book wants the ruler changed. It thinks a man who has actually looked at him has the only chance of doing it.

The limit is real, though. This is a method for dealing with power from inside its reach, for surviving it and sometimes turning it. It is not a method for overthrowing it. Nobody in these stories raises an army or organises the people who are dying under that ruler.

That also corrects the belief that skill means applying the rules more carefully. Rules and principles are real, and Yan Hui's wish to help the dying is good. What has to go is letting the principle you walk in with decide what you see. Yan Hui's principle blinded him to the man, and the mantis's pride blinded it to the wheel.

The skill of response, then, is trained by subtraction. The Hui who was sure has to go before the man in front of him can be seen. What remains is a person who does not insist, does not display his goodness, and will not be the mantis. By the standards of most workplaces, and most families, that person looks like someone who has given up. The book thinks the world's measure of worth is exactly what is wrong here, and it makes the case with a tree.

Part 7: The Useless Tree

The things a system finds useful are very often the things it uses up. A person's worth is not the use other people assign them, because that use is measured from where those people stand. The book first made this point with Hui Shi's knotted tree and the lazy afternoon under it. It comes back to the tree with a much harder argument, and with a warning about where the argument stops working.

A master carpenter and his apprentice are travelling, and they pass a sacred oak by a village shrine. It is so big it could shade thousands of oxen, and a crowd stands around it, staring up. The carpenter does not even turn his head. The apprentice runs after him. I have never seen timber like that, he says, and you did not even look. Useless wood, says the carpenter. Make a boat from it and the boat sinks. Make a coffin and it rots. A door would ooze sap, and a pillar would fill with worms. That is why it has lived so long.

The carpenter is right. He knows wood, and as timber the oak is worthless. The story never says he is wrong about that. What it does next is let the tree reply.

That night the oak comes to the carpenter in a dream. What were you comparing me with, it asks, the fruit trees? The pear and the orange, the ones people call useful. When their fruit is ripe, they get stripped. Their big branches are broken and their small ones pulled about. Their gift is what makes their lives hard, and they die halfway through their years. I have been trying to be useless for a long time, the tree says. I nearly died more than once. Now I have finally managed it, and that is my great use.

It is easy to hear that as a teaching about laziness. Do nothing, be good for nothing, and you will be left alone. It is worth checking that guess against what the tree actually says. The tree does not say being useful is bad. The fruit trees do give fruit, and the fruit is good. What the tree says is that the fruit trees are used, by others, on others' schedule, until they break. Its great use is a use to itself: it survived, and it grew to shade thousands of oxen.

The same chapter gives a human case, and it is harsh. There was a man so twisted that his chin sat at his navel and his shoulders stood above his head. When the army officers came to take men for the wars, he strolled through the crowd swinging his arms, because nobody would take him. When the great labour gangs were rounded up, he was let off as chronically ill. And when grain was handed out to the sick, he got three measures of it and ten bundles of firewood.

The two cases differ on one axis, and they differ on it the same way. The straight tree and the straight young man are useful, so they are taken: cut for boards, marched to the front. The knotted oak and the twisted man are useless, so they are left, and both live out their years. In each case, the use belonged to someone else's plan, a carpenter's or a general's, and being useful meant being consumed by that plan.

The principle is the salve from the start, taken one step further. Usefulness is a fit between a thing and a user. When the user is a system, a trade or an army or a market, "useful" means useful to it. And a system spends what is useful to it. So the question "what am I good for" always has a hidden second half, which is: good for whom, and at what cost to me.

The pattern needs no ancient army to show up, and it has been measured. Researchers described two versions of the same student: one who resisted buying something he wanted, and one who gave in. People expected more from the one who resisted. Asked to share out ten essays to proofread among four volunteers, they handed the most to the disciplined one. In a workplace survey, disciplined employees said they felt weighed down by how much they did for colleagues, and the colleagues did not see it. In couples, the more self-controlled partner was leaned on more, and felt more worn out.

The limits: these are ratings and surveys, not whole careers, and nobody measured resentment, only the burden. But it is the pear tree in modern clothes. The trait that makes someone useful goes with carrying more, and feeling it, and the people handing out the load rarely notice.

The next chapter turns from bodies to people who drew followers without any of the usual gifts. One was a man whose foot had been cut off as a legal punishment, so he carried a criminal's mark. He taught nothing you could write down, and yet, the book says, as many people followed him as followed Confucius. Another was a man so ugly he frightened people. Yet men who spent time with him could not bring themselves to leave. More than a dozen women told their parents they would rather be his concubine than another man's wife.

The book's summary is short. Where what a person has inside stands out, people forget the body. The word for what a person has inside is often translated "virtue", but here it means something closer to a person's power or presence. And then the book turns the point around. Our trouble, it says, is that we forget the wrong thing. We remember the missing foot and forget the person.

That is the same mistake the carpenter made, aimed at a human being. The world's first measure of a person is often a trade's measure: can he march, can she lift, does this body fit the job. By that measure the one-footed man is a criminal and a cripple. By a different measure, the one his followers used, he is the person they could not stay away from.

A story about the man himself, from the later chapters, shows what the useful path looks like when it is offered to you. Zhuangzi is fishing when two officials arrive from the king. The king, they say, would like to trouble you with running his country. Zhuangzi keeps hold of his rod and does not look round. I hear, he says, that your king has a sacred turtle that died three thousand years ago. He keeps it wrapped in cloth, in a box, in his ancestral temple. Would that turtle rather be dead with its bones honoured, or alive, dragging its tail through the mud? Alive in the mud, they say. Then go away, says Zhuangzi. I will drag my tail in the mud.

The honour and the use are the same thing seen from two sides. The temple honours the turtle by keeping its shell, and the court would honour Zhuangzi by keeping his days. The offer is real, and so is the price, and the story's whole point is that the price is paid by the one being honoured.

The book also raises the obvious objection against itself, in a story from the later chapters about the man himself. Zhuangzi is walking in the mountains and passes a huge tree that the woodcutters leave standing. Its wood is no good for anything, they say. This tree gets to live out its years by being useless, Zhuangzi says. That night he stays with an old friend, who is delighted and tells a servant to kill a goose for dinner. The servant asks which one: the one that can honk, or the one that can't? Kill the one that can't, says the host.

The next morning a student asks the obvious question. Yesterday the tree lived because it was useless. Today the goose died because it was useless. So where would you stand, master? Zhuangzi laughs. I would stand somewhere between useful and useless, he says. Then he takes it back. The in-between only looks like the answer, and it does not get you clear of trouble either.

That is the simple version of the useless tree, broken. If uselessness were a policy, the goose would have followed it and died. Being useless is just another fixed position, and a fixed position can always be caught by the next situation.

So Zhuangzi gives a different answer, and it is the one that matters. Someone who drifts along with the Way is different, he says. No praise and no blame. Now a dragon, now a snake, changing with the times and never willing to be just one thing. Now up, now down, with harmony as the only measure. Such a person treats things as things, and is not made into a thing by things.

That last line is the ruling. The lesson of the tree was never "be useless". It was: do not let one standpoint's measure become your whole measure. The carpenter's measure is real, and so is the general's, and so is the host's at dinner. The mistake is to live entirely inside one of them, so that it can spend you. Sometimes the dragon is useful and sometimes the snake lies low. The one who can be either is the one no single measure can use up.

The strongest form of the objection still stands, and it is a moral one. This sounds like a recipe for dropping out. The twisted man takes grain at the handout, grain that other people grew. If everyone were the oak, nobody would build boats, bury the dead or bring in the harvest. A philosophy that teaches you to avoid being used is teaching you to let others carry the load.

The answer has three parts, and the first is in the story itself. The same passage says the twisted man earns his living sewing and winnowing grain, and earns enough to feed ten people. He is spared the army and the labour gangs, not spared work. Second, the book is written from the side of the one being used. Its examples say who that is: men taken for the army, men rounded up for labour, trees cut in their prime. Its advice is for someone in danger of being spent by a system that does not care about them. Third, the dragon also rises. The one who changes with the times is useful when the time calls for it, and simply refuses to become only a tool. One concession stands. The book never asks what would happen if everyone refused to be used. It is a defence of the used, not a theory of what everyone owes.

So people do have uses, and giving fruit is good, and none of that is taken back. What is taken back is the idea that the use someone else measures is what you are worth. The feeling of uselessness beside people who are going somewhere is the silk family's measure applied to a life. "Somewhere" is a direction on someone's map, and it is worth asking whose.

Worth, like usefulness, is measured from somewhere, and the dangerous measures are the ones that spend you. The oak survived by refusing the carpenter's measure, and the goose shows that refusing it is not enough. One measure is bigger than all of these, and it seems to belong to nobody in particular. Living is good and dying is bad. Whether even that is a standpoint is the book's hardest question.

Part 8: Drumming on a Bowl

The fear of death is the last case of "this" against "that". It is a preference for the one standpoint you now occupy, being alive, over one you have never stood in. In the story it is best known for, the book's answer to that fear is not indifference. It is a second look, taken after the first grief and added to it. Its early chapters hold a harder ideal, and that has to be faced too. The book knows how cold that can sound. So it tests the claim on the hardest case it has, a man whose wife has just died.

It starts gently, with a story told by the same old teacher who said we interpret our dreams while still inside them. There was a border guard's daughter who was taken off to be a king's woman. When they came for her, she cried until the front of her dress was soaked. Then she reached the palace, shared the king's bed, ate the finest meat, and was sorry she had ever cried.

Then the teacher asks three questions, and they are questions, not claims. The first is how he knows that loving life is not a mistake. The second is how he knows that fearing death is not like a child who wandered off and forgot the way home. And the third is how he knows that the dead are not sorry they ever clung to life.

Nothing in those lines says death is better. The story is an analogy, and a rough one: the girl was taken by force, and nobody should hear it as praise of that. Its point is narrower. She judged the palace from the road, from inside the life she was losing. Her tears were real, and her judgment about what lay ahead was made from the only place she had ever stood.

A modern study caught the same mistake in a much smaller form. Researchers asked young professors, still waiting to hear if they would get a job for life, how happy they would be after a refusal. They also asked former colleagues who had already been refused. In the first few years afterwards, the people it had actually happened to were clearly happier than the waiting professors had predicted. The same pattern showed up with students predicting how a breakup would feel. The limits are strict. The study was small, the loss was a job and not a person, and it measured a few years, not a lifetime. It says nothing about death. What it shows is the girl's error in miniature. People judging a place they have not reached, from the place they are losing, expect it to be worse than the people living there report.

That is the structure of the whole argument about "this" and "that", applied to the largest pair of all. Life is "this", because it is where you are standing. Death is "that", seen from here. The teacher does not claim to know the view from the other side. He only points out that no one judging it has stood there, and the girl's tears show how badly that kind of judgment can go.

The book then shows what living by this looks like, with four friends. They agree that before birth, life, and death belong to one body, and that anyone who sees this is their friend. Then one of them falls ill. His back hunches, his chin sinks to his navel, and his shoulders rise above his head. The chin at the navel and the shoulders above the head are word for word the book's description of the twisted man who escaped the army. He hobbles to the well and looks at his reflection. How great the maker of things is, he says, to fold me up like this.

The phrase "the maker of things" means whatever it is that makes things, and the book builds no temple to it. The line itself can be heard with a dry smile. A friend asks him if he resents it. Why would I, he says. If this goes on and turns my left arm into a rooster, I will use it to crow the hours before dawn.

Later another of the friends lies dying, gasping, with his wife and children crying around him. He talks about a smith. Suppose a smith is casting metal, and the metal jumps up in the pot and shouts, make me into a famous sword. The smith would think that metal was cursed. I have happened into a human shape this once, he says. If I went around shouting "only a human, only a human", whatever shapes things would think me cursed as well.

Those two friends make one case from two sides. The first is changing into something worse, a crooked body, and he treats the change as the next thing he will be. The second is changing out of being human altogether, and he treats his insistence on staying human as the metal's vanity. This is the transformation of things again, the butterfly's lesson, now pointed at the body. You are wholly this shape for now, and it was never promised to be the last one.

Then comes the story the book is best known for, and it comes from the later chapters, a story about the man himself. Zhuangzi's wife died. Hui Shi, his old friend and rival, came to pay his respects. He found Zhuangzi sitting on the ground with his legs sprawled out, beating on a basin and singing. She lived with you, Hui Shi said. She raised your children and grew old with you. Not weeping would be bad enough. Drumming and singing is too much.

Most people hearing that side with Hui Shi at once, and they are right to feel the shock. A man drumming at his wife's death looks like a man who did not love her. Or he looks like a man who has turned a philosophy into an excuse for coldness.

No, said Zhuangzi. When she first died, how could I not have felt it? But then I looked back to her beginning. Once she had no life. Not only no life, but no shape. Not only no shape, but not even breath. In the blur of things something changed, and there was breath. The breath changed, and there was a shape. The shape changed, and there was a life. Now there has been another change, and she is dead. It is like spring, summer, autumn and winter following each other.

She is lying at rest now, he said, in the great room of the world. If I followed her with sobbing and wailing, I would be showing that I did not understand what happens to all of us. So I stopped.

Set beside the first reaction, the answer looks different. The story does not show a man who felt nothing. It shows a man who grieved first, by his own account, then looked again, and then stopped. The drumming is what came after the second look, not in place of the first. The word that carries the whole story is the small one near the start: when she first died.

The early chapters, the ones held to be closest to the man, test the idea at another funeral, and there the book is at its hardest. A famous old master has died. His friend comes to mourn, wails three times, and walks out. A student stops him and asks whether that is really enough for a friend. The mourner explains what he saw inside. Old people were weeping as if they had lost a child, and young people as if they had lost a mother. All of them were crying more than they meant to. The old sayings, he says, called that running away from heaven, and the pain it brings is its own punishment. The master came when it was his time, and left when it was his turn to go. If you settle into your time and go along with how things turn, grief and joy cannot get in.

That is harsher than anything Zhuangzi says over his wife. The ideal here is not grief followed by a second look. It is a settling so complete that grief never gets in at all. So the book holds two pictures of mourning. In the early chapter, grief is kept out. In the later story, grief comes first and the second look follows. The account given here builds on the later picture, because it is the one a grieving person can actually live. The early one is still the book's ideal, and it should be heard as the book's.

The same early chapter ends on one short image, the gentlest line the book has about death. The fuel burns up, but the fire passes on. Nobody knows where it ends. The image has an edge. What carries over is a process, the burning. What does not carry over is the fuel. The line promises no personal survival, and it does not say what the fire is.

The book gives the man's own death the same treatment, again told by his followers. As Zhuangzi lay dying, his students planned a grand funeral. I will have heaven and earth for my coffin, he said, and the sun and moon for my jade ornaments. The stars will be my pearls, and every living thing will see me off. What is missing? But master, they said, we are afraid the crows and kites will eat you. Above ground, he said, I feed the crows and kites. Below ground, I feed the ants and crickets. You would take me from one lot and give me to the other. Why be so one-sided?

One-sided is the right word, and it goes back to the monkeys. The students had picked a side, the ants over the crows, without noticing that it was a side. The dying man had found the hinge, even for his own body.

The later chapters push further still. On a journey, Zhuangzi finds an old, dried-out skull by the road. He taps it with his riding whip and asks how it ended up like this: greed, war, a crime, cold and hunger, or just old age. Then he uses it as a pillow and goes to sleep. At midnight the skull comes to him in a dream. In death, it says, there is no ruler above you and no one beneath you, and no seasons of work. Not even a king on his throne is this happy. Zhuangzi does not believe it. He offers to have the god of fate give it back its body, its family and its friends. The skull frowns hard, and asks why it would give up a king's happiness to go back to the labour of being alive.

The skull is the girl at the palace, speaking back to the road. She wept on the way, and was sorry once she arrived. The skull has arrived, and it refuses to go back. It even answers the teacher's third question, in the direction he raised it: the dead, it says, are not sorry at all. But the skull's report comes from somewhere too. It speaks from the far side, and the far side is a standpoint, as partial as the living one. The book gives the line to a skull in a dream, and Zhuangzi does not believe it at first. The story works as a joke against the living, who are just as sure the other way. It does not settle which side is better, and nothing in the book's own arguments could.

Put at its strongest, the complaint is simple. This is coldness wearing the clothes of wisdom. Love is measured by grief, and a person who can look at a dead wife and think about the seasons has loved less. Worse, this kind of consolation is cruel when offered to someone else. Telling a grieving mother that death is like autumn is an insult.

The answer is in the text, and it is specific. Zhuangzi did not begin with the seasons. He began with the grief, and said so to his friend's face. He does not tell Hui Shi to drum. The second look is something he takes for himself, afterwards. What the book claims is narrower than the objection fears: the first reaction to a death does not have to be the only one. A love that can survive the second look has lost nothing by it.

There is a real limit, too. The seasons are a picture of a death that comes at its time, from age or illness, to a life that ran its course. They are no comfort about a death that is wrong: a child killed, a prisoner executed, a woman killed. Autumn does not console a frost in spring. The book's consolation is about death as change. Its word for such harm, when you are the one it happens to, is to know what cannot be helped, and rest in it as fate. Another man whose foot was cut off says it about his own maiming, and a victim may rightly refuse it. The book's anger at such harm lives elsewhere, in its chapters about thieves who become lords.

The belief that death is the worst thing, and that grief is the measure of love, is left half standing. Grief is real, it is right, and love does grieve. Even Zhuangzi, by his own account, felt it. What falls is the idea that dying is simply bad, seen from nowhere, and that the size of the grief is the size of the love. The fear of your own death, the one nobody talks about, is the girl on the road, crying at a place she has never seen.

Even life against death, then, is a "this" against a "that". Seeing it is a second look, taken after the first. The man at the basin grieved, and then stopped. But the argument has now taken away a lot. Nothing is simply right from nowhere, the self is a moving pattern, and not even death is simply bad. It can look as if nothing is left to condemn anyone with, and the book's last stories set out to show that something is.

Part 9: Seven Holes

An ethics does survive all of this, and it is a specific one. Its central wrong is doing your good to someone who does not stand where you stand. And its central anger is at goodness stolen and worn by the powerful. The book puts the first half into its strangest story, which is also the last story of its first seven chapters.

There were three emperors. The emperor of the southern sea was called Swift. The emperor of the northern sea was called Sudden. And the emperor of the middle was called Hundun, which means something like the Unformed. Swift and Sudden often met in Hundun's land, and he always treated them very well. They wanted to repay his kindness. Everyone has seven openings, they said, to see and hear, to eat and breathe, and he alone has none. Let us give him some. So each day they bored one hole. On the seventh day Hundun died.

The usual first reading is that the story is about meddling: leave things alone. That reading misses what the text is careful to say. Swift and Sudden were not meddlers or tyrants. They were grateful guests, returning a kindness. They gave Hundun the best thing they knew, the thing that made their own lives good, which was a face. What killed him was their generosity, measured by their own shape.

The later chapters tell the same story with a bird. Once a sea bird landed outside a king's capital. The ruler welcomed it into the temple, poured it wine, and had the grandest court music played for it. He laid out a feast of the finest sacrificial meats. The bird stared, dazed and miserable. It would not eat a bite or drink a cup, and in three days it was dead. The text's verdict is exact. He cared for the bird the way he would want to be cared for, and not the way a bird lives.

Two stories, one structure, and the horse lover from the dangerous court makes a third. In each, the one who harms is sincere. The emperors are grateful, the ruler is honouring a guest, the horse lover adores his horse. And in each, the good they do is good from where they stand: a face, a feast, a slap at a fly. The harm comes from never asking where the other one stands.

With the particulars removed, the case looks like this. There is an old rule that you should treat others as you would want to be treated. The ruler with the sea bird followed that rule in its crudest form: give others what you yourself would want. He gave the bird wine, music and the best meat. The rule, used that way, killed the bird, because it measures the other by your own shape. The book's version, stated generally, is different. Treat each creature from where it stands. The text even says what that means for the bird. Let it nest in the deep forest, eat fish, and fly with its flock.

That positive half is the whole book's ethic in small. It is the cook's attention to the ox, and the keeper's attention to the tiger, turned into care. And it is the fasting of the heart, which stops you from matching the other person to what you already have. The wrong the book can name is the wrong of the seven holes: goodness imposed from one standpoint onto a life that stands in another.

The ethic has an obvious hard case. Standing where the other stands sounds like giving everyone what they want. But a child wants to skip school, and a man who drinks wants another drink. If the rule is to treat each creature from where it stands, it seems to hand the bottle to the drinker.

The sea bird answers this better than it first seems. The ruler's failure was not that he refused the bird what it asked for. The bird asked for nothing. His failure was that he never looked at it: at what it ate, where it slept, how it lived when nobody was honouring it. The book's cure is not obedience to wants. It is knowledge of how the creature actually lives. So the rule is to look from where the other stands before acting, and then to act, sometimes against what they want in the moment. The stories themselves never go that far; this is a step beyond them, in their direction. What they forbid is acting from your own shape without ever having looked. A parent who has really looked at a child, and still sends him to school, has bored no holes in anyone.

The same later chapters take the seven holes from one friendship to a whole trade. A horse, says one of those chapters, has hooves that can walk on frost and snow, and a coat that keeps out the wind. It eats grass, drinks water, kicks up its heels and runs. Then came a famous horse expert, who said, I am good at handling horses. He branded them and clipped them, cut their hooves, tied them up and packed them into stables, and two or three in every ten died. Then he starved them and drilled them, with the bit in front and the whip behind, and more than half were dead. The potter boasts the same way about clay, and the carpenter about wood, and people praise them for generations. This, the chapter says, is exactly the mistake of the people who run the world.

Here the imposed good has become a profession. The horse expert is not cruel. He is proud of his skill, and he turns out useful horses. What the passage counts is the cost his trade never writes down. There are the dead horses, and there is the living horse that no longer runs for its own sake. It is the sea bird's feast, organised and repeated. Any institution can run on the same pattern whenever it measures a creature only by the use it wants from it. These chapters also appeal to a horse's true nature, which is a firmer standard than the early chapters ever use.

The second half of the ethic is anger, and it lives in another later chapter, in a sharper voice than the rest. Steal a belt buckle, it says, and they execute you. Steal a whole country, and you become a lord. And once you are a lord, goodness and justice move into your palace. Then comes the question: is that not stealing goodness and justice too?

The target of that anger is precise. The powerful take the words for good along with the land, and then use those words to decide who gets executed. The thief of a state becomes the judge of right and wrong, and his "this is right" is now the kingdom's law. That chapter's voice is angrier than the rest of the book, and its own cure goes further than taking goodness back. Get rid of the sages and throw away their knowledge, it says, and the great thieves will stop. On that view the vocabulary of goodness is the thief's favourite tool, and the safest thing is to stop making it.

Now the old worry about the cruel man returns. If right is only right from somewhere, it seems nobody can say the cruel man is wrong. The book's answer has two layers, and one holds better than the other.

The first layer comes straight from the argument about judges. The thief of a state does not merely have a "this". He claims his "this" is justice itself, and he sits in the judge's seat that the book showed no one can occupy. So the book can condemn that claim without claiming a higher seat for itself. It is the seat the book has argued no one can show they hold. And refusing him is not forcing a good on him. It is refusing to have his good forced on you.

The second layer is weaker. The book condemns the harm itself, the bored holes and the dead bird, from the place of the one harmed. That is where it always stands. It never argues why that place outranks the place of the one doing the harm. A cruel man who makes no claim to goodness escapes the first charge. Against him, the book has only its choice of where to stand, and that is a real gap.

A related gap was named, sharply, by a Confucian philosopher a generation or two after Zhuangzi. He went through the thinkers of his age and said each one was blinded by a single thing. Zhuangzi, he said, was blinded by heaven, by the way things naturally go, and did not know the human. And he said what that blindness costs. If you treat the way things naturally go as the whole Way, the Way turns into nothing but going along.

The charge lands on every hero so far. The cook goes along with the ox. The keeper goes along with the tiger. The monkey keeper went along with the monkeys. And the human world, for this critic, is exactly what does not go along. Laws, schools, rituals and promises are all things people build against the way things would drift on their own. A philosophy of going along cannot build a court, a school or a just law. It seems to leave the tiger fed and on its throne.

Part of that objection can be answered. The going along in the book is steering, not submission, as the keeper showed. The anger at stolen goodness is the opposite of going along with power. And the seven holes are a warning to exactly the builders the critic admires: reformers who give everyone a face whether they want one or not.

But the larger part of the objection stands. The book's own advice to rulers is to impose nothing. Follow things as they are, it says, admit no private view, and the world will be governed. How to stop a ruler who imposes by force, or when to resist one, it never says. It has no account of which laws to write and no duty to resist. The critic is right that the human world of laws and schools is largely missing from the book. So the ethic that survives is real, and it is small. Do not impose your good. Look for where the other one stands. Refuse goodness that has been stolen. It is a check on every reformer, and it is not itself a reform.

The book leaves that ethic standing, beside a gap it never fills. Everything so far now has to fit into one life. Standpoints without a judge, a moving self, a trained response, worth outside any single measure, a second look at death, and this small ethic. What that life looks like from the inside, on an ordinary day, is where the book ends.

Part 10: Wandering

The book's name for the life all of this makes is wandering. Wandering means moving without a fixed destination, going where the situation opens, and not belonging to any single place you stand. It is what is left when every earlier argument has done its work. The word sits in the title of the book's first chapter, the one with the giant bird. The book has been walking toward it the whole time.

At the top of that chapter's ladder stood three short lines: no self, no achievement, no name. There they sounded like riddles. Now each has an argument behind it.

No self is the moving self from the butterfly, and the student who said there had never been a Hui. It does not mean nobody is there. It means there is no fixed judge inside to defend. No achievement is the useless tree. It means a life not measured by the use someone else can make of it. No name is the hinge. A name is a position, a "this" you have to keep defending, and a reputation for goodness is exactly what the thieves of states put on. The person with no name has nothing of that kind to protect.

A later story shows what having no self looks like on an ordinary river. Say you are crossing a river, and an empty boat drifts into yours. Even someone with a short temper will not get angry. But if there is a person in that boat, you shout at him to steer away. If he does not hear, you shout again, and by the third time you are swearing at him. You were not angry before and you are angry now, the story says, only because before the boat was empty and now someone is in it. If a person can empty himself and drift through the world like that, who could hurt him?

The story has two sides. Seen from your own boat, anger follows the person more than the bump. Seen from the other boat, a person who has emptied himself gives the anger nothing to catch on.

A study found the first side in numbers. People were told about a village whose river had dried up, and shown the same list of losses in dollars. Asked to add up the total, those told it dried up for lack of rain got it about right. Told that a man upstream had diverted it on purpose, they guessed nearly double. Even when paid for accuracy, they still added about half as much again. The limit is that this was money in a story, not a blow to anyone's own life, and no study can tell you whether to forgive. But the damage was identical, and a person who did it on purpose made it bigger. The boat story has no purpose in it, only a person, and the anger arrives anyway. The argument you replay at night is crowded with people in boats.

The book has a story about what it looks like when someone tries to pin such a person down. In one state there was a shaman who could tell you the year, the month and the day you would die, and he was always right. People ran when they saw him coming. A student was so impressed that he brought the shaman home to meet his own teacher. The next day the shaman came out and took the student aside. Your teacher is finished, he said. He will not last ten days. I saw wet ashes in his face.

The student ran to his teacher in tears. The teacher only said, bring him again tomorrow. Each day the shaman came back, and each day the teacher showed him a different face. Still as earth, then alive as the sky, then perfectly balanced. Each day the shaman's reading changed. On the fourth day, before he had even sat down, the shaman lost his nerve and ran. Chase him, said the teacher, but he was gone.

The story is aimed at the student, not the shaman. The shaman was genuinely good at what he did: he read a fixed face and forecast its future. The student was dazzled by that kind of reading. The teacher showed him that a person who is not fixed cannot be read that way. Every forecast assumes the face will stay the face it is now. The one who can be earth one day and sky the next cannot be pinned down by any fortune-teller or any single measure.

And then the ending, which is the most important detail in the story. After that, the student decided he had never really started learning. He went home and did not go out for three years. He cooked for his wife. He fed the pigs the way you would feed people. He took no sides in things. He stripped away the polish he had been proud of, and went back to being plain wood.

That is where wandering ends up. Not on a mountain, and not riding the wind. In a kitchen, cooking for someone, feeding the animals with the care the sea bird never got. The student who went looking for marvels ends by attending to the pigs as they are. It is the cook's attention and the keeper's, turned on an ordinary household.

The dragon and the snake from the goose story are wandering described from outside. From inside, it is the plainer thing the student did: this meal, this animal, this person, each met from where it stands.

If every judgment is from somewhere, the book's own words are from somewhere too. A later chapter gives a picture for how to hold them. A fish trap is there for the fish, and once you have the fish, you forget the trap. A snare is there for the rabbit, and once you have the rabbit, you forget the snare. Words are there for the meaning, and once you have the meaning, you forget the words. Then it ends with a joke. Where can I find someone who has forgotten words, so that I can have a word with him?

The joke is the point. Words are needed, and the book is full of them. But the words are a trap for something that is not a word. It is the cook's eye, the keeper's feel for the tiger, the second look at a death. A person who has that no longer needs the sentences, and can still talk.

Laid end to end, the whole argument runs in ten steps. One: usefulness, size and sense are measured from somewhere, and the same salve is worthless or a fortune depending on whose hands hold it. Two: right and wrong work like here and there, so every "this" makes a "that". Three: no judge can stand outside a dispute between standpoints, because every judge is somewhere. Four: the self who judges is itself moving, a run of notes from one shape in changing wind. Five: so acting well is a trained skill of response, attending to the thing and not to a plan for it. Six: the training is subtraction, letting go of the view you arrived with. Seven: a person's worth is not the use a system measures, and no fixed position, not even uselessness, is safe. Eight: even life against death is a "this" against a "that", seen after grief as change. Nine: what ethics survives is the wrong of imposing your good, and the anger at stolen goodness. Ten: the life that holds all this is wandering, which ends in a kitchen, attending to what is in front of you.

That leaves the first sensible belief, that a good life is spent finding the correct answer and holding to it. Inside a shared standpoint there are correct answers, and they are worth finding and holding: the count of nuts is seven. What goes is the idea that the good life means choosing one standpoint and defending it against all the others.

For the night the argument began with, that changes a good deal. The argument you keep replaying is often the monkeys' quarrel, morning against evening, over the same seven nuts. Not always, since the book gives no test for that, but often. You can see it as two standpoints and still choose, the way the keeper still chose what to feed. If you feel useless in your job beside people going somewhere, you are the giant gourd, judged by someone who only wanted to hold water. The fear of losing someone ill is the drumming man's first grief, and it is right, and the second look can come after it. And the fear of your own death is the girl on the road, crying at a palace she has never seen.

The price has not changed. This is a way to stand, not a way to fix things. The book can see the tyrant, name his stolen goodness, and teach you to live beside his tiger. When to resist him, it never says.

So the book leaves you in a particular place. You can stand at the hinge and see every side of the quarrel, including the side of the sea bird and the side of Hundun. And from wherever you happen to be standing, you will still have to decide whether seeing clearly is enough. Or whether someone still has to build the thing this book never built.
