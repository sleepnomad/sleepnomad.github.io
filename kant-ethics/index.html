Cold open

A man comes to your door. He is holding a knife, and he asks, politely, whether your friend is in the house. Your friend is in the house. You know what the man wants. Every person who has ever heard this story knows what to do, and it takes less than a second. You lie.

Immanuel Kant said you may not. He said it in print, late in his life, to a critic who had put exactly this case to him, and he did not soften it. You may not lie to the man with the knife.1 If you tell him the truth and your friend dies, the death is not yours. If you lie, and the lie goes wrong, and your friend dies anyway, the death is yours.2 Ever since, this answer has been the joke people tell about Kant. The philosopher so in love with rules that he would hand a friend to a murderer rather than bend one.

Most people carry a picture of morality that is roughly the opposite of this. Being good is about the results an act gets and the kind of person someone is. Rules are rough guides, and a kind person knows when to bend them. A small lie that keeps the peace is fine. A person's kindness is theirs, and they deserve credit for it. That picture is attractive, and most of the people who hold it are decent, and it breaks in three places the moment it is pushed. If goodness is the kind of person someone is, then a lucky temperament is virtue and an unlucky one is vice. Nobody deserves praise or blame for either. If goodness is results, then two men pull a child from the river and one does it for the reward. He is exactly as good as the one who did it because she was drowning. And if a kind person may bend a rule when it seems right, every promise made to anyone lasts only as long as their judgment does. Which means it was never a promise. Everyone who holds the attractive picture refuses all three of these the moment they are on the receiving end.

Kant's morality is what comes of taking those three refusals seriously and following them all the way down. It ends at a place harder than most people are willing to stand. A morality that nobody can be seen to keep, that gives no credit, that allows no exception, and that binds everyone anyway. And the man at the door is where it finally breaks, in a way that shows what it was made of.

Part 1: The Only Good Thing

Kant's whole morality starts from one sentence, and the sentence is a claim most people would deny on first hearing. Nothing in the world, or outside it, is good without any condition except a good will. Not intelligence, and not courage, and not a calm temperament, not health, not wealth, and not even happiness. Every one of those is good only on a condition, and the condition is the will of the person who has it.8 The will is the one thing that is good on no condition at all. What follows is the reason to believe it.

That sounds like a beautiful thing to say and a hard thing to defend. Kant's own defence is the corrupt villain and the observer who takes no pleasure in his prospering. The defence that follows is built on what luck can and cannot reach, and it is offered as the reason his line holds. Two people each try to pull a child out of a river. One reaches her, and the other, with the same will, the same effort, the same risk, is beaten by the current, and the child is lost. Which of them is the better person. The question feels wrong, and the reason it feels wrong is the whole point. The current is not a fact about either of them. If goodness lived in the outcome, then the river would decide who was good, and the river cannot decide anything. Everything that separates the two rescuers is luck. Luck reaches the outcome, and it reaches the surgeon's hands, the timing, the weather, the strength of the current. There is exactly one thing in the whole scene that luck cannot reach, and that is what each of them was trying to do and why. The claim under Kant's line is that this is where goodness lives, because it is the only place goodness could live and still be theirs.

The surgeon objection has not gone away. The shaking hands matter, of course they do, and Kant never says otherwise. Skill is good, and you should want the surgeon with the steady hands. But skill is on the first list, the list of conditional goods, and the fact that you would rather have it does not move it. The steady-handed surgeon who does not care about you is useful to you. That is a different thing from being good, and the proof is that his steadiness would serve a worse purpose exactly as well.

So much for the first step. Every gift you have, chosen or not, is good on a condition, and the condition is the will that holds it. Happiness is on that list, which is why the prosperity of a cruel person looks wrong to everyone who sees it. The will is the only thing that is good on no condition, because it is the only thing luck cannot decide. And that means goodness does not live in what you achieve, but in what you will, and why. If the good will is not the good it does, then something else makes it good, and nothing said so far says what.

Part 2: Duty Against Inclination

A will is good when it does what is right because it is right, and not because of anything it wants. Kant's word for that is duty. An act from duty is one that would still be done if every wish, mood and appetite in the person pointed the other way. It is easy to state and very easy to misread. The misreading is so old that Kant answered it himself. First the cases that make the distinction visible.

Two shopkeepers, the first of them Kant's and the second the one his argument needs. Both keep a fixed price, so that a child who comes in with her mother's money pays the same as anyone else. Both are honest, in the plain sense that neither of them ever cheats a customer. The first one keeps his fixed price because it is good for business.14 A shop where a child gets cheated is a shop people stop coming to, and he knows it. He is honest the way a man is honest who has done the arithmetic. The second one keeps his fixed price because cheating the child would be wrong. He would keep the price if business fell, and he would keep it in a town with no competitors, where nobody would ever find out. The two men do the same thing every day. From outside they cannot be told apart. Kant says one of them is honest from duty and the other is honest in a way that merely agrees with duty. Only the first has what he calls moral worth.

Something subtler is left over, and it survives the answer just given. Grant that Kant is not praising coldness. He is still saying that the cold helper's worth is visible and the warm helper's is not. Does that not make the cold man better, in the only sense that can be checked. That gap, between a worth that is real and a worth that can be seen, is the strangest thing in Kant's morality. It reaches all the way to your own view of yourself. There is one more case, and it is the one that shows duty is not the enemy of your own life. A man in despair, worn down by misfortune, wishes for death. Part 6: What You Owe Yourself

A person can wrong himself, and it is the same wrong he does to anyone else. It is the part of Kant's ethics that almost nobody carries out of the room, because it sounds like a figure of speech. It is not one. Kant means that a person has, in his own case, someone he can use merely as a means. When that happens, the thing damaged is the same thing the false promise damages, and the knife in the taxi. Three cases show it, and the third is the one that matters most for the rest.

Before the cases, the thing that makes the whole idea look confused, and Kant raised it himself. A duty is something one person owes another. If I owe it, I am bound, and if another is owed it, he can release me. But a duty to myself would mean that I am both the one bound and the one who can release. And a person who can release himself whenever he likes is not bound at all. So a duty to oneself looks like a contradiction.39 Kant's answer uses a distinction that is going to carry the whole account of freedom later. The one who is bound is a person as a creature in the world, with appetites, moods, a body that wants things. The one who binds is that same person as a lawgiver, the one who can ask what a rule for everyone would be. They are one person regarded two ways.40 And the second cannot release the first. Releasing would mean asking whether a rule that lets me off could hold for everyone, and no such rule can. So the duty holds. The person who owes it and the person who is owed it are the same person, seen from two places.

Part 9: Freedom

Nobody can prove that they are free. Nobody can act without presupposing that they are. And that presupposition, Kant says, is all that morality needs. Those are three claims, and each is harder than it sounds. The first one Kant learned the hard way, by trying the proof and failing, and saying so. The second is the centre of his whole account of what a person is. The third is what lets the argument stand without the proof.

First, the problem, felt properly. Everything that happens in the world has a cause. A stone falls because it was dropped, and a leaf turns because the light moved. A person is a thing in the world. A body, a brain, the mood he woke up in, the thing he is about to say. All of it has causes running back through his childhood into the weather of centuries before he was born. If that is the whole story, then the second shopkeeper's because was a cause like any other. His honesty was produced in him by the chain, the way the leaf's turning was produced in the leaf. And then the law he gave himself was not given. It was delivered, by everything that made him, and he was the place it arrived. On that picture, there is nothing to praise and nothing to blame. Everything said so far was a description of how some machines happen to run.

Kant does not deny one word of the causal story. Most people expect him to. He does not say there is a gap in the chain where a free choice slips in. He holds that everything in the world of experience has a cause and could in principle be predicted. That includes every human action, as an event. What he says instead uses an idea from his account of knowledge, and the ethics needs it. The world of experience is the world as it appears to a being built the way people are. Space, time, cause and effect are the form experience takes. They are how things show up to a mind, and not necessarily how things are apart from being shown. The red of an apple is not in the apple and not in the light; it is made, every time, by the eye that meets it. So there are two ways of regarding anything, including a person. As it appears to a being built the way people are, in time, under causes. And as it is in itself, about which almost nothing can be known. Now Kant's move. When a person regards himself as a thing in the world, an object among objects, he falls under causes, and there is no freedom. But when he regards himself as the one deciding what to do, he is not looking at himself from outside. He is standing in a second place, the place from which the question what should I do is asked. And from that place, the things that would look like causes from outside look like reasons. From outside, the shopkeeper's honesty was produced by his upbringing. From inside, when he decides, his upbringing is not what he consults. He consults whether it is right, and the same fact, seen from the two places, is a cause and a reason. Kant's phrase is that a rational being has two standpoints from which it can regard itself. One under the laws of nature, one under the law of reason.80 The duty to the self already used them, when the one who owes and the one who is owed turned out to be one person.

Part 12: How Much Is Owed

Kant's law allows no exception, and most of what it asks leaves room. Both of those are true at once. The second makes the first bearable, and the first makes the second honest. There are two kinds of duty, and there are two kinds of law, and the murderer at the door sat on the seam between them. Getting the seam right is what stops Kant's ethics from being the machine its critics describe.

The two kinds of duty were named when the false promise and the cold world failed in two different ways, the perfect and the imperfect. A perfect duty, Kant says in a footnote, is one that admits no exception in favour of inclination.122 Do not make the false promise, do not keep the deposit, do not destroy yourself. These say exactly what not to do, and there is no room in them. An imperfect duty is the other kind. Help people. Develop what you could do. These say what to aim at and not how, or how much, or when, or for whom. And this is the sentence that changes what the whole law feels like. A wide duty, which is Kant's other word for an imperfect one, is not a permission to make exceptions to the maxim of your actions. It is a permission to limit one maxim of duty by another. His example is general love of your neighbour limited by love of your parents.123 A person is bound to help, and not bound to help everyone equally. That is one duty giving another its shape, and the room is inside the law.

Something has to do the lifting. The question has been asked twice here and walked away from twice. Part 15: One Thought Too Many

The strongest living objections to Kant are not about the murderer. They are two. That he asks you to think one thought too many, so that a person who lived by his law would be a worse person. And that moral judgment is simply not made the way he says it is, and a science has now shown that. The first is answered, and the second is half conceded, and the half that is conceded does not touch the law. Two older objections come after them, and the answers to those were given long before they were asked.

The first objection has a case, and the case is famous for a reason. A man is at the water when two people are drowning, and he can save one. One is his wife. He saves his wife. Now ask what was going through his mind as he went in. On the picture the objection attributes to Kant, the man's thought, fully spelled out, has two parts. That it was his wife, and that in situations of this kind it is permissible to save one's wife. The philosopher who made this case famous wrote that this construction gives the man one thought too many. His wife might have hoped that his motivating thought was simply that it was her.163 Not that it was her, and that the rule allowed it. The charge under the case is larger than the case. It is that Kant's morality stands between a person and the people he loves. That the shopkeeper who gives correct change because it is right is admirable. But the husband who saves his wife because it is right is a kind of monster. And that a whole life run this way, with the law consulted before every attachment, is not a moral life but a thin one.

Part 16: What Survives

What survives of Kant is the shape of a moral demand, and the shape is this. A rule either holds for everyone or it is not a rule. Every person on the other end of an act can refuse to be merely used, and that refusal is not something anyone gets to overrule. Freedom holds, not because anyone proved it, but because nobody can decide anything without standing where the demand is made. And nobody gets credit, because the one place worth lives is the one place nobody can see. What falls is smaller than people think. The answer at the door, as Kant gave it. The claim that the first formula alone can fix what it tests. The door needed the second to say who was in the maxim. The postulates, if anyone tries to make them more than a hope, as Kant himself did. That is the ruling. What follows is what it does when it is carried out of the argument and into the small hours of an ordinary life.

The small lie first, because it is the one everyone tells. Not the polite formula at the foot of a letter, which Kant left open and stays open. The lie told to manage someone: the diagnosis softened, the money trouble hidden, the evening smoothed by a fact withheld from the person it belongs to. The attractive picture said this was fine, and Kant's argument says something more exact than that it is wrong. It says the small lie is the whole of morality failing at one point. Not a small failure. The whole thing, small. Because the rule under it is: when someone cannot be trusted with a truth about their own life, I decide it for them. The person spared is being managed, kindly, by someone who has decided they cannot handle their own life, and they could not agree to that. So the small lie is the false promise and the taxi driver with the knife, reduced to a size where nobody feels it. That is not a reason to be cruel. The truth can be told with every gentleness the situation permits. It is a reason to notice that the gentleness was never the problem, and the management was.

Then the private exception. The one nobody knows about, that has been earned, that this once is fine. Kant's argument does not say that exceptions are wrong. It says that there is exactly one kind of exception that nobody may make. The kind that comes from a judgment that this case is different. The murderer showed the shape: an exception from the rule is the rule applied, and an exception from the person is the clause Kant refused. The private exception is the second kind, every time, and the dear self is the one doing the judging, always with a good reason.

Then the credit. The good thing a person did, and wanted to have been his. The ruling here sounds cruel and turns out to be the kindest thing in the account. He cannot have it, and the reason was the dear self: whether it was his is a fact he has no access to. So the question behind every kind act ever done, did I do it for the right reason, is dismissed. Dismissed for lack of a court that could hear it. What is left when the credit goes is the act, and the rule under it, and the next act. That is all Kant ever said anyone had. It is more than most people think they have, because it is the only part that luck cannot reach.180
