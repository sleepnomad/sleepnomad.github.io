Cold open

Suppose you stopped believing years ago, and you are not sorry. You are a decent person, with work, a few people you love, some goals, and a rule that you try not to hurt anyone. Under all of it there is a hollow feeling you cannot name, and the advice to make your own meaning has never told you how. The explanation you have probably settled on is that the hollow feeling is the price of honesty. The universe does not care, so nothing can matter very much, and a grown-up lives with that.

Nietzsche's book points the other way, and the claim built from it here is this. The hollow feeling comes from a very specific set of values that you never chose and cannot pay for. Suffering is the worst thing there is, everyone is equal, stay safe and do not stand out.1 Those were guaranteed, once, by a God who was going to make it all add up. The God is gone, and the bill still arrives every month. What you feel is a morality with its guarantee removed.2 The book's name for the person who lives contentedly on that code is the last man. The hollow feeling is what the same code feels like in someone who is not content yet.

The decent, comfortable, modern person is not living without values. He is living on a very particular set of them. Suffering is the worst thing there is.16 Everyone is the same as everyone else.17 Keep your health, keep your small pleasures, and do not stand out.18 That set used to be paid for by a God who promised that a life lived that way would add up to something. A moral code that says do not hurt people, treat them as equals and look after your health is not a failure of nerve. And now you can see why the ranking hurts, even though it looks like sanity. A value at the top of a life has to be worth living for, not just worth avoiding. Try to wake up tomorrow and pursue the absence of pain. It cannot be pursued, only defended, and a life that consists of defence has a particular texture. Everything in it is there to protect, and nothing in it is the thing worth protecting, so none of it can ever feel like enough. That texture is the hollow feeling. The old code put the sufferer at the centre of the moral world. It could afford to, because the suffering was going to be repaid. Everyone equal, be humble, do not rise, was a code that came with a guarantee. A life lived that way would be judged, rewarded, made to add up. Part 3: The Person Who Decided

A decision can carry a person to the place where a new value could grow, and it can never be the value. That is the claim of this part, and it is here because the last part has an obvious hole in it. People do change what they live for, and they do it on purpose, and everyone knows somebody who has. The dentist and the flossing showed that a weak resolution fails. It showed nothing about a strong one.

So here is the objection at its full strength, as three people. A woman stopped drinking on a date she can still name, after nineteen years. A man who cared for nothing but his career joined a lifeboat crew at thirty-five because a friend asked him. At forty-five it is the thing he would give the career up for. A couple who liked their freedom sat at a kitchen table and decided to have a child. The child is now the centre of two lives. Each of them decided, and each of them now holds something at the top of their life that was not there before. If that is not choosing a value by an act of will, the objection says, then the words mean nothing. And a book that tells the last man he cannot fix himself by resolution has been refuted at every meeting in every church basement.

What anyone would expect is that the decision made the value. She decided to value sobriety, and then she valued it. Look at what happened in her case in the order it happened, because the order is the argument.

On the morning she decided, she wanted a drink exactly as much as she had the morning before. The decision changed nothing in what she wanted, and she will tell you so. What it changed was where she put her body. It poured the bottles into the sink. It drove her home by a road that did not pass the shop, and it sat her in a room of strangers on a Tuesday night. It picked up the phone at eleven at night when every part of her wanted something else. For months she did not value being sober. She endured it, with her jaw set, one evening at a time. All of that was the conscious will at full strength, and all of it was a no.

Then, on some ordinary evening she cannot date, she noticed that she did not want it. Something else had come to matter more, a clear head in the morning, or her daughter no longer checking her face at the door. She did not decide that. She found it already there, the way you find that a wound has closed. So her story has two events in it and not one. The decision has a date, and the value has none, because it arrived, and she found it after it had come.

Now the second person, whom you may also have met. He made the same decision, poured out the same bottles, sat in the same room, and has not had a drink in eleven years. And nothing ever arrived. He is dry and he is bitter, and his whole life is organised around the thing he does not do. He is sober against drink, the way the man who left the church at nineteen is free against the church. Part 9: Willing Is Not Accepting

Accepting a past means you have stopped fighting a fact you cannot change. Willing a past means that your yes to your own life no longer waits on that fact being different. That is the difference the speech on redemption promised, and this part is where it has to be paid. A fair listener can still say that the man in the cave only did what most of us were told to do with a bad memory. He made his peace with it. If that is right, the book's hardest demand is an old one with a fiercer name, and the argument has been standing on a word.

Two people with the same past are needed here, because one example only teaches the example. Each of them trained from childhood as a dancer. Each of them, at twenty-four, was hit by a car, spent two years learning to walk again, and never danced after that. It is the thing each would have given anything to undo. Both have had twenty years and a great deal of honest work since then.

The first has accepted it. She stopped arguing with it years ago. She can say, without her voice changing, that it happened and that no amount of rage will make it not have happened. She sleeps. That is a real achievement, and nothing said here is a sneer at it. An ordinary day of hers shows what that peace is made of. Those two years are a sealed room in her house, and her life is built carefully around it. She does not go to the ballet, and she changes the channel. When a friend is in hospital for months, she sends flowers and a kind message, and she does not visit. Suppose she were offered a button that erased the accident. She would lunge for it before the offer was finished.

The second woman has the same event behind her, and it is exactly as bad. She lost the one thing she had trained for, and she does not tell herself that this was for the best. But on an ordinary day she is the one people telephone when somebody faces a long time in a hospital bed. She can sit in that room for hours, because she knows to the inch what a month on your back does to a person. She goes to the ballet, and it hurts, and she goes. Offered the button, she does something the first woman did not. She stops to think, and the reason is not that the accident was secretly good. She cannot find the line where those years stop and she begins. In the end she may well press it. What matters is that she did not lunge.

Those are the two cases, and the lunge is what separates them. The music teacher gave the measure already. A grudge is a thing you need undone before you can be content with your own life. The first woman looks as though she needs nothing, because she sleeps. The button shows otherwise, since her contentment was never with her life as it is. It was with a life in which that room stays shut. The button is only a probe, and the need is the thing it finds. For the second woman the accident is part of what she is standing on when she says yes to anything at all. Her yes does not wait on the button. The button is also the gate with its direction reversed. The gate asks whether you would have it all again, and the button asks whether you need it gone.

The defence of acceptance deserves a proper hearing. You have described bad acceptance and good acceptance, its defender will say, and given the good one a harder name. Acceptance done properly means taking the past in and growing from it. The second woman is what that looks like when it is finished. Part of that is simply granted. If acceptance means the second woman, the quarrel is about a word, and the word can go to whoever wants it. But the sentence most of us were actually handed stops short at one exact place. You cannot change the past, so make your peace with it. The premise is powerlessness, and the peace is drawn from the premise. It is a treaty signed because the other side cannot be beaten. The button does one thing, which is to take that premise away. And the first woman's peace is gone in a second, because it was never peace with the event. It was peace with her own helplessness in front of it. The speech on redemption asked for something higher than all reconciliation, and that phrase now has a meaning.226 Reconciliation is the treaty. What stands higher is a yes that would survive the treaty's premise being removed.

And now the things you would undo, because that is where the test bites hardest. The book ran it on Zarathustra's contempt for other people. Running it on your own past is an extension made here, and the book's own phrase, to redeem those who are past, invites it.329 The doors come first. Go through the ones you keep closed because of what happened, and ask of each whether it protects anybody. Keep those. Of the rest, ask whether opening them would feel like getting off too lightly. Those are the sentence, and by now you know who is serving it. Then the button. If you could erase the thing, would you. If you would lunge for it, you have made peace with being helpless. That peace is worth having, and the book asks for more. It asks whether you could say yes to the person standing here, and let that yes run back along everything tied into them. It would have to run through the worst of it too. That would not mean the thing was good, because every cruelty stays exactly as cruel. It would mean the thing no longer casts a vote on who you are allowed to be. You would still remember it, and you would still know what it cost.
