Cold open

Stoicism is not a set of calming tricks. It is one hard claim. Nothing that happens to you is good or bad, and the only thing that is, is the quality of your own judgement.1 The Stoics built an entire universe to hold that claim up. Part of what they built has lasted two thousand years, and a small piece of it now has laboratory support. The rest has collapsed, and what is left still works, once you refuse a price the modern version rarely mentions.

The case to hold onto is a small, ordinary injury. In a meeting, a colleague says something dismissive about your work, in front of people whose opinion you care about. You say nothing. At two in the morning you are still replaying it, rewriting your answer, feeling the heat in your face all over again. Nothing new has happened since the meeting, and yet it keeps happening to you.

The modern advice for that night comes on a poster. You have power over your mind, not over outside events.2 Focus on what you can control and let the rest go. It is good advice, and it helps for about a day. Then the next remark arrives, and you are back where you started, because the advice never touched the thing that was keeping you awake.

And there is a cost. If the only good is the quality of your judgement, then losing someone you love cannot make your life go worse. The ancient Stoics said exactly that, out loud, and even the best of them struggled to live it. The modern version keeps the calm and rarely mentions the bill.

The poster does get one thing right: its tools are real, and they come from the ancient texts. The trouble is the exact place where it goes wrong, because that is the place where the remark keeps you awake.

Part 1: The Version You Were Sold

The modern Stoic toolkit is a real piece of the ancient school, and it has kept the one belief the school existed to remove. That belief is that the outcome is what matters. Every tool on the poster still treats the job, the praise and the result as the prize, and only changes how you chase it. The Stoics thought the prize itself was the mistake.

The modern version deserves to be stated at its strongest first, because it is not foolish. It comes as four tools, and each of them can be found in the ancient texts. The first is the line about control.11 Sort every worry into what you can affect and what you cannot, put your effort into the first pile, and stop spending sleep on the second. The second tool is reframing. When something bad happens, look for the description of it that hurts less and helps more: a setback becomes practice, a rejection becomes information. The third tool is rehearsal, and it sounds stranger than the others. Spend a few minutes picturing the loss of the things you depend on. Then, when one of them really goes, the shock has already been half absorbed. The fourth tool is composure, the one the word stoic now names. Do not let your feelings run the meeting, the argument or the crisis.

Behind the tools sits an attitude that sounds like common sense. The ancient Stoics wrapped their advice in talk of gods, fate and a universe that thinks. Nobody needs to believe any of that to use the tools, any more than you need to believe in Greek gods to use Greek geometry. And modern psychology has already done the sorting: it kept the methods that work, tested them, and threw the cosmology away.

Much of that is true, and the tools do help. People use them in hospital corridors and in divorce courts, and they report that the tools carried them through. The ancient texts really do contain all four. So this is not a fake version of Stoicism. It is a fragment, and the question is what the fragment loses by being cut off from the rest.

It breaks in three places. The first is the word control. The second is what happens to the tools when the belief underneath them is left in place. The third is the word stoic itself.

Control is easiest to see on a case. You have a job interview next week. The poster version says: the preparation is in your control, the decision is not, so prepare well and let the decision go. That sounds exactly right, and most people would stop the analysis there. You prepare for a week, you give a good interview, and the job goes to someone else. What you feel then is the test. You are crushed, and the poster has nothing to say to you, because the poster never asked what you were preparing for. You prepared in order to get the job. The preparation was valuable to you as a way of getting the outcome, so when the outcome failed, the preparation failed with it.

The ancient version makes a stranger and harder demand. It says the job was never good in the first place, only something it made sense to go after. The preparation, done well, honestly and with care, was the whole of what was good in that week. You had it the moment you did it, and nobody on the hiring panel could take it back. The poster moves your effort onto what you can affect, and leaves your heart where it was, on the outcome. Epictetus moved the heart as well, which is a far bigger operation.12

The second break is subtler, and it is the one that explains the two in the morning. Let the remark in the meeting land on two different people. The first person uses the tools. They tell themselves not to let it get to them. They breathe, they keep their face still, they reframe the remark as the colleague's problem. And underneath, they still believe exactly what they believed at the moment it was said. They were made smaller in front of people who matter, and that is a real harm. The second person does something that looks the same from the outside. The difference is that they no longer believe the remark harmed them. It said something false about their work, or something true, and in either case nothing good was taken from them.

In the meeting, the two people look identical. Both are calm, and both say nothing sharp in reply. But only one of them sleeps that night. The first person is holding a feeling down while keeping the belief that produces it, and the belief keeps producing it. The second person has changed the belief, so there is much less left to hold down. The tools, used without the belief they were built to change, can turn people into the first person.

The third break is the word itself, and how far it has drifted. In ordinary English, stoic means someone who keeps a straight face, who does not cry at the funeral, who grits their teeth through pain. That is very nearly the opposite of the school.13 The Stoics did not tell anyone to hold a feeling in. They held that the feelings that wreck us are mistakes of judgement, and that a mistake is corrected, not hidden. A person who is furious inside and calm on the surface was, by their lights, simply a furious person with good manners. And they never wanted a person with no feelings at all.

So the strong version of the modern view can now be sorted. What is kept: the tools are real, they come from the ancient texts, and they help. What is discarded: the idea that the system behind them is optional packaging. The tools were not invented separately and then wrapped in a philosophy. They were drawn out of a few claims, one at a time, and they hold up best where those claims are believed. Cut the claims away and, on this argument, the tools tend to work for a day, which is what the poster delivers.

Rehearsing losses is the clearest case. The modern reason for it is a kind of inoculation: a small dose of imagined loss so that the real loss stings less. The Stoics gave that reason too, and then they added a second one. It was to correct a belief, the belief that the thing you were about to lose had ever been yours to keep. Picturing the loss of your health, on the first reading, is preparing for bad news. On the second, it is reminding yourself that your health was always on loan, so that its return to the lender is not a theft.14 Same exercise, different claim underneath, and only the second one reaches past the first shock.

So the poster version kept the method and dropped the claim, and every one of its failures traces back to that. It leaves the outcome in charge of your peace, lets reframing slide into holding feelings down, and confuses the school with a stiff face. The missing piece is a claim about what is actually good. Somehow a lost job, a lost reputation or a failing body has to take nothing good away, and the Stoics thought they could prove it.

Part 2: The Only Good Thing

The claim at the root of Stoicism is an argument, and it came from Socrates. Money, health, beauty and power can each be used well or badly. Something that can be used badly cannot be good in itself, since in the wrong hands it does harm.15 So the only thing that is good in itself is the wisdom that uses all the rest well.16 Everything else in Stoicism grows out of that one inference.

The argument is easier to feel on a case than to state, and money is the easiest case. Give a large sum to a compulsive gambler, and within a year it has wrecked his marriage, his health and his friendships. Give the same sum to a careful builder, and within a year there is a house, a business and three people employed. The money did not change. What changed was the person using it. So whatever was good in the second case was not in the money. It was in the builder's judgement, and the money only carried it.

Strength works the same way. A strong man uses his strength to bully the people around him. Another strong man uses exactly the same strength to pull a stranger from a canal. Again the strength is identical, and again the difference lies entirely in what the person does with it. Whatever is good in having money or strength turns out, on inspection, to be borrowed from the person who handles it.

Socrates ran this test, in a dialogue his student wrote down, on the whole list of things people think make a life go well. Wealth, health, good looks, power, honour, even courage when it is not guided. Each of them, he argued, does good only when knowledge directs it. In the hands of someone who does not know how to use it, each does harm.19

The Stoics sharpened this into a definition. A thing is good, they said, only if it always benefits and never harms. By that standard, money fails at once, because money has ruined people. Health fails too, since a healthy tyrant does more damage than a sick one. What passes the test is only the skill of using things well, which is what they called virtue. And virtue cannot be misused, because a misuse of it would simply not be virtue. A doctor who uses medicine to poison is not practising medicine badly, because he has stopped practising it at all.

Laid out as plain steps, the argument runs like this. One. A thing is good only if it benefits in every use. Two. Money, health, status and the rest can each be used to do harm. Three. So none of them is good. Four. The skill of using things well cannot be used to do harm, because using it is doing well. Five. So that skill, the quality of a person's judgement and character, is the only good there is.

That is the claim the poster left out, and it changes what happiness means. For the Stoics, happiness was not a feeling and not a run of good luck. They described it as a smooth flow of life, a life going right in the one way a life can go right, by being lived well.20 The gambler with a fortune is not happy, however it feels on the night he wins. The builder is, whether or not the house survives the next flood.

Part 3: Preferred, Not Good

Two doctors make the split easy to feel. Each has a patient with the same illness. Each examines carefully, orders the right tests, chooses the right treatment and watches through the night. One patient recovers and goes home within the week. The other dies, from a hidden condition no test of the time could have found. Now ask which of the two doctors did the better job. So the Stoic goes to the doctor, and not grudgingly. Health has real value, a value worth acting on. What it lacks is goodness, in the strict sense from the use argument. Part 4: Living According to Nature

The preferences come from nature, and so does the good, but at opposite ends of a single human life. Every animal starts out caring for its own body, and that first care is where health, food and safety get their value. A human being keeps growing. It comes to care about reason itself, and about other people far beyond itself, and that growth, finished, is what the Stoics meant by virtue. To live according to nature is to complete it.

The Stoics began their account with a newborn. Nobody teaches a baby to want air, warmth and milk. Nobody teaches a toddler to keep standing up after every fall. Seneca watched children learning to walk, and noted that they fall, cry and get up again, over and over. Nobody has explained to them why walking is worth it.36 The Stoics read a claim off that scene. The first thing every animal is drawn to is not pleasure. It is its own body and its own survival.37 Pleasure comes along afterwards, as a sign that something went right, the way a plant looks fresh after it has been watered.

Part 5: The Gap Between Seeing and Agreeing

Every thought you have arrives first as an appearance, a way things seem. Nothing becomes your belief until you agree to it, and agreeing is a second act, separate from the seeming. The Stoics put the whole of human freedom in the gap between those two acts. That gap is small, and most people never notice it, but it is the place where the quality of your judgement is made or lost.

Slow the meeting down to see it. The colleague speaks. A moment later something arrives in your mind, fully formed: he has just humiliated me in front of everyone. It does not feel like a thought you are having. It feels like seeing, with the same force as seeing that the table is brown. But it is not the same kind of thing as the brown table. Packed inside it are several claims. That the remark was about you, which is probably true. That it lowered you in the eyes of others, which may or may not be true. And that being lowered in their eyes is a harm to you, which on the Stoic account is false. All of that arrived as one picture, and the picture asked for one thing: your agreement.

The Stoics called the arriving picture an impression, and they called the agreement assent. An impression is how things seem to you, and you do not choose it. Assent is your yes to it, and that, they said, is up to you. A belief is an impression you have said yes to.51 So every belief you hold, every one, passed through a moment of agreement.

Seneca wrote a whole treatise on one passion, anger, and his case against it is the strongest form of the theory. The usual defence of anger is that it is useful. Reason wants to reach a just verdict, he wrote, while anger wants whatever verdict it has already reached to look just.69 He gave a case. The replay feels like work, and behind it sits a theory most people hold without ever saying it. Anger, on this theory, is a pressure. It builds up inside, and letting it out brings the level down. That is why people say it helps to get it off your chest, or to go over it until it has been dealt with. The person lying awake, rewriting the reply to the colleague, is following that theory. Each pass through the scene, they believe, uses up a little of the heat. Seneca held the opposite. On the two-stage account, every replay is a new agreement to "he harmed me", so there is nothing stored to let out.

The two theories make opposite predictions, which means they can be tested. One experiment set up the test almost exactly. Students wrote a short essay, and a partner they never met sent back a harsh verdict on it. Then they were split three ways. One group hit a punching bag while thinking about the partner who had insulted them. A second group hit the same bag while thinking about getting fit. A third group sat quietly and hit nothing. Afterwards everyone rated their anger, and then got the chance to blast the partner with loud noise.97 The people who hit the bag while picturing the insulter should have come out calmest, with the heat used up. They came out the angriest of the three groups, and the most aggressive toward the partner. The people who did nothing at all came out best. The researcher's own summary was that venting to reduce anger is like using gasoline to put out a fire.98

The argument, laid out plainly, runs in five steps. One: a replay is a fresh agreement to the verdict that you were harmed. Part 9: What Is Up to You

Epictetus drew the line of what is up to us so tightly that almost nothing is inside it. Your judgements, your impulses to act, what you desire and what you refuse. That is the whole list. Your body is outside it, your property, your reputation, your position and the outcome of everything you try. The narrowness is the theory's best idea, because it marks the one region where your responsibility can be complete.

The man who drew the line knew from the inside what it meant to own very little. This is the map of the self that the rest depends on. Your agreements, impulses, desires and refusals are yours, and everything else, including the body, belongs to the world. A deeper worry sits underneath it. The outcome is not up to me, and everything in the world, including my character, has a cause. Then it becomes unclear whether anything is up to me at all.

Part 10: The Cylinder and the Dog

The Stoics believed that everything that happens is fixed by an unbroken chain of causes, and they still held people fully responsible for what they do. Their way of joining the two was that a cause which works through your character is still yours. That answer has held up remarkably well. The best reply uses nothing the Stoics did not already have. A cylinder cannot change its shape. Whatever made it a cylinder made it one for good, and every push leaves it exactly as it was. That changes what responsibility needs. The critics assumed that for the rolling to be yours, the shape had to come from nowhere. No shape comes from nowhere, and none has to. Responsibility needs a shape that answers to reasons, and that its owner can rework over time. Part 11: A Universe That Thinks

Stoic physics made the whole universe a single living body with a mind, and that mind arranges everything for the best. It held the ethics up, because it turned acceptance into agreement: if the universe is reason itself, then what happens is what a perfect reason chose. That picture is the part of Stoicism that does not survive, and its loss changes what acceptance can mean.

The founders said so themselves. Chrysippus wrote that there is no better or more fitting way to approach the theory of good and bad than from the nature of the universe.138 The modern habit of keeping the ethics and throwing away the physics is a choice the founders would have rejected. So the physics has to be looked at squarely, because the ethics was built to stand on it.

Part 12: Providence or Atoms

Marcus Aurelius believed in a universe ordered by reason, and he kept testing that belief against its opposite: a universe of atoms falling together by chance. Again and again in his private notebook, he asked what he should do if the opposite were true. His answer was that one thing held either way: his own mind was still his to steer. That is the live modern question about Stoicism, and his answer mostly holds. What does not hold is the comfort he took from the first option.

The notebook was never meant to be read. It is written in Greek, by a Roman emperor, mostly during years of war on the northern frontier of the empire. At the end of one book he noted where he was: in an army camp beside a river, in what is now central Europe. Later readers called it the Meditations, but the old title is closer to its nature: to himself.155 What it contains is a man repeating the same few thoughts to himself, in new words, because he kept forgetting them. That is what makes it useful. It shows what Stoicism looked like as a daily practice, under pressure, in a person who was not a sage and knew it.

That last clause is the one that matters most. The advice changes from branch to branch, and so does the comfort. In the branch with a god, everything is well. In the third, nothing is well, and you have only your own steering in the storm. What stays the same in every branch is the steering mind itself.

This is exactly the question modern Stoics face. The founders said the ethics should start from the universe. The question that matters for anyone deciding how to live is whether it can stand without it, and it can be answered piece by piece.

The use argument stands without the god. Money and health can be misused whether or not the universe has a mind. The theory of emotion stands. The jolt and the verdict are claims about human beings, and what the laboratory has found sits close to them with no help from providence. The narrow list stands. Nothing outside you can force an agreement in a universe of atoms, any more than in a universe of reason.

The ought question is only half answered. Without the god, there is still an argument for valuing your judgement above everything else. It is the use argument, run once more. Everything except your judgement can be taken from you, and can be misused even while you have it. A life that stakes its peace on those things has staked it on things that can be taken back. But that argument tells you to value your own judgement. It does not tell you why to widen your circles to everyone. Human beings are social animals, built to depend on each other, and that is a fact about how we are made.165 Whether that fact makes a duty is the ought question again, and biology alone does not answer it. So concern for others survives as a fact about how we are made. The duty built on it has lost the warrant the Stoics gave it.

Part 13: The Grain Ship

A duty to others that has lost the god behind it can still decide a hard case, and it can do so honestly. The test is an old one about a grain merchant who knows something his buyers do not. The Stoic claim that only a person's judgement is good does not tell the merchant what to do. What it does is take away the pull of the money, so that only the question of what he owes is left. That question is answered by a narrower rule: a seller may not keep people ignorant, for his own profit, of what is their business to know. The rule stands without the god, it is weaker than what the Stoics had, and it does not ask anyone to give everything away.

Start with the meeting, a week later. You find out that the figure the colleague's next report rests on is wrong. He does not know, and in three days he will present it to the people who decide promotions. You are both up for the same promotion. You would never lie to him, and nobody is asking you to. The question is smaller and harder than that: whether you have to say anything at all. A duty to others that has lost its warrant has to say something here, or it says nothing anywhere.

Cicero set the ancient version of this case, and he built it so that nothing could be blamed on a cheat. A good man, he says, brings a large cargo of grain from Alexandria to the island of Rhodes. There is a famine on the island, and grain is selling at the highest price anyone has seen. He knows that several other grain ships have left Alexandria and are already on their way. Must he tell the buyers, or may he keep quiet and sell his grain for as much as it will fetch.166 Cicero insists the merchant is a good man, so the question is what a decent person owes. And two Stoics, a teacher and his own student, gave opposite answers. They held the same theory of value and still split on a grain sale.167

Now the objection, and it is aimed at the whole school. The claim that the right and the profitable never conflict wins by definition. What, then, holds the narrow premise up, now that the universe has stopped thinking. The student's own reason was nature's fellowship, and that reason is gone. The reason that is left is plainer. The buyer you keep in the dark is you in the other seat. If a seller let you overpay for bread while ships he knew about sailed toward your harbour, you would call it taking. That is all the duty now stands on, once the god is gone. It leaves the ought question where it was, and nothing here pretends to bring back the warrant the god once gave. Its job is smaller: to hold a person who already counts other people to his own standard.

Here is the whole argument as plain steps. One. Your judgement is the highest good, and no profit outweighs it. That is the Stoic premise kept here, and minds already treat some things as beyond any sum. Two. So the profit cannot weigh against a wrong, and the only question left is whether the silence is a wrong. Three. A silence is a wrong when it keeps people ignorant, for your profit, of something that is their business to know. Four. The duty, weakened as it is, does not license the demand to give everything away, because it was a duty not to take from the start. That is less than the Stoics believed they had, and a good deal more than nothing. Tell the colleague, then, and do it for the reason the ring gives, which has nothing to do with how it will look later. He is the same colleague the morning rehearsal said you were made to work with, like the rows of upper and lower teeth.

So the core claim does its work in a market. It keeps the money from outweighing a wrong, and a duty not to take decides the rest. But a Stoic teacher and his own student, holding the same doctrine, could not agree on a single grain sale. A practical question remains, and the ancient Stoics asked it of themselves. Even if the core survives, it is not clear that anyone has ever actually lived it.

Part 14: The Sage Who Never Lived

The Stoics defined the good person so strictly that nobody, including their own founders, ever qualified. They called that person the sage, and admitted that a sage might appear once in many centuries, if ever.177 Alongside that ideal they kept an ethics anyone could use, about doing what fits your situation and your nature as well as you can. That second ethics is the one a living person can actually use. The all-or-nothing scoring of the first is the part to throw away.

So the sage works as a direction. It points at a single, fully sound judgement that nothing can shake, and it is useful to know where that point is. The all-or-nothing scoring is the part to discard. It turns the ideal into a reproach and makes every real improvement invisible. The school itself, with its appropriate acts and its four roles, had built something more usable beside it. The sage never lived, the Stoics knew it, and their usable ethics asks you to be the best of who you are. Even its most famous writer fell short of it in public, and admitted he was no sage. That usable ethics carries one exercise nearly everyone has heard of, picturing a loss before it comes, and whether it works can be checked.

Part 15: The Loss Rehearsed

Picturing a loss before it comes does soften the blow, but only one of the two ways of doing it is worth keeping. If the event is pictured and the verdict rehearsed, the Stoics claimed, the shock arrives smaller, and nothing measured here contradicts them. If the event is pictured and the distress rehearsed, that is worry. In people who worry chronically, worry also buys a smaller shock, and on its researchers' account it pays with days of dread. Neither version does anything to the loss itself.

Rehearsal is the Stoic exercise the poster version keeps beside the line about control, and the obvious question about it is rarely asked. Does going through a loss in your head make the real one hurt less. The ancient schools fought over exactly that, and the best of the fight survives in Cicero's dialogue on distress. Since then the question has been measured from several sides, and the results are not all friendly.

The claim that started the fight was not Stoic. A rival school held that distress is not produced by every evil, only by an evil nobody saw coming.193 Cicero tells of a philosopher who heard that his son had died, and answered: I knew I had fathered a mortal.194 The news could not ambush a man who had already said it to himself. Cicero puts the rule in one line: this rehearsal of future evils softens their coming.195 If the rival school was right, rehearsal is the whole cure, and the only task is to leave nothing unexpected.

It leaves alone the thing nothing here shows foresight can change, which is the loss. Part of the old inoculation reason survives, the smaller shock, but only the verdict form aims at it without the dread.

None of this is advice to picture the worst more often. One pass through each picture is the exercise, however often the picture comes up. Staying inside the picture and going round it again is worry with a Stoic label on it.

Set out as plain steps, the argument runs like this. One: a passion is a verdict, and part of its force is the belief that the loss is fresh. Two: picturing an event in advance lets the verdict be examined early, so it lands already old. Three: rehearsing the distress instead keeps a bad state running, which shrinks the shock and stretches the dread. Four: the picture of a future loss is roughly right about the sting and wrong about how long it will rule a life. Five: so the verdict is rehearsed in one pass, and the exercise stops there, aiming at the shock and the reach, never the loss.

Before the next meeting with the colleague, the exercise takes about a minute. The remark is pictured arriving. Then the verdict is said once: my work is mine, and I would prefer his opinion of it to be kinder. Then turn to something else. Going over it again is the replay at two in the morning run in advance, and the replay makes the feeling again each time it plays.

So one form of rehearsal survives, untested but unrefuted, and the other fails, and the form that survives is cheap: one picture, one verdict, then stop. It is easy to defend on a job and a remark. Epictetus did not stop there, and he carried the same rehearsal to the moment of kissing a child. The hardest objection to all of this is about love, and about what losing a person you love can do to a life.

Part 17: The Grief That Is Owed

Grief for someone you love stays, and the Stoics' own best practice, looked at closely, never argued with it while it was fresh. What can go is a belief that rides on top of it: that heavy and lasting grief is owed, as proof of love. That belief makes predictions about love and about later collapse, and checked against what happens to people who lose someone, the predictions fail. Whether some grief fits a real loss is a separate question, and the answer already given was yes. Either way, nobody should be argued out of the belief that grief is owed while the loss is fresh. Cicero, who recorded that rule, broke it himself.

Two places are left to look for how full love and calm at a loss could fit together. One is what the Stoics did when they sat beside a grieving person. The other is what happens to grieving people, which can be measured.

As plain steps, the argument runs in four. One: grief holds the pain a real loss brings and, often, a further belief that heavy grief is owed. Two: that further belief makes predictions about love and about delayed collapse. Three: followed from before the loss, those predictions failed, while the first grief appeared in everyone. Four: so the first grief stays, the owed grief can be put down, and argument waits until the shock has passed.

Beside a grieving friend, that means saying nothing about whether the loss was bad, and arguing nothing while it is fresh. Later, if they believe their grief has to prove something, that is the belief worth gently questioning. For oneself it means that feeling less than expected is not a failure of love, and feeling more is not a failure of philosophy. With the guarantee gone and the price admitted, one question remains. What is actually left to do tomorrow morning, when the first impression arrives.

Part 18: What Survives

What survives of Stoicism is a discipline, not a consolation. Test each impression before you agree to it. Treat every outcome as something preferred, never something owed. Do your work for the people around you, whatever the universe turns out to be. That is smaller than what the ancient school promised, and larger than what the poster delivers.

The whole system can be sorted in a few sentences. Kept: the use argument, the gap between impression and agreement, the narrow list, and the cylinder. Revised: the theory of emotion, which now has a jolt before the verdict. Revised: anger, which has one measured use, though not at two in the morning, and whose work a plain verdict can do. Revised as well: rehearsal, kept as rehearsing the verdict and dropped as rehearsing the distress. Revised too: the split between the good and the preferred, since your judgement becomes the highest and only secure good, not the only one. Weakened: the duty to everyone, which survives without the universe that grounded it. It still decides the hard case: do not keep people ignorant, for your own profit, of what is their business to know. Discarded: the thinking universe, the welcome of whatever happens as part of a perfect order, and the all-or-nothing sage. Discarded as well: the claim that losing those you love cannot make your life worse. And discarded with evidence against it: the belief that heavy grief is owed, that love must be proved by being crushed.

The poster version was right to drop one thing, and wrong to drop another. It threw away the thinking universe, and that really can go. But it also threw away the claim about value, and treated the tools as if they stood on their own. Reframing works in the laboratory on ordinary students who were never taught any Stoic belief.255 What the claims add, on this argument, though nothing has measured it, is staying power. A reframe that leaves the outcome as your good holds only until the next outcome, and then the verdict has to be won again.

What survives of Stoicism is one hard claim. The quality of your judgement is the good that matters most, and the one nothing outside you can take. Its account of emotion holds, revised into a jolt and then a verdict. Its caring universe does not hold, and acceptance shrinks to refusing the second injury. Its strictest claim, that losing those you love cannot make your life worse, goes too far. The strict claim carried a price, a love held a little at arm's length. It follows from what the ancient Stoics said out loud, though they never called it a price, and the poster never mentions it. What survives asks for less: keep the love, and refuse only the verdicts piled on top of the loss.

Tomorrow, sometime in the first hour, an impression will arrive with the force of seeing. Someone will say something, or fail to, and a verdict will be standing there, fully formed, asking only for your agreement. What this philosophy leaves you is the second after that, and what you do with it.
