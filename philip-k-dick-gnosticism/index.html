Cold open

From inside a world, no test can prove that it is real, or catch a fake that is made well and never slips. No glitch can settle it, and no vision either, however strong. Philip K. Dick spent his writing life asking how a person could tell a fake world from a real one. Then, in 1974, he had an experience he believed had answered it. He spent his last eight years failing to show, even to himself, that it had. What his work found instead is smaller and harder to dodge. The fake that matters is not the fake world but the fake person, the human being turned into a tool. And the one test nobody else can run for you is whether you treat the person in front of you as real.

Here is what happened to him. In February 1974, groggy from a dental operation, he opened his door to a young woman delivering a prescription.1 She wore a small gold fish on a chain.2 When she told him what it meant, something hit him that felt like memory and not like news.3 A few weeks later, as he told it, a beam of pink light poured information into his head. Most of it was about the world and what it really was.4 One piece was about his baby son's health, and that piece could be checked.5

Every test Dick tried for telling a fake world from a real one fails. That includes his best definition, used as a test, and it includes his own vision. The failures are not random. Each one fails for a reason. Put side by side, the reasons point away from the question he asked and toward the one he said mattered more. That question is what makes a human being real, and what turns one into a fake. It has an answer you can check every day, from the inside, without a vision and without a doctor.

Part 1: The Soft-Drink Stand

A fake world is never caught by how it looks. It is caught, if at all, at its seams, the places where it shows it was made. And from inside, every seam can be explained away, because the cheapest explanation is always available: something is wrong with you. That is the first thing Dick worked out about fake worlds, fifteen years before the pink light. He worked it out in a novel about a small town. The choice of a small town, and not a dream or a spaceship, is part of the argument.

The novel is called Time Out of Joint, and it came out in 1959. Its hero is a man named Ragle Gumm.13 He lives with his sister and her husband in a quiet American town, in a house with a lawn, on a street where everyone knows everyone. He has no ordinary job. He makes his living from a puzzle in the local newspaper, a contest called "Where Will the Little Green Man Be Next?". Every day the paper prints a grid, and readers guess which square a little green man will turn up in next. Ragle guesses right almost every day. The paper calls him its grand all-time winner, and he mails off his entry every afternoon like a man clocking in.14

Hold that slip of paper for a moment, because the whole problem is folded into it.

Suppose it happened to you. You walk up to a newspaper kiosk, and the kiosk thins out into nothing, and in its place lies a slip that says NEWSPAPER KIOSK. What would you do next? Most people, asked honestly, give one of three answers. I was tired and I saw something that was not there. Someone is playing a trick on me. Or, the quiet one, the one that comes at night: I am starting to lose my mind. Almost nobody answers that the world was built and the builders made a mistake. That answer is available, and it is the last one anyone reaches for.

Ragle reaches for it last as well. He turns away from it. His first suspicion falls on himself. He wonders whether the strain of winning the contest every single day has cracked something in him.16 And that is exactly the right thing for a reasonable man to wonder, which is why the novel works.

This is the first thing the slip teaches. It has two explanations, and both of them predict the slip. One says the world is fake, and the builders slipped. The other says the world is fine, and the man looking at it is breaking. Each explanation accounts for every fact Ragle has. The paper is in his hand either way. The stand is gone either way. So the slip, by itself, cannot tell him which one is true.

Call that the seam. A seam is any place where a world shows that it was made. It might be a stand that turns into its own label, or a coincidence too neat to be chance. What the slip shows is that a seam always has a second reading. It can be the world's mistake, or it can be yours. And from inside, there is no third place to stand and compare them.

Dick then piles up the seams, and the way he piles them is worth following, because it shows what more evidence does and does not do. His brother-in-law, in a bathroom he has used for years, reaches up in the dark for a light cord that is not there.17 His hand knows a house he does not remember living in. Ragle finds an old magazine with a film star named Marilyn Monroe on the cover. Nobody in the town has ever heard of her.18 The children's homemade radio picks up voices that sound like pilots, and the voices are talking about Ragle Gumm.19 Each seam is stranger than the last. And each one, taken alone, still has its second reading. A man reaching for a cord that is not there has a habit from a childhood house. A forgotten actress is a gap in a small town's memory. Voices on a cheap radio saying your name sound exactly like what a man under strain would hear.

So the evidence grows, and for Ragle's own seams the deadlock does not break. A man who is cracking would see more and more strange things, and so would a man in a built world. One kind of seam does tip the scale, and the novel uses it. A brother-in-law's hand reaching for a missing cord is not Ragle's breakdown. A seam that other people share is how a private crack is told from a public fact. But shared seams still only catch a builder's mistake. A fake made well never slips, and then there is nothing to share.

The fake has a purpose, and in this novel the purpose is the work of the person inside it. Ragle's comfortable life is not a cage he notices. It is a desk he does not know he is sitting at. The builders of the town did not need to make their work perfect. They only needed a man who would rather doubt himself than doubt the street he lives on. Nearly everyone is that man.

Notice how Ragle actually gets out, because it is not by reasoning. No amount of thinking about the slip could have settled the question. He gets out by trying to leave. Keep that in view. The seam could not tell Ragle the truth, but what the world did when he acted could.

There is an obvious objection. This is a novel. Real towns do not dissolve into labels. Real people do not find slips of paper where a soft-drink stand used to be. A writer can make a world fake by deciding that it is, and then hand his character the clues. Nothing follows about the world the reader lives in. That objection is right about the slip and wrong about the structure. The point of the slip is not that such slips exist. The point is the shape of the evidence a person inside a world could ever have.

Try it on the seams real people actually report. A coincidence so precise it feels arranged. The sudden strong sense that you have lived this exact moment before. A stretch of days when the news, the feeds and the people around you all seem to be performing a script you were not given. A computer glitch that seems to show the scaffolding behind the screen. Every one of these has the shape of the slip. It can be read as the world showing its construction. It can be read as the mind misfiring, tired or anxious or primed to see patterns. And nothing inside the experience itself chooses between the readings.

So the novel is not a claim that the world is fake. It is a demonstration of what it would be like if it were, and the demonstration has a hard result. It would feel almost exactly like this. A few seams, each explainable. A growing unease that fits a failing mind just as well as a failing world. The fake is never caught by its surface, because its surface is built for exactly the person looking at it.

Step by step, the argument so far runs like this. First, a fake world, if one existed, would be built around the people in it for some use. Second, a fake world is detectable only at its seams. Third, every seam has two readings, the world's error or the observer's. Fourth, from inside, both readings predict the same private evidence, and shared seams catch only a builder's slip. So, fifth, a person inside a world cannot prove by looking that it is real, or catch a fake that never slips. The only thing that broke the deadlock for Ragle was acting against the world, by leaving it. Even that worked only because his world was badly made.

This is also, already, the shape of Dick's own life after 1974. When the light came and the fish pendant meant more than a pendant should, he faced Ragle's exact choice. Either the world had shown him its seam, or his mind was breaking. He had written that choice fifteen years earlier, and he knew there was no way to settle it by looking. It did not make the choice any easier when it was his turn.

The result is simple to say. A seam proves nothing to a person who wants an explanation, and an explanation is always available, the cheapest one being that the problem is you. Looking at the world harder will not tell you whether it is fake. Dick knew that waiting for a seam was a poor test. So he went looking for a better one, a test you could run on purpose instead of waiting for a stand to dissolve. He found one, and it is the best one anyone has given. The question is whether it works on the kind of fake that Ragle Gumm was living in.

Part 2: When You Stop Believing

Dick's best test for reality fits in one sentence. Reality is that which, when you stop believing in it, doesn't go away.22 It is a good test, better than most philosophers have offered, and it does real work. It catches the illusions a mind makes for itself. And it fails, precisely and for a clear reason, on the fakes Dick cared about most, which are the ones other people build and share.

The first thing to see is why it beats waiting for a seam. A seam is something that happens to you. Ragle Gumm had to wait for a soft-drink stand to dissolve before he had anything to think about. Dick's sentence is a test you can run on purpose. Anything you are unsure of can be put to it: withdraw your belief, and see what the thing does. If it was being held up by your believing, it falls. If it was being held up by something else, it stays where it was.

On the cases it was built for, it works cleanly. A dream is the easy one. While you are in it, the dream is utterly convincing, and the moment you wake and stop believing in it, it is gone. A night-time terror that the shape in the corner is a person goes the same way. With the light on and the belief gone, the shape is a coat on a chair. A phone you were sure you heard ringing in the shower turns out, when you go and look, never to have rung. In each case the thing depended on you, and the test exposed the dependence. It sorted out what the mind was supplying from what the world was supplying.

The opposite cases work just as cleanly. A wall does not care what you think about it. A person who stops believing in it and walks at it still meets it, and keeps the bruise. The weather, a tumour, a bill that has fallen due, none of these goes away when belief is withdrawn. That is exactly what makes them real in the ordinary, working sense. They push back.

So the test is honest. It answers a real question with a clear method, and for private illusions it is very hard to beat. The test is right about what it is right about. He offered it as a definition, not as a detector of frauds, and as a definition it holds. It just turns out to be right about the wrong kind of fake.

The test can be carried back to the town Ragle Gumm lived in. Suppose Ragle stops believing in the town. Does it go away? It does not. The houses are made of real wood. The neighbours are real people, doing a real job. The newspaper is printed on real paper and lands on the real porch every morning. By Dick's own test, the fake town is real.

That is the problem, and it is not a small one. A built fake is made of real things. What makes it fake is not the wood or the people but what they are arranged to make you believe, and why. The town was built to make Ragle believe it was 1959 and that his work was a game. His disbelief does not dissolve the wood. It only changes what he thinks the wood is for. And the test has nothing to say about what things are for.

The other way round, the point gets sharper. A well-made fake is exactly the one that does not go away when you stop believing in it. That is what it means for a fake to be well made. A poor fake, like a soft-drink stand that dissolves into a label, fails Dick's test and gets caught. A good fake passes it, because a good fake is built out of the same stubborn material as everything else. So the test catches the builders' mistakes and lets their successes through.

Dick knew this better than anyone, and he put it at the centre of the strangest novel he wrote in the 1960s. In the 1978 speech he said, with some cheerfulness, that he liked to build universes which do fall apart. He wanted to watch what his characters did when the ground went.24 The universe that falls apart most thoroughly is the one in Ubik, and it is the one where his test is no use at all.

There is a second kind of case where the test fails, and it needs no science fiction at all. It is the fake that many people share. A bank note is the plainest case. If you alone stop believing that it is worth anything, nothing happens. The shop still takes it, your rent is still owed in it, and its value does not move. The same holds for a border between two countries, a brand, a reputation, or a rumour that has run through a whole town. None of these goes away when one person withdraws belief, because each is held up by the belief of everyone else. The test, run by a single person, counts all of them as real. The bank note is honest, worth what everyone agrees it is worth. The rumour may be a lie that a whole town has agreed to live inside. The test cannot tell which is which.

The weakness belongs to any test a single person can run from inside, and Dick's sentence only makes it plain. Withdrawing belief is a private act. It can only expose what was being propped up privately. Anything propped up by machinery, by other people's labour, or by other people's belief survives it, whether it is honest or not.

That ruling decides a great deal about the rest of Dick's life. It says that the fakes his test can catch are the ones inside one head. And it says that the fakes it cannot catch are the ones somebody else made. Those are precisely the fakes Dick spent his career warning about. A dream does not need a warning. It ends by itself every morning. A built world does need one, because it does not end, and because the person inside is often doing its work.

The test's best defence says this: whatever survives my doubt is real enough to live in, and I have no better option. That much stands. If the stale cigarettes are what there is, Joe Chip still has to decide what to smoke. What falls is the hope that the test could ever expose a fake that was made well. It cannot, and a test that only catches bad fakes is no protection from good ones.

The upshot fits in two lines. What doesn't go away is real in the sense that it is not in your head. It may still be a fake in the sense that matters, because someone made it to be believed, and made it well. That moves the question somewhere new. If the fakes worth worrying about are built, the next thing to ask is who builds them. After that comes what they do to the people who live inside them.

Part 3: Fake Realities Make Fake Humans

Dick's real worry was never that the universe might be a dream. It was that realities are manufactured every day, by people with the power to do it. And living inside a manufactured reality does something to a person. It turns them, slowly and usually without their noticing, into an instrument. That is the step that joins his two great questions into one. What is real turns out to matter because of what it does to what is human.

He named the two questions himself, in the same 1978 speech. The two topics that fascinated him, he said, were what is reality, and what constitutes the authentic human being. For most of the speech they look like separate subjects, one for philosophers and one for moralists. Then, partway through, he joins them. In his words, my two topics are really one topic; they unite at this point.30 The point where they unite is the manufactured world.

Part 4: The Blind Creator

There is an old myth that says, almost line for line, what Dick's novels had been saying. This world was made by a lesser power that does not know it is lesser. Something better than this world is trapped inside the people in it. And the way out is not obedience or effort but waking up, and waking up turns out to be a kind of remembering. When Dick later tried to explain what happened to him in 1974, this was the myth he reached for again and again. So it has to be told properly, and told as the people who believed it would have wanted it told.

The myth's pull is real, and it explains why the story keeps coming back. It does three things that most accounts of the world cannot do at once. First, it explains suffering without blaming a good God. If the world is full of cruelty, disease and waste, the myth has a clean answer. And that is much closer to the manufacturers of the 1978 speech, whom Dick called victims of their own product. A newsroom that believes its ten stories are the day's news is a small blind creator. So is a company that believes its own advertisements, or a party that believes its own story about its rivals. So the myth gives Dick something the novels alone did not. In the novels, the fake was built by someone in particular, and the question was who. In the myth, the fake is the whole visible world, built by a maker who cannot see past it. That raises the stakes as high as they go. It is no longer a town that might be fake, or a drug, or a news story. It is everything you can see.

It also changes what waking up would be. In the novels, waking up means finding the edge of the built thing and walking through it. In the myth, there is no edge to walk to, because the built thing is the whole world. Waking up has to happen inside. It has to be an inner event, a recognition, that comes from above the creator's sight. It comes the way the call comes down in the hymn. No test run inside the world can produce it, because every test inside the world is made of the creator's materials.

Dick's test, recall, could not catch a well-made fake from inside. The myth agrees, and goes one further. It says the only thing that could ever catch the fake is something from outside it altogether. That might be a light, or a voice, or a knowledge from beyond. If you have never had such a thing, the myth says, you are still asleep. If you have, you know.

That is the myth, at its strongest. It explains the fake world, the fake human and the way out, all in one story. And it adds one thing that will matter enormously. It says that the proof of the fake is not a test but an experience, a moment when the spark remembers. For most readers, the myth stays a story about other people. Dick had written novels with its shape, and never expected to be in it. Then, in February 1974, a young woman came to his door with a gold fish on a chain, and he believed that he had remembered.

Part 6: Eight Thousand Pages

The journal Dick kept after 1974 is the record of a man finding out, the hard way, that an experience cannot certify its own source. He built explanation after explanation for what had happened to him. Every one of them fitted. And that is exactly why none of them won. Eight years of honest work produced no verdict, and the absence of a verdict is itself the most important finding in the pile.

He called the journal his Exegesis, an old word for the close interpretation of a sacred text. The text he was interpreting was his own experience. Part 7: The Diagnosis

A diagnosis can explain how an experience was produced. It cannot, on its own, rule on whether what the experience said was true. For Dick, the honest ruling is this. Two-three-seventy-four was real as an experience, as real as anything that ever happened to him. And it was no proof about the cosmos, either way. The doctor cannot take it away from him, and it cannot be handed on to anyone else.

Most people, hearing the story of the fish, the light and the dead Christian, reach for a diagnosis almost at once. It is a good guess. The guess runs like this. Experiences like Dick's have been studied. There are known conditions of the brain and mind that produce visions, voices, floods of meaning and the certainty of revelation. Once the right condition is named, the mystery is over. A neurologist settles it, and the Exegesis becomes a long, sad record of an illness.

Two candidate explanations have actually been put forward, and each deserves to be heard properly before the guess is tested.

The first comes from psychiatry. In 2003 a psychiatrist named Shitij Kapur proposed an account of how psychosis begins, which became one of the most discussed ideas in the field.75 His proposal was about salience, the quality that makes some things in your surroundings stand out and grab you. Normally, salience tracks what matters. Your own name in a crowded room stands out. A car coming at you stands out. The pattern on the carpet does not. Kapur's idea was that, in early psychosis, the brain's chemistry starts to hand out salience wrongly. Ordinary things start to stand out as if they mattered enormously. A stranger's glance, a number plate, a song on the radio, all seem charged, as if they were meant. And then, on his account, the person does something entirely natural. They try to explain why everything suddenly means so much. The delusion, in his words, is a cognitive effort by the patient to make sense of these aberrantly salient experiences.76

Part 8: Made Is Not Fake

One quiet inference runs under the popular reading of the Gnostic picture, and under every modern fear of living in a simulation. This world was made by someone else, so it is fake. The myth itself, read carefully, rests on more than that, on a blind maker and a world used to keep people asleep. But the bare inference is what most people hear in it, and the bare inference is false. A world that was made is still a real world, with real tables, real hands and real people in it. What can be fake is something narrower: a lie told inside it, an arrangement built to make someone believe something false about their situation. Once that is seen, the fake-world question turns into a question about persons.

The modern version of the fake-world question does not need a pink light. It needs a computer. And Dick, characteristically, said something close to it before most people had thought about it. In 1977 he gave a speech in Metz, in France, about the feeling of having lived a moment before. Such an impression, he said, is a clue that at some past time point a variable was changed, reprogrammed, as it were.84 He was picturing the world as a program, rewritten from outside.

Part 9: The Prison Built of Knowing

The Gnostic answer does not free anyone from a fake world. It builds a second one, inside the head, and seals it. In that second world the knower is awake and everyone else is asleep. Every piece of evidence against the belief is read as more evidence for it. That is what the answer costs, and the price is paid mostly by other people. They stop being persons in the believer's eyes and become sleepers, guards or scenery. The escape from the fake world ends by making fake humans.

Start with a case that has nothing to do with gods. A man becomes convinced that his phone is being monitored. He takes it to a technician, who examines it and finds nothing. The man is not reassured. He concludes that whoever is monitoring him is good enough to hide from technicians. He tries a second technician, who also finds nothing, and his confidence goes up again. A friend tells him gently that he seems stressed, and he wonders, for the first time, whose side the friend is on. Every check that comes back clean makes the belief stronger. There is no result that could count against it, because every result has already been given a place inside it.

Call that a sealed world. A sealed world is a belief arranged so that every piece of evidence against it counts as evidence for it. It is not the same thing as a strong belief or a strange one. A person can hold a very strange belief and still be ready to drop it. What makes a world sealed is its shape: nothing outside can get in.

Psychiatry defines its own central term by exactly this shape. The manual American psychiatrists use describes delusions as fixed beliefs that are not amenable to change in light of conflicting evidence. The definition turns on how the belief is held, not on what it says.92 A belief that the neighbours are spies, or that one has been chosen, can be held openly or held sealed. What marks the delusion is that conflicting evidence no longer moves it. That is worth hearing carefully, because it is not a verdict on Dick. He was never diagnosed with a delusion, and nobody is entitled to diagnose him now. The point is about the shape, and the shape can be found in perfectly healthy people holding perfectly ordinary opinions.

Part 10: God in the Gutter

Dick's most lasting idea gives the Gnostic myth its strangest turn. In the old myth, the saviour already slips down into the prison in disguise, so that the rulers do not recognise it. Dick's turn is the disguise itself. In Dick's best books, the real shows up inside the counterfeit world, in its cheapest and most worthless places, dressed as junk. And because it comes from the trash, where it came from can never tell you what it is. You know it only by what it does between people.

Part 11: The Test Nobody Can Run for You

Every test of whether someone else is truly human can be fooled or can misfire. Dick showed it with the most famous test he ever invented. There is one test that nobody else can run for you. It is the test a person runs on their own conduct. It asks whether they just treated the one in front of them as a person, or as a part. That is not a dodge of the fake-world question. After everything else has failed, it is the only form of the question that has an answer from inside.

The test is the one at the centre of the android novel. Its hero, Rick Deckard, is a bounty hunter. His job is to find androids who have escaped from the colonies and kill them, which the novel calls retiring them. The newest androids are almost impossible to tell from people. They look human, talk like humans and can pass most checks. So the police use a special test, and the test aims at the one thing the androids are supposed to lack.

It is called the Voigt-Kampff test, and it measures empathy. The examiner reads out short scenes, most of them involving cruelty, many involving animals. Meanwhile instruments watch the subject's face. What they measure, in the novel's words, is the so-called shame or blushing reaction to a morally shocking stimulus. The point of measuring a blush is that it can't be controlled voluntarily.113 A clever android can say the right words. It cannot, the theory goes, make its own skin flush at the thought of a boiled lobster.

Part 12: What Doesn't Go Away

The fake world cannot be found by looking up for a light, or by looking through for a seam. Each failed test left something behind. The soft-drink stand showed that a seam can always be explained away, so looking harder at the world will not settle whether it is fake. The belief test showed that what survives your doubt is real, but cannot tell an honest world from a well-made lie. The 1978 speech showed what the well-made lies are for. They turn people into instruments, into forgeries of themselves. The blind creator showed how the whole visible world could be read as such a lie. The fish at the door showed what it feels like to believe you have woken from it. The eight thousand pages showed that the feeling cannot vouch for its source. The diagnosis showed that knowing how the feeling was made does not tell you whether it was true. And the simulation showed that being made is not being fake.

Then the turn. The sealed world showed that the Gnostic escape turns other people into sleepers. That is the android move, made by the one who thinks he is awake. Mercer showed that a fake exposed to its roots can still be real in its fruits, when what it does is let people feel one another. The empathy test showed that every judgment of someone else's humanity is made from outside, where every fake works. And Angel shows the one thing left, a person deciding what to do for the person in front of her, without settling what is real first.

Dick's definition can be run one last time, on the one job it was always good at. Reality is that which, when you stop believing in it, doesn't go away. It could never tell an honest world from a well-made lie. What it can tell is what was being held up by someone's believing. Tim's search was held up by Tim's believing, and when the believing went into the desert, the desert took it. Bill is not held up by anyone's believing. He is still there, ill and alone, needing a room, whatever anyone believes. By Dick's definition, Bill is as real as anything is. Whether he is more than that, nobody in the novel can say, and Angel does not need to.

None of this answers the question that comes on a night when the world feels staged. Whether the feeds, the jobs and the performances are a built world, a simulation or simply the world, is left where Dick left it, open. What it changes is which question comes first. The question that cannot be answered can be put down without being answered. The one that can be answered is standing in front of you, and it is the same every time. It asks whether the one in front of you is a part in something, or a person. Dick could not decide what happened to him in 1974. He could decide what the person in the room was owed, and in his last book he let his narrator decide it. The world may be a fake. The person in front of you does not go away when you stop believing in the world.
