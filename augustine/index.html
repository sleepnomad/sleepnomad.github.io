Sixteen hundred years ago a man in Milan sat down to explain a failure. He had decided. Nothing was stopping him, and for years he did not move.

Two exits were open to him, and he closed them both. He would not blame a stranger inside him, and he would not call it low willpower. What he wrote instead removes something you have assumed about yourself since your first broken promise. Three hours on where the wanting goes. Start with something you can test before this sentence ends.

00:00:50Part 1: The One Order You Cannot Give Yourself

Raise your hand. It is already up. Somewhere between the deciding and the arm there was supposed to be a gap, and nobody has ever found it. The arm is meat at the end of a nerve, a different kind of stuff, wired in from a distance. It obeyed before the order had finished being given. Now give the other order. Command yourself to WANT to be awake at five tomorrow. Not to set the alarm. To want it. Nothing happens.

The strange part is which order failed. The hand is the hard case, in principle. It is separate, it is elsewhere, and a command has to cross something to reach it. The wanting is not elsewhere at all. There is no distance for the order to travel and nothing standing in the way. And that is the one command that comes back refused.

The ordinary explanation is a shortage. Something ran low, and the words for it are discipline, resolve, willpower. People argue about what kind of thing willpower is. A tank that empties by the evening. A muscle that thickens with use. The lab findings behind the tank have been hard to replicate, and the muscle is a metaphor rather than anything measured, so treat neither as settled. But both sides are standing on the same floor. The floor is the assumption that there is an instrument doing the trying, and that the instrument is basically sound. It wants exercise, or fuel, or a better system built around it. That is a reasonable picture, because it is what every other kind of failure looks like. An engine that will not turn over has run out of something.

Book Eight of the Confessions has the sentence that takes the floor away. Imperat animus corpori, et paretur statim. Imperat animus sibi, et resistitur. The mind commands the body, and it obeys forthwith; the mind commands itself, and is resisted. Then he presses it one turn further. Imperat animus ut velit animus, nec alter est nec facit tamen. The mind commands the mind to will. It is not another thing. And still it does not do it.1

What it does not say
'Nec alter est' denies a second SUBSTANCE, which is Augustine's anti-Manichaean point; it does not by itself deny that the mind has parts or levels. A listener will hear a modern claim about a unitary self. The Latin Library page was read for this sentence only; the surrounding sentences of 9.21 were verified separately from O'Donnell's text.

Nec alter est. Not somebody else. Hold on to that, because everything hangs on it. When your hand fails to move there are candidates for the blame: a severed nerve, a dead muscle, a rope round the wrist. When the wanting fails to arrive there is no candidate at all. The commander and the commanded are the same item, in the same place, at the same moment.

Take the argument in its parts, because the conclusion carries more than it looks. One. The mind commands the body and the body obeys at once. Two. The mind commands itself to will, and meets resistance.2 Three. In the second case there is no second thing available to do the resisting. Four, and this is the load-bearing one. A whole will commanding itself to will would already BE the willing, so it could not fail. Five. Therefore the resistance proves that the command was never whole in the first place. Non ex toto vult, he writes. It does not wholly will, and so it does not wholly command.

Follow what that removes. If the command failed because the commander was partial, then there is nothing standing behind the will and failing to push hard enough. Which means you have been keeping a ledger of a quantity that is not there. Resolutions made in one column, resolutions broken in the other. That is why it has never predicted anything.

Then there is the question of how a person gets into a state where the order fails at all. He answers it as a sequence of links. From a perverse will, lust was made. Serving the lust, custom was made. Not resisting the custom, necessity was made.8 Every single link there is voluntary. The chain is not. No individual act was irreversible when it happened, and all of them together produced something that cannot now be reversed by an act. A person can be the author of a necessity he has no power to revoke.

He puts up a man deliberating between two crimes he cannot both commit. Two wills, and not a good one among them, so the good nature and the evil nature have nowhere to sit.10 Then he adds a third option, and then a fourth.11 By the principle on offer, that man now contains three natures, and then four, and the count rises with every additional thing he might do. And run it the other way. Two good options, equally worth taking, produce two competing wills as easily as two bad ones do. On the same principle that would prove two natures, one of which has to be the evil one. So the inference fails in both directions. Wanting several things at once is what deliberating IS, and it counts no species living inside a person.

Now the argument has a soft joint, and honesty requires pointing at it rather than around it. It is premise four. Why should commanding yourself to will be the very same act as willing? A critic can hold them apart without contradiction. Commanding is one act. Willing is another. There is nothing incoherent in the first occurring and the second not following, the way a person can sincerely resolve and then not act. On that reading the resistance shows only that the second act failed to happen. It shows nothing whatever about the integrity of the first. And if that is right, then his verdict, that the will does not wholly will, stops being a diagnosis. It becomes a restatement of the failure with Latin on it. Augustine needs the identity of self-command and willing to get from a failed order to a fractured willer, and he does not argue for it.13 He treats it as obvious, which is what people do with the premise they most need.

Book fourteen of the City of God contains a list. For about a page it reads like a collection of party tricks. There are people, Augustine writes, who can move their ears, either one at a time or both together. There are some who can bring the hair down onto the forehead without moving the head, sliding the whole scalp backwards and forwards at pleasure. So whatever weight is in this paragraph, it cannot be heaviness, because heaviness has one direction and this has several. The premises are plain, and the definition falls out of them. First, every body has an inner tendency by which it seeks its own place. Second, that place differs by the kind of thing it is: down for the stone, up for the flame, above the water for the oil. Third, one principle explains all three motions, and they point in different directions. So weight names directedness toward a proper place, and heaviness is only one case of it.

Now the transposition, which is the reason the paragraph exists. Pondus meum amor meus. My weight is my love, and by it I am carried wherever I am carried.30 It is built to sound like poetry and it is a claim about mechanics. Love occupies, in a person, the exact position that weight occupies in a stone. It is the thing in virtue of which motion happens at all, and it is not something the stone does. What you love is the direction you already are.

Now the part where the theory looks monstrous, because it does, and the objection has been live for a hundred years. Arendt's word for what the doctrine does to the person you love is annihilation. And the doctrine gets there by a distinction Augustine draws in the first book of a work on Christian teaching.

The argument runs like this. Everything you love, you either enjoy or use. To enjoy something is to rest in it for its own sake. To use it is to love it for the sake of something further. Then comes the premise. Anything that can change can be taken away from you, so resting in it means eventually losing it. On Augustine's account only God cannot change. The conclusion is immediate and it is horrible. Everything else falls under use. Your friends fall under use, and so do your children.

Annihilation is the charge, and this is how her readers state it. Loving a person in God means the person is not what the love terminates on. They become the occasion for it. And an occasion is replaceable, because any other neighbour would have served, which is what the love command was supposed to rule out. The second charge cuts from the other side and is at least as bad. Both charges are aimed at a premise Augustine actually holds, and neither is a misreading of an incautious line.

The reply worth hearing is an inversion, and Augustine's own image is what sets it up. He describes exiles who cannot be happy away from their own country and who must cross water to get back. They board a ship. And then the beauty of the country they are passing through gets into them, the passage becomes pleasant, and they stop wanting to arrive. That image says something the critics do not expect. The failure in it is that the travellers stopped. In his vocabulary, disordered love is love terminating on a good it was only meant to pass through.

Before any of the terms get redefined, here is the case they are answerable to. It requires no decision from you in advance. Think of a friendship you would defend in public. Now change the friend. He becomes tedious. He becomes ill in a way that is boring and long. He has nothing to give you, no prospect of having anything, and the conversation is work now. If the love was terminating on what he did for you, it evaporates here, and the friendship was consumption wearing a friendlier word. If it was not, it survives the change, and it survives without any decision from you.

The test has just killed one of two loves, and which one it killed is the whole argument. It killed the love that rested in the friend for what resting in him gave you. The love that survived was never resting there at all. So the vocabulary turns over. The language of use is a warning rather than a permission. What it forbids is any attitude toward a person that ends their meaning in what they can do for you. On that reading, enjoyment is the dangerous word. A pear tree stood near a vineyard, loaded with fruit nobody wanted. The pears were poor ones, dull in colour, worse in taste. You add a column of figures and get four thousand three hundred and twelve. You check it, and this time the total comes out four thousand three hundred and thirteen. This is the ownership claim, and it makes Augustine's account harder than the modern sub-systems picture, not softer. It is a philosophical refusal, not evidence against psychological partitioning; the argument he gives against it is a reductio about the number of minds, not a finding.

The structural form of the charge is harder to answer, and it does not depend on guessing his motives. Augustine needs the will to be permanently incapable of repairing itself. If ordinary effort could heal the division, then grace becomes optional equipment, and Pelagius was right all along. So the psychology is set at precisely the strength the theology requires, no weaker and no stronger. A thesis calibrated to a conclusion it is obliged to reach has worse standing than one that is not. That holds whether or not the thesis happens to be true.

The one piece of evidence pointing the other way is in his own hand. It is the collapse in Part Nine, when the questions from Simplicianus made him set out to defend free choice and he could not.158 He set out to argue the case Nietzsche says he needed to lose, and he lost it. Then he wrote the loss down. That is not proof of good faith, though it is evidence running the opposite way from the charge. It is also the only kind of evidence available.

So here is the audit, without taking a side. He is stronger than the modern literature in three places. First, he already has the observation that broke the modern orthodoxy. Grant me chastity and continency, but not yet. That is a sincere judgment being revised, in the same breath, by the very temptation it was aimed at. And he tells you why. He was afraid of being heard too soon. The textbook definition says weakness of will is acting against your own better judgment.159 Richard Holton denies that, and the denial is contested. On his account it is abandoning a resolution too readily, because by the time you cave your judgment has already quietly moved. Alfred Mele has pushed back on that reading, and the dispute is live. But the phenomenon is on the page in the fourth century, described from the inside.

What it does not say
The second half is what the meme version cuts, and without it the line reads as comic hypocrisy rather than as the diagnostic exhibit it is: the petition is sincere and engineered by the will against itself. This is the NPNF English; the Latin was not read for this source.

Second, his mechanism is habit, and habit has held up better than its rival. Willpower as a tank that drains has one central experimental prop underneath it. The replication record is contested. It has faced substantial replication challenges. It is a model, not a measured quantity of anything, and it should not be spoken about as established. Habit itself has been measured on a curve. In one study people performed a chosen daily action in a stable setting, day after day. The time to reach hardened automatic behaviour ranged from eighteen days to two hundred and fifty-four. Sit with what that range does to the figure you arrived carrying. Twenty-one days is not a slow estimate or a fast one. It sits inside a spread more than eleven times its own width.

Third, he refuses the comfort everybody else takes. The tempting move is to split the failing agent into parts and let one part be the culprit. He will not do it. It was I, even I myself, as Part One had it. There is no second nature to hand the bill to, and no subsystem to be disappointed in. There is one will that is not entire.

Now the other column, and it is not shorter. He has no account of ordinary success. People quit. A man stops drinking at forty-one, with no conversion, no visitation, nothing he would call grace. Augustine's framework must call that either an illusion or a hidden operation of grace the man did not notice. Both are unfalsifiable, and an explanation that cannot fail is not an explanation. Second, the diagnosis is cut to fit the cure, which is the Nietzschean point standing unrefuted. Third, he has no degrees and no instruments, only a switch with two positions. Divided, or healed from outside. There is nothing in him that tells you what to do on a Tuesday.

The current debate has a hole in it. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on weakness of will does not engage Augustine or the Christian tradition. And the story that nobody thought seriously about this between Aristotle and the analytic philosophers has been dismantled at book length. Risto Saarinen's reviewers report that he found the medieval and Reformation arguments alive and inventive. The hole in the field is a hole in its reading list.

The most useful reader of all this was twenty-three years old and had not written anything yet. In 1936, Albert Camus spent his thesis year on Christian metaphysics and Neoplatonism. Which is to say, on exactly this question. I am describing that thesis from a study in the journal Studi Francesi, not from the text itself. Take the formula as that study reports it. The conclusion he reached was that Augustine is Greek in his need for coherence and Christian in his disquiets. Then Camus did the thing that conclusion makes possible. He kept the disquiet and refused the cure. He wrote his absurd books out of the half he retained. That is the position you are standing in, whether or not you chose it.

So what is left, once the theology is taken out. Three claims, all of which a thoroughgoing naturalist can hold without flinching. Willing is not one thing, and it can issue an order to itself and be disobeyed. The disobedience comes from your own past, laid down by repetition, and now operating as weight rather than as choice. And a divided will cannot be its own remedy, because every repair it attempts is performed by the thing that needs repairing.

Modern moral psychology accepts all three and then secularises the outside. The resolution made in a cool hour. The app that locks the phone. The room with no bottle in it. The friend who is told the plan, so that breaking it costs something. Each of those is genuinely outside the moment of temptation, and each works better than trying harder. And his objection survives all of them intact. It is the same weight that buys the device, the same divided will that installs the app and deletes it at eleven at night. The resolution, the device and the empty room are things the hand arranges, and the hand was never the part that failed.

He has the better description and a medicine that may not exist. Nothing built since has closed the circle he opened.

Which leaves the accounting you have been running on yourself since you were old enough to make a plan. The column of resolutions kept and resolutions broken, the running total, the private grade. That ledger has never predicted anything about you, and now you know why. It measures a quantity that does not exist as a separable thing. You have been failing an exam in a subject nobody teaches. What can be inspected instead is what actually has hold of you, and what has ever in fact moved it. Rooms, company, other people, the ordering of what gets placed in front of you every morning. None of that heals anything. It is only the one direction that has ever moved anybody. Every plan you have ever made with a start date on it has the shape of that prayer. And the timing clause was the sincere half. The things that have ever changed anyone were never inside them.
